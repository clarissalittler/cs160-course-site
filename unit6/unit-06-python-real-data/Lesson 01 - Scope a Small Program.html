Lesson 1 - Scope a Small Program

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe two styles of getting started on a program, planning and bricolage, and recognize which one sounds like you.
  • Describe an interactive program as input, state, processing, and output.
  • Write a short sample interaction before writing code.
  • Separate the smallest complete path from optional ideas.

Two ways to get started

Here's something that happens to almost everyone the first time they sit down to write a program of their own, instead of an exercise someone else designed. You open a blank file, you've got a fun idea in your head, and you have no idea what the first line should be. It's hard to translate from an idea to a specific set of instructions, the same way it's hard to take an idea for a story and turn it into a real plot. This lesson is about how to bridge that gap.

Now, people bridge that gap in really different ways, so let me tell you two stories about getting started.

The planner. Before touching the keyboard, the planner figures out what the finished program is supposed to do. They sketch out how someone would use it, break the job into parts, decide what each part is responsible for, maybe write the whole thing out in pseudocode, and only then start typing Python. When the code doesn't work, they go back and fix the plan.

The bricoleur. The bricoleur (a French word for someone who tinkers, who builds with whatever happens to be lying around) starts by getting something on the screen. Maybe it's a title, or one room of a game, or one silly line of output. Then they poke at it. They run it, change one thing, run it again, move the pieces around, and let what they see suggest what to try next. The program takes shape a little at a time, almost like a conversation, and the mistakes along the way are part of how it gets there.

These two stories come from a famous 1990 paper by Sherry Turkle and Seymour Papert called "Epistemological Pluralism", which is a very academic way of saying "there's more than one good way to know things." They spent a long time watching people learn to program, from elementary-school kids up through college students, and they found really strong programmers in both styles. There's a lovely example in the paper of a nine-year-old named Alex who was building a Lego robot. Instead of attaching the wheels to the motor the usual way, he turned them into flat "shoes" and let the motor's vibration shuffle the whole robot across the floor. You're not going to come up with something like that by planning! You find it by messing around.

So why write a whole paper about this? Because they were worried. Computer culture at the time treated planning as the right way to program, and it treated bricolage as something sloppy that you'd eventually grow out of. Students who naturally worked like bricoleurs often felt like they had to pretend to be planners, and some of them decided programming just wasn't for them. In Turkle and Papert's studies the bricoleur style showed up more often among girls and women, and they argued this was part of why so many women felt unwelcome in computing. It wasn't that there were rules keeping anyone out. It was a culture that told people their way of thinking didn't count. Their suggestion was that formal planning should be "on tap, not on top": a really useful tool that you reach for when you need it, not the only acceptable way to think.

So which one are you? Honestly, most programmers mix both, and the same person might plan out a big project and tinker their way through a small one. Neither story is the "real" way to program, and everything in this lesson works from either direction. Take the very next idea, the sample interaction. If you're a planner, it's the first draft of your plan. If you're a bricoleur, it's a cheap first thing to play with before there's any code at all.

Begin with one ordinary interaction

Okay, so let's start with a person using the finished thing. Say you're in charge of the music at a community event, and you've set up a little laptop on a table where people can request songs. Before deciding anything about how the code works, write down what one successful use of it would look like:

MUSIC REQUEST DESK
1. Add a song
2. Show the queue
3. Play the next song
4. Quit
> 1
Song title: Dancing in the Moonlight
Added to the queue.
> 2
1. Dancing in the Moonlight
> 3
Now playing: Dancing in the Moonlight
> 4
Thanks for helping with the music.

A made-up transcript like this is sometimes called a sample interaction, and it's about the cheapest prototype there is. You don't need to know any Python to write one, and you can hand it to a friend and ask "is this what you'd expect to happen?" before you've written a single line of code.

It's also secretly a plan. If you read back through it, you can pull out what the program needs: a menu, a way to read the person's choice, a list of requested songs, a loop so that it keeps going, and actions that add, show, and remove songs. You can also see what it doesn't need yet, like accounts, actually streaming the audio, voting, album art, or hooking it up to Spotify. Those might be fun to add later (and I'd encourage you to try!), but nobody needs any of them to request a song, so they can wait.

Input, state, processing, and output

PartMusic-kiosk exampleAnother example
InputA menu choice or song title.A café order, quiz answer, or search word.
StateThe current request queue.A basket, score, selected screen, or list of results.
ProcessingAdd a title, choose the first title, or reject an unavailable action.Calculate, compare, filter, or update.
OutputThe menu, queue, confirmation, or error message.A receipt, recommendation, status panel, or story response.

This table is a way of sorting out what's going on inside a program like this. Input is whatever arrives right now. State is what the running program has to remember from one moment to the next. Processing looks at both of those and might change the state. Output tells a person what happened. So in the kiosk, typing 1 and a song title is input, adding the song to the queue changes the state, and Added to the queue. is the output.

State is the one people forget about, because you can't see it in the transcript. It's the reason typing 2 shows something different before and after someone adds a song: the program remembered. If you ever catch yourself asking "but how does the program know that?", the answer is almost always "it's in the state." Once you start looking for it you'll see it in every app on your phone. Your messaging app's state is all of your conversations, a game's state is where you are and your score and what's in your inventory, &c.

The smallest complete path

Here's the piece of advice I'd give for any first project: find one thin path through the whole program, from start to finish, and get that working before anything else. People sometimes call this a vertical slice. For the kiosk it might be:

  1. Display the menu.
  2. Add one request.
  3. Show that request.
  4. Play and remove it.
  5. Quit cleanly.

Why bother? I can't stress this enough: the most common way small projects die is that someone starts five features and finishes none of them. A path that works all the way through, even a small and boring one, is something you can run, test, show a friend, and build on. Voting and album art can wait until it runs.

Pseudocode

set the request queue to an empty list
set running to true

while running:
    display the menu
    ask for a choice

    if the choice is add:
        ask for a title and add it to the queue
    otherwise if the choice is show:
        display every request
    otherwise if the choice is play next:
        display and remove the first request
    otherwise if the choice is quit:
        set running to false
    otherwise:
        explain the available choices

Pseudocode is a program written in plain language instead of Python. It has the structure of the real thing with all the fussy details left out. There aren't any official rules for it, you just write the steps clearly enough that you could turn them into Python later. If you compare this with the transcript we started with, you can see how closely it follows along: each menu option turned into one branch, the queue turned into a list, and "keep going until they quit" turned into a while loop with a running flag, the same kind of loop from Unit 4.

Now, why write a program in English when you could just write it in Python? Because it lets you check the order of the steps before punctuation and exact syntax start demanding your attention. In my experience nearly every bug in a program like this is a logic bug in one of those branches, and it's a lot easier to spot a logic bug in fifteen lines of English than in forty lines of Python.

Same four parts, different program

Let's try the input/state/processing/output idea on a real program, and one that isn't built around a menu at all. Say you're at a potluck and someone wants to know which dish people liked best. Everyone types in a vote, and when they're done the program prints a tally:

votes = {}

print("POTLUCK VOTE: type the dish you liked best.")
print("Press Enter on an empty line when everyone has voted.")

vote = input("Vote: ").strip().lower()
while vote != "":
    if vote in votes:
        votes[vote] += 1
    else:
        votes[vote] = 1
    vote = input("Vote: ").strip().lower()

if len(votes) == 0:
    print("No votes were cast.")
else:
    winner = None
    for dish, count in votes.items():
        print(f"{dish}: {count}")
        if winner is None or count > votes[winner]:
            winner = dish
    print(f"Most votes: {winner} ({votes[winner]})")

So what are the four parts here? The input is one vote at a time. The state is the votes dictionary, which keeps track of how many votes each dish has so far. The processing cleans up each vote and adds one to the right count. The output is the tally and the winner. It looks completely different from the music kiosk, and it still has the same four parts!

The loop is the sentinel pattern from Unit 4, back when a negative number meant "I'm done entering donations." Here an empty line means "done." You might have noticed there are two input calls, and you really do need both of them: one before the loop, so there's a vote to check the first time through, and one at the bottom, to get the next vote. If you forget the second one, the loop counts the same vote over and over, forever.

The counting works like the counters from Unit 5, except that every dish needs its own count, so the counts live in a dictionary. Have we seen this dish before? Then add one. Haven't we? Then start it at 1.

Every vote also goes through .strip().lower() first, so that Lentil Soup and lentil soup (with a sneaky space at the end) turn into the same key. Without that, Python would treat them as two different dishes and the soup would end up splitting its own vote, which hardly seems fair to the soup.

The last part finds the dish with the most votes, using the same idea as the min/max lesson in Unit 5: don't make up a starting value, start from the first real one. winner begins as None, meaning "no winner yet," and the first dish the loop sees takes the spot. After that, a dish only takes over if its count is strictly bigger.

So here's a fun question: what happens when there's a tie? Dictionaries remember the order their keys were added in, and > never switches on an equal count, so a tie goes to whichever dish got its first vote earliest. That means this program has a tie-breaking rule, even though nobody sat down and decided on one! It's just a side effect of how the loop happens to be written. (We'll come back to rules like that at the end of the unit.)

Try it: Pick a small program you'd actually like to exist, like a song-request queue, a packing checklist, a "what should I cook tonight?" helper, or a tiny interactive story. Then:

  1. Pick a route. Planner: start with step 2. Bricoleur: first write the smallest Python program that shows something from your idea, like a title or one menu, run it, and change one thing at a time for a few minutes; then do step 2 based on what you discovered.
  2. Write a five-to-eight-action sample interaction.
  3. Circle the inputs and underline every value that must change.
  4. Describe the smallest complete path.
  5. Write one test for it: a starting state, one action, and what you should see afterward.
  6. Put every other idea under an “only after the basic path works” heading.
  7. Which route felt more natural to you, and why? (There's no wrong answer here!)
  8. Change the potluck program so a tie is announced as a tie (for example, Tie: lentil soup and cornbread (2 each)) instead of just picking one.
