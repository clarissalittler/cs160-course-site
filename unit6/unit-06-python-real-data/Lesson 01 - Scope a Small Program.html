Lesson 1 - Scope a Small Program

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe two styles of getting started on a program, planning and bricolage, and recognize which one sounds like you.
  • Describe an interactive program as input, state, processing, and output.
  • Write a short sample interaction before writing code.
  • Separate the smallest complete path from optional ideas.

Two ways to get started

Here's a thing that happens to nearly everyone the first time they sit down to write a program of their own, as opposed to an exercise someone else designed: you open a blank file, you have a fun idea in your head, and you have no idea what the first line should be. It's hard to translate an idea into a specific set of instructions, the same way it's hard to take an idea for a story and turn it into a real plot. This lesson is about how to bridge that gap.

People bridge it in really different ways. Here are two stories about getting started.

The planner. Before touching the keyboard, the planner works out what the finished program should do. They sketch how someone would use it, split the job into parts, decide what each part is responsible for, maybe write the whole thing out in pseudocode, and only then start typing Python. When the code doesn't work, they go back and fix the plan.

The bricoleur. The bricoleur (a French word for someone who tinkers, building with whatever's at hand) starts by getting something on the screen. Maybe it's a title, or one room of a game, or one silly line of output. Then they poke at it. They run it, change one thing, run it again, rearrange the pieces, and let what they see suggest what to try next. The program takes shape gradually, a bit like a conversation, and its mistakes are part of how it finds its way.

These two stories come from a famous 1990 paper by Sherry Turkle and Seymour Papert called "Epistemological Pluralism". (That's a fancy way of saying "there's more than one good way to know things.") They watched lots of people learning to program, from elementary-school kids to college students, and found strong programmers in both styles. One nine-year-old bricoleur named Alex, building a Lego robot, skipped the usual way of attaching wheels to the motor. Instead, he turned the wheels into flat "shoes" and let the motor's vibration shuffle the robot along. No plan would have predicted that. Tinkering found it.

Their worry was that computer culture treated planning as the right way to program, and bricolage as something sloppy that you'd grow out of. Students who naturally worked as bricoleurs often felt pressure to pretend to be planners, and some decided programming just wasn't for them. In their studies, the bricoleur style showed up more often among girls and women, which they argued was part of why so many women felt unwelcome in computing: not rules keeping people out, but a culture that told them their way of thinking didn't count. Their conclusion was that formal logic and planning should be "on tap, not on top": a powerful tool to reach for, not the only acceptable way to think.

So which one are you? Honestly, most programmers mix both, and the same person might plan a big project and tinker their way through a small one. Neither story is the "real" way to program. The tools in this lesson are useful from either direction, and the first one is a good example. It's a plan for planners, and for bricoleurs it's the first small thing to play with.

Begin with one ordinary interaction

Start with a person using the finished thing. Imagine a music-request kiosk at a community event, a little laptop on a table where people can ask for songs. Before deciding anything about how the code works, write down what one successful use should look like:

MUSIC REQUEST DESK
1. Add a song
2. Show the queue
3. Play the next song
4. Quit
> 1
Song title: Dancing in the Moonlight
Added to the queue.
> 2
1. Dancing in the Moonlight
> 3
Now playing: Dancing in the Moonlight
> 4
Thanks for helping with the music.

A made-up transcript like this is sometimes called a sample interaction, and it's the cheapest prototype there is. You don't need to know any Python to write one, and you can hand it to a friend and ask "is this what you'd expect?" before you've written a single line.

That transcript is already a plan. Look at what it tells us the program needs: a menu, a way to read a choice, a list of requested songs, a loop, and actions that add, display, and remove songs. Now look at what it doesn't need yet: accounts, streaming audio, voting, album art, a connection to Spotify. Those might be fun later (and I'd encourage you to try!). They're not the program.

Input, state, processing, and output

PartMusic-kiosk exampleAnother example
InputA menu choice or song title.A café order, quiz answer, or search word.
StateThe current request queue.A basket, score, selected screen, or list of results.
ProcessingAdd a title, choose the first title, or reject an unavailable action.Calculate, compare, filter, or update.
OutputThe menu, queue, confirmation, or error message.A receipt, recommendation, status panel, or story response.

Input is what arrives right now. State is what the running program has to remember from one moment to the next. Processing reads both and may change the state. Output tells a person what happened. In the kiosk, typing 1 and a song title is input, adding it to the queue changes the state, and Added to the queue. is the output.

State is the one people forget about, because you can't see it in the transcript. It's the reason typing 2 shows a different answer before and after someone added a song. If you ever find yourself asking "but how does the program know that?", the answer is almost always "it's in the state." Once you've seen this you'll notice it in every app on your phone: your messaging app's state is your conversations, a game's state is your position and score and inventory, &c.

Protect the smallest complete path

A vertical slice is one thin path through the whole program, top to bottom. For the kiosk, it might be:

  1. Display the menu.
  2. Add one request.
  3. Show that request.
  4. Play and remove it.
  5. Quit cleanly.

Get that path working before you add anything else. I can't stress this enough: the most common way small projects die is by starting five features and finishing none. A slice that works all the way through, even a small and boring one, is something you can run, test, show a friend, and build on. Voting, accounts, and album art can wait until it runs.

Pseudocode gives the loop a shape

set the request queue to an empty list
set running to true

while running:
    display the menu
    ask for a choice

    if the choice is add:
        ask for a title and add it to the queue
    otherwise if the choice is show:
        display every request
    otherwise if the choice is play next:
        display and remove the first request
    otherwise if the choice is quit:
        set running to false
    otherwise:
        explain the available choices

Pseudocode is a program written in plain language instead of Python: the structure of the real thing, with the fussy details left out. There aren't any rules for it, you just write the steps clearly enough that you could translate them later. Look at how closely this follows the transcript we started with. Each menu option became one branch, the queue became a list, and "keep going until they quit" became a while loop with a running flag, the same kind of loop from Unit 4.

Pseudocode isn't busywork you throw away. It lets you check the sequence before punctuation and exact syntax start demanding your attention. In my experience nearly every bug in a program like this is a logic bug in one of those branches, and it's a lot easier to spot a logic bug in fifteen lines of English than in forty lines of Python.

Same four parts, different program

Let's try the input/state/processing/output idea on a real program, and one that isn't a menu at all. Say a potluck wants to know which dish people liked best. Everyone types in a vote, and when they're done, the program prints a tally:

votes = {}

print("POTLUCK VOTE: type the dish you liked best.")
print("Press Enter on an empty line when everyone has voted.")

vote = input("Vote: ").strip().lower()
while vote != "":
    if vote in votes:
        votes[vote] += 1
    else:
        votes[vote] = 1
    vote = input("Vote: ").strip().lower()

if len(votes) == 0:
    print("No votes were cast.")
else:
    winner = None
    for dish, count in votes.items():
        print(f"{dish}: {count}")
        if winner is None or count > votes[winner]:
            winner = dish
    print(f"Most votes: {winner} ({votes[winner]})")

The input is one vote at a time. The state is the votes dictionary, which maps each dish to how many votes it has so far. The processing cleans up each vote and adds one to the right count. The output is the tally and the winner. So it's shaped completely differently from the music desk, and it still has the same four parts!

The loop is the sentinel pattern from Unit 4, where a negative number meant "I'm done entering donations." Here, an empty line means "done." There are two input calls, and you need both of them: one before the loop, so there's a vote to check the first time through, and one at the bottom, to get the next vote. Forget the second one and the loop runs forever.

The counting works like the counters from Unit 5, except that every dish needs its own count, so the counts live in a dictionary. Seen this dish before? Add one. Haven't? Start it at 1.

Every vote also goes through .strip().lower() first. That way Lentil Soup and lentil soup (with a sneaky trailing space) end up as the same key. Without it, Python would treat them as two different dishes, and the soup would split its own vote. Poor soup.

The last part finds the dish with the most votes, using the same idea as the min/max lesson in Unit 5: don't make up a starting value, start from the first real one. winner begins as None, meaning "no winner yet," and the first dish the loop sees takes the spot. After that, a dish only takes over if its count is strictly bigger.

So here's a fun question: what happens in a tie? Dictionaries remember the order their keys were added, and > never switches on an equal count, so a tie goes to whichever dish got its first vote earliest. That means this program has a tie-breaking rule, even though nobody sat down and decided on one! It's just a side effect of how the loop happens to be written. (We'll come back to rules like that at the end of the unit.)

Getting-started studio. Choose a small request queue, packing checklist, recommendation kiosk, resource finder, or interactive scene. Then:

  1. Pick a route. Planner: start with step 2. Bricoleur: first write the smallest Python program that shows something from your idea, like a title or one menu, run it, and change one thing at a time for a few minutes; then do step 2 based on what you discovered.
  2. Write a five-to-eight-action sample interaction.
  3. Circle the inputs and underline every value that must change.
  4. Describe the smallest complete path.
  5. Write one test for it: a starting state, one action, and what you should see afterward.
  6. Put every other idea under an “only after the basic path works” heading.
  7. Which route felt more natural to you, and why? (There's no wrong answer!)
  8. Change the potluck program so a tie is announced as a tie (for example, Tie: lentil soup and cornbread (2 each)) instead of just picking one.
