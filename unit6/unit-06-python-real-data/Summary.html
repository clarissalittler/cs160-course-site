Unit 6 Summary

Unit 6 didn't ask you to memorize a new corner of Python. It asked you to recognize the same handful of roles showing up in program after program. A request queue, a community guide, an event display, a lending kiosk, and an interactive story look unrelated on the surface, but every one of them is structured data, a few focused functions, some changing state, a couple of conditions, and a loop. Once you can see that skeleton, a new project stops being a blank page, and that's the thing I most want you to take away from this unit.

Along the way, a few ideas came back from earlier units in more serious clothes. The aliasing that was a surprise in Unit 5 became a tool, the way a function can change a list it was handed. Tuple unpacking came back as a function returning an index and a message together. And the loop patterns from Unit 5 showed up inside bigger programs, as the main loop and as checks that walk through every connection. None of these were new this week; what was new was seeing them work together, in a vote counter, a bus guide, a recipe scaler, a scorekeeper, a checklist, and more.

Then the last lesson turned the question around. Every one of those programs has a single person at the keyboard who can notice a mistake and try again. When a program applies one rule to a whole list of people, a mistake that's rare for any one person becomes certain across thousands, and nobody is watching each case. The Robodebt rule was a division, a comparison, and a loop, and it sent debt notices to people who didn't owe anything, because of an assumption about how income arrives. A scam filter can wrongly flag a message or let a scam slip through, and those two mistakes land on different people. Choosing between them isn't only a programming decision.

What to carry forward

  • Start with a sample interaction and protect the smallest complete path.
  • Input arrives now; state is what the running program has to remember; processing may change state; output explains the result.
  • Nested dictionaries can represent related records and the connections among them.
  • Square brackets are for keys that must exist; .get() is for keys whose absence means something.
  • Data describes what varies. Reusable functions do the actions and the presentation across that data.
  • Triple-quoted strings keep their line breaks, and multi-line f-strings can drop in changing values.
  • join turns several strings into readable display text without touching the source collection.
  • A small command vocabulary should be visible, normalized, and consistent.
  • Returning an updated value and mutating a list or dictionary are two different ways state can change, and a caller has to store a returned value or nothing happens.
  • A main loop should direct traffic among understandable functions rather than doing every job itself.
  • Trace to the first step where what you see differs from what you expected.
  • Reset changing data before an independent test.
  • Playtesting reveals assumptions about labels, commands, goals, and feedback.
  • The same program pattern carries into a new setting even when every noun changes.
  • A rule that's right 99% of the time is wrong for thousands of people once it runs on hundreds of thousands.
  • Every rule carries assumptions about the people it's applied to; the program only knows the facts someone wrote down.
  • A yes/no rule can wrongly flag a case or let one slip through. Count them separately, and ask who bears each.
  • Before a rule runs on everyone, ask whether people can see why they were flagged, who has to prove what, and whether a person can step in.

Readiness check

Can you follow a nested lookup, explain what .get() adds, and format a record as a readable multi-line display? Can you tell a returned update from a list mutation, trace one command through a function, and describe a manual test as starting state, action, and expected result? If so, you have the central ideas, and the capstone is just putting them in one file. And can you look at a rule that's applied automatically to many people, name an assumption inside it, and say who gets each kind of mistake? That's the question to carry out of the Python sequence and into the rest of the course.
