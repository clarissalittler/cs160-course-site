Closing Words: Where Computing Goes from Here

Ten weeks ago, we started with a single bit, a 1 or a 0. Since then you've turned bits into text and pictures, followed a packet across the internet, written programs that make decisions and loop and keep track of records, compared algorithms and run into the things no computer can ever do, made charts and caught other people's charts lying, trained a model and watched it get fooled, and learned how to keep your accounts and your messages safe. That's a lot! I hope you're a little proud of it.

So before you go, I want to talk about the question a lot of people have been asking lately: where is all of this going?

Nobody knows, and that's okay

I'll be honest with you: I don't know where computer science is going as a field, and neither does anyone else. When this course was last revised, in 2026, AI systems were writing real software, finding security holes that people had missed for decades, and helping solve math problems that had been open for years. You saw some of that in Unit 9. By the time you're reading this, some of those examples probably look old-fashioned already. People make confident predictions about it all the time, like "programming will be obsolete" or "English is the new programming language," and honestly, most predictions about computing don't age very well.

But there's one thing I'm confident about, and it's the thing this whole course has been quietly pointing at.

Computation is underneath everything

Think back over the examples we've seen this term. A rule in a government computer decided who owed money, and got hundreds of thousands of people wrong. A school's enrollment form decided which families count. A bug at one company, set off when a single customer changed a setting, took a big piece of the web down all at once. A chart on the news made a 4.6-point change look enormous. A spam filter decided which messages you'd never see. And encryption, quietly, is the reason you can type your password into your bank's website without everyone along the way reading it.

None of those are really "computer" stories. They're stories about money, school, news, safety, and how we talk to each other, and computation happens to be the layer underneath every one of them. That layer keeps getting thicker. More of what happens in our lives, our jobs, and our governments runs on code every year, whether or not anybody involved thinks of it as "technology."

And that's why I'm sure there will always be a need for computer scientists, no matter how good AI gets at writing programs. Somebody has to understand what a program is actually doing, and how it does it. Somebody has to decide what we should be asking a computer to do in the first place, check whether it really did it, notice when a rule that works for most people fails Jordan, and say "no, we shouldn't use this" when the answer is no. If anything, the better AI gets at writing code, the more important it is to have people who can read that code, question it, and take responsibility for what it does. Writing the program was never the only hard part. Knowing what the program should be, and whether it's any good, is the part that doesn't go away.

You're one of the people who can ask

You don't have to become a computer scientist to be part of that, by the way. Whatever you end up doing, whether that's nursing, teaching, art, business, law, the trades, or something that hasn't been invented yet, computing is going to be part of your work. And you now know how to ask the questions that matter about it. What does this data actually represent, and who got left out? What rule is this program following, and who does it get wrong? Where's the evidence that it works? And underneath all of it, the three questions we started with: who benefits, who's harmed, and who decides?

Those questions don't go out of date, even when the technology does. And the world needs a lot more people asking them, in every field, not just in computer science departments.

And if this class made you curious, and you want to keep going, please do! You've already done the hardest part, which is getting started. There's a whole lot more to learn, and it only gets more interesting from here.

Thank you for spending these weeks with me. Good luck on the final, and take care of yourselves.
