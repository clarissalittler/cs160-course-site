## Unit 8 Quiz — Data

### Multiple Choice

**1.** A food cart tracks every sale in a table: each sale gets its own row, with columns for drink, size, time, and price. In the language of Lesson 1, what do the rows and columns of a dataset represent?
- A) Rows are the facts; columns are the things being described
- B) Rows are the things being described (one sale each); columns are the facts about those things
- C) Rows and columns are interchangeable — it doesn't matter which way the data is arranged
- D) Rows hold categorical data and columns hold quantitative data

**2.** A dataset of students includes a column of zip codes (97214, 97202, ...). A classmate says the column must be quantitative, since the values are all made of digits. What does Lesson 1 say?
- A) They're right — any value made of digits is quantitative
- B) The column is categorical: zip codes are numbers used as labels, and doing math on them (like averaging two zip codes) doesn't mean anything
- C) The column is quantitative and continuous, since there are so many possible zip codes
- D) The column is quantitative and discrete, since zip codes come in whole numbers

**3.** An ad shows two food carts' monthly orders as bars: Cart A sold 1,000 orders and Cart B sold 1,020 — about a 2% difference — yet Cart B's bar is drawn three times as tall as Cart A's. Which chart trick from Lesson 2 is at work?
- A) A cherry-picked time window
- B) A dual y-axis with two different scales
- C) A truncated y-axis that starts near 990 instead of at zero
- D) A 3-D pie chart tilted toward the viewer

**4.** A news graphic announces: "City A had 500 burglaries last year; City B had only 50." Before concluding City B is safer, what does Lesson 2 say you should ask?
- A) Which city has more police officers
- B) "Out of how many?" — a raw count needs a denominator; if City A has 20 times the population, it's actually the safer city per person
- C) Whether burglary counts are discrete or continuous
- D) Nothing — both numbers are true, so the comparison speaks for itself

**5.** You make a bar chart of the Max Weight column from the dog dataset and it's a mess — dozens of skinny bars, with nearly every weight getting its own bar. What does Lesson 6 suggest instead?
- A) Switch to a pie chart so every weight gets its own slice
- B) Make a histogram, which groups the weights into ranges (bins) and shows how many dogs fall into each range
- C) Remove rows from the dataset until there are fewer bars
- D) Make a crosstab chart, since weight is categorical data

**6.** Ice cream sales and drowning deaths rise and fall together, month after month — a real, strong correlation. According to Lesson 8's four possible explanations, what's actually going on?
- A) A causes B — eating ice cream makes swimming more dangerous
- B) B causes A — drownings drive grieving communities to buy ice cream
- C) A third factor (a confounder) causes both — warm summer weather sends people to the ice cream stand *and* into rivers and pools
- D) Pure coincidence — the two numbers just happen to line up

### True / False

**7.** Data cleaning is only necessary for huge datasets; a small table collected from a class survey can safely be charted exactly as it was entered.

**8.** Lesson 5's rule of thumb is: if you're tempted to make a pie chart, make a bar chart instead — because humans are bad at comparing angles and areas, but good at comparing bar lengths against a common axis.

### Multiple Choice (continued)

**9.** A coffee shop records (a) drink ordered, (b) number of drinks sold each day, and (c) preparation time. Which classification is correct?
- A) (a) categorical; (b) quantitative discrete; (c) quantitative continuous
- B) (a) quantitative discrete; (b) categorical; (c) quantitative continuous
- C) (a) categorical; (b) quantitative continuous; (c) quantitative discrete
- D) All three columns are categorical because they appear in a table

**10.** You want to know whether dog breeds with heavier maximum weights tend to have shorter maximum life spans. Which chart is the best choice, and why?
- A) A scatter plot, because it shows the relationship between two quantitative variables
- B) A pie chart, because each breed is part of one whole dog population
- C) A histogram, because it directly pairs each weight with its corresponding life span
- D) A bar chart, because bars are required whenever the data includes breed names
