Lesson 6 - Protecting Yourself Online

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Create strong, unique passwords and explain why password reuse is dangerous
  • Explain how a password manager works and why it helps

Time to Fight Back

Okay. We've spent several lessons talking about all the ways things can go wrong—data breaches, phishing, malware, ransomware, social engineering. It's a lot. Let's talk about what you can actually do about it.

The good news: the most effective defenses are things anyone can set up in an afternoon. You don't need a computer science degree, you don't need expensive software, and you don't need to go live in a cabin without Wi-Fi (tempting as that sometimes sounds). You just need a few habits.

Passwords: The First Line of Defense

Let's start with passwords, because they're still the most common way you prove to a website that you are who you claim to be.

You probably already know that "password123" is a terrible password. (It is. Please don't use it.) But here's the thing most people don't fully appreciate: the biggest password problem isn't having a weak password—it's reusing the same password across multiple sites.

Here's why. Data breaches affect millions of people every year—the question isn't whether your data will be in one, but when. When a site gets breached and its user database gets leaked, attackers don't just try those stolen passwords on that one site. They take every email-and-password pair from the breach and try them on Gmail, Amazon, your bank, your school account—everywhere. This is called credential stuffing, and it's devastatingly effective because so many people use the same password everywhere.

So even if you have a great password, if you use it for both your email and some random forum you signed up for in 2019, and that forum gets hacked—now your email is compromised too.

What makes a good password? Length beats complexity. A 20-character sentence you can remember is better than a random 8-character jumble you can't. "my-dog-hates-the-vacuum-cleaner" is a vastly stronger password than "Xk#9pL"—and you'll actually remember it. Security researchers have been saying this for years: long passphrases are the way to go.

But here's the real problem: you're supposed to have a unique strong password for every single account. And you probably have dozens of accounts. Maybe hundreds. Nobody can remember 50+ unique long passwords. Which brings us to the actual solution.

Password Managers

This is what password managers solve. A password manager is a tool that generates strong, random, unique passwords for every account you have—and stores them securely so you don't have to remember any of them. You only need to remember one thing: your master password for the manager itself.

Here's how it works in practice. You install the password manager (usually as a browser extension and a phone app). When you create a new account somewhere, it generates a strong random password for you. When you go back to that site, it fills in the password automatically. You never see, type, or memorize most of your passwords—the manager handles it.

Some good options:

  • Bitwarden—free and open source. This is a great place to start if you've never used one.
  • 1Password—paid, polished, widely recommended.
  • Your browser's built-in password manager—Chrome, Firefox, and Safari all have them. They're not as full-featured as dedicated tools, but they're infinitely better than reusing passwords.

If you take one single action from this entire unit, make it this: start using a password manager. It's the single most impactful thing most people can do to improve their online security. It takes maybe 20 minutes to set up, and from then on, every new account gets a strong, unique password automatically.

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

Passwords are your first line of defense, but they're not enough on their own. Even a great password can be stolen in a data breach or tricked out of you by a phishing attack. That's where multi-factor authentication comes in.

The idea is simple: instead of proving your identity with just one thing (your password), you prove it with two or more different kinds of things. Security folks break these into three categories:

Something you know—your password.

Something you have—your phone, a security key, &c. This is the most common second factor. You've probably experienced this already: you log in with your password, and then the site sends a code to your phone that you have to type in. Even if someone steals your password, they can't get in without also having your phone.

Something you are—a fingerprint, face scan, retina scan. Your body itself becomes a form of identification. This is the third factor, and combined with the other two, it makes unauthorized access extremely difficult.

Multi-factor authentication means using at least two of these categories together. And it works—it blocks the vast majority of automated attacks, even when your password has been compromised.

Turn on MFA for every account that offers it. Especially email—if someone gets into your email, they can reset passwords for everything else. Your email account is the skeleton key to your digital life, so protect it accordingly.

Keep Your Software Updated

This one is short because the advice is simple: when your phone, computer, or apps say there's an update available, install it.

New security vulnerabilities are discovered constantly—in operating systems, browsers, apps, everything. When a vulnerability is found, the developers release a patch (an update) to fix it. But that patch only protects you if you actually install it. Every day you wait is a day you're running software with a known hole in it.

That annoying "update available" notification? It's not just adding new emoji. It's often fixing security holes that attackers are actively exploiting. The WannaCry ransomware attack in 2017 hit systems that hadn't installed a security patch Microsoft had released two months earlier. Two months. The fix existed. People just hadn't installed it.

Turn on automatic updates wherever you can. Let your phone update overnight. Let your computer restart when it asks. It's a small inconvenience that prevents real harm.

Right now, pick one thing from this lesson to actually do today: set up a password manager, turn on MFA for an important account, or check for software updates on your phone or computer. Which one are you going to do?
