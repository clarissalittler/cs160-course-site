Lesson 10 - When Things Go Wrong

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain malware, including ransomware, and identify habits that reduce the risk of infection
  • Explain how software updates and recoverable backups reduce different forms of risk
  • Choose appropriate first-response steps for a compromised account, malware, fraud, or a data breach

Sometimes, despite everything, something goes wrong. A message is just too convincing, a service you use gets breached, or someone exploits a software flaw nobody knew about yet. It happens to careful people and to security experts too! When it does, having a plan ready makes a big difference in how much damage it does.

So this last lesson comes in two parts. First, some habits that protect your devices and data ahead of time. Second, a set of checklists for what to do when a few common kinds of trouble happen for real.

Part 1: Protecting devices and data

Malware, in plain terms

Malware is short for malicious software: software built to damage a system, steal information, spy on what you're doing, or let someone in who shouldn't be there. There are a few main kinds. Viruses attach themselves to other files. Spyware watches what you do. And ransomware locks up your data, usually by encrypting it, and demands payment to give it back. Some ransomware steals a copy of the data too, and paying is no guarantee you'll get anything back.

The defenses here are unglamorous, but they work: install software from places you trust, be careful with attachments you weren't expecting, leave your device's built-in security protections turned on, and get rid of apps you don't use or that aren't supported anymore.

Updates repair known flaws

Those update notifications that interrupt you at the worst possible moment? Many of them are fixing security holes, not just ordinary bugs. And once a flaw is publicly known, attackers know about it too, so a device that hasn't been updated is left open to attacks the update would have stopped. There's a strange twist, too: publishing a fix can actually help attackers, because they can compare the old version with the new one to see exactly what was broken. So the clock starts ticking the moment an update comes out.

So turn on automatic updates for your operating system, browser, and apps whenever you can. Restart when an update asks you to, since many updates don't take effect until you do, and replace software that doesn't get security updates anymore.

Backups provide a recovery path

A backup you can actually restore from is what saves you when ransomware hits, your laptop gets stolen, coffee meets keyboard, a drive dies, or you just delete the wrong folder. You might still need to clean up or rebuild a compromised device first. And a backup only helps if it has a good, earlier copy of your stuff in it!

Keep more than one copy of the files you care about, and make sure at least one copy is kept separate from the device, like a cloud backup service that keeps old versions, or an external drive you unplug after backing up. One catch: ordinary file syncing isn't always a backup. If you delete a file, or ransomware scrambles it, that change can sync right over to the other copy too. Every so often, test that you can really get your important files back.

Part 2: Incident-response checklists

When something seems wrong, the first step is to figure out what happened, because the right response depends on it. Did you type in information? Install something? Lose control of an account? Send money? Each of those calls for different steps, so here's a checklist for each:

"I entered my password or verification code on a phishing page."

  • Open the real service independently and change the affected password immediately. Change it anywhere else it was reused.
  • Sign out other sessions, review recovery information and recent activity, and remove unfamiliar connected applications or settings.
  • Enable the strongest available multifactor authentication or passkey. If a one-time code was disclosed, do not approve any unexpected login.

"I clicked a suspicious link or think I installed malware."

  • If suspicious software ran or the device is behaving as though it is compromised, disconnect it from networks to limit further communication.
  • Update the device's trusted security software and run a full scan. Remove only what the trusted tool identifies; a suspicious web page alone is not evidence that an application was installed.
  • From a different, clean device, change credentials that may have been captured and review important accounts.
  • If the problem persists, seek qualified support or erase and rebuild the device before restoring verified data from a clean backup.

"My account got hacked."

  • Use the service's official account-recovery process.
  • Change the password, sign out other sessions, and check recovery addresses, forwarding rules, connected applications, and recent activity.
  • Enable strong multifactor authentication or a passkey.
  • Tell contacts if the compromised account may have sent them messages.

"I got scammed out of money."

  • Contact the bank, card issuer, gift-card company, payment application, wire service, or other payment provider immediately and ask what recovery options remain.
  • In the United States, report the incident at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If personal information was exposed, use IdentityTheft.gov for a tailored recovery plan.
  • Preserve screenshots, names, account details, amounts, dates, and transaction records.
  • Expect possible follow-up "recovery" scams from people who promise to retrieve money for an upfront fee.

"My info showed up in a data breach."

  • Read the breach notice to determine which information was involved. If a current password was exposed, change it and every place it was reused.
  • Review affected accounts, enable strong authentication, and follow the organization's recovery instructions using contact information you verify independently.
  • If a Social Security number or information useful for identity theft was exposed, visit IdentityTheft.gov/databreach and consider a free credit freeze with each nationwide credit bureau.

The checklists differ, but they share a common core. Act fast, but don't throw the same fix at every problem. Save the evidence, use a clean channel you found yourself, secure whatever was affected, tell the institutions that need to know, and follow the recovery plan that fits what was actually exposed.

Try it: Take this fictional case. Morgan's laptop is up to date, files sync right away to a cloud folder, and a separate versioned backup last ran two weeks ago. Today Morgan accidentally saved over an important file. What could each copy get back, and what would you check before counting on it? Or privately check one of your own devices' update and backup status. If you like, try restoring a file that isn't sensitive to a separate location, without replacing the original. Report only your reasoning, not your personal filenames or settings.

That's the whole toolkit for the unit! Recognize manipulation. Check claims and requests through channels you find yourself. Use a different password everywhere, and phishing-resistant logins when you can. Don't hand over data you don't need to. Keep your updates and backups going. And when something does go wrong, respond to what actually happened. None of this makes you immune to everything, but it does make the most common problems less likely, and easier to recover from.
