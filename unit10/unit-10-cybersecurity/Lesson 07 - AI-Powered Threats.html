Lesson 7 - AI-Powered Threats

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe how AI is being used to create more sophisticated cyberattacks
  • Explain how deepfakes and voice cloning work at a high level
  • Identify strategies for detecting AI-generated content

When AI Goes on Offense

In Unit 9, we talked about how AI can generate text, images, audio, and video. That's impressive when you're making art or writing code. It's terrifying when someone uses it to impersonate your boss, your bank, or your family member.

The same capabilities that make these tools useful also make them dangerous. This lesson is about that dark side.

AI-Powered Phishing

Back in Lesson 3, we looked at phishing emails. Traditional phishing was often easy to spot if you knew what to look for: awkward grammar, generic greetings ("Dear Customer"), suspicious sender addresses, &c. For years, "check for typos and weird phrasing" was decent advice.

That advice is becoming less reliable. Fast.

Large language models can generate perfectly written, natural-sounding emails. They don't make typos. They don't use weird grammar. An attacker can feed an LLM information scraped from your social media and get back a personalized phishing email that references your real job, your real coworkers, and a project you're actually working on.

Traditional phishing was like a mass-produced flyer shoved under your door. AI-powered phishing is like a hand-written letter from someone who seems to know you—and it can be generated at scale, thousands of personalized emails per hour.

The old warning signs still matter—urgency, unexpected requests, links that don't match where they claim to go. But "it's well-written, so it's probably legit" is no longer a safe assumption. Remember the phishing email from Lesson 3 with the typos? AI doesn't make typos.

Deepfakes

In Unit 9's lesson on generative AI, we talked about how AI can produce realistic images and video. Deepfakes take that capability and weaponize it: AI-generated video of real people saying things they never said, doing things they never did.

The technology works by training a model on existing images and video of a person—their face from different angles, their expressions, how their mouth moves when they talk. The model learns enough to convincingly generate new video of that person from scratch.

The real-world consequences are serious:

  • Financial fraud: In 2024, a finance worker in Hong Kong was tricked into wiring $25 million after a video call with what appeared to be the company's CFO and other colleagues. Every person on the call was a deepfake. Twenty-five million dollars, gone.
  • Political misinformation: Deepfake videos of political figures making statements they never made have circulated during elections around the world. When you can't trust that a video is real, it undermines the shared reality that democratic debate depends on.
  • Non-consensual intimate imagery: By volume, the most common harmful use of deepfakes. AI tools generate fake explicit images of real people—overwhelmingly targeting women and girls. It's happening at an alarming scale.

Voice Cloning

If deepfake video feels like science fiction, voice cloning might hit closer to home. AI can now clone someone's voice from just a few seconds of audio. A voicemail greeting, a clip from social media, a snippet from a video call—that's enough to build a model that can generate new speech in that person's voice, saying anything the attacker wants.

The most common scam is heartbreakingly simple: someone calls a parent or grandparent, and the voice on the line sounds exactly like their child or grandchild. "Mom, I'm in trouble. I've been in an accident. I need you to send money right now. Please don't tell anyone." The panic is real. The voice is fake. And by the time the victim realizes what happened, the money is gone.

These scams exploit the same thing all social engineering does—emotion. But the cloned voice adds a layer of credibility that makes them far more effective than a stranger's voice ever could.

How to Protect Yourself

So—what do you do when you can't necessarily trust what you see, hear, or read? The defenses are less about technology and more about habits:

  • Verify through a second channel. If you get a suspicious email from your boss, don't reply to the email—call them. If you get a text from your bank, don't click the link—open your banking app directly or call the number on the back of your card. If something seems off, reach out through a different method than the one the message arrived on.
  • Be skeptical of urgency. "Act now! Don't tell anyone! There's no time to verify!" Urgency is the universal tool of scammers, and AI-powered attacks use it just as aggressively. Legitimate organizations will give you time to verify.
  • Establish family code words. Pick a word or phrase that your family members know—something that wouldn't appear in any public recording. If someone calls claiming to be a family member in an emergency, ask for the code word. It sounds paranoid. It's not. It's practical.
  • Check for deepfake artifacts. AI-generated video sometimes has telltale glitches: unnatural blinking, inconsistent lighting, weird edges around the hairline, hands that don't look quite right. But be warned—these artifacts are getting harder to spot as the technology improves. Don't rely on this alone.

The most important defense against AI-powered attacks is the same as the most important defense against regular attacks: slow down, verify, and don't act out of panic. Scammers—whether they're using AI or not—are always trying to get you to act before you think. Refuse to play that game.

If someone calls claiming to be a family member in an emergency, hang up and call them back on a number you know. That moment of discomfort is much cheaper than $25 million. Or even $500.
