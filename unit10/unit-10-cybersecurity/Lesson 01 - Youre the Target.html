Lesson 1 - You're the Target (and That's OK)

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why ordinary people—not just celebrities and companies—encounter online attacks.
  • Describe online safety as a set of habits rather than deep technical skill.
  • Name the three kinds of threat this unit will help you handle: scams, misinformation, and threats to your accounts and privacy.

A common myth about online safety is that only wealthy, famous, or otherwise prominent people become targets. Many attacks, however, do not begin with a criminal choosing one particular person.

Attacks at scale

Much online crime is a numbers game. A scammer can send the same false text to millions of phones and wait for a small fraction of recipients to respond while busy, distracted, or unlucky. The attack is broad even though the consequences for each recipient are personal.

Ordinary accounts also have value. An email account may support password resets for other services. A payment account gives access to money, while a familiar social-media account can be used to scam the contacts who trust it. Attackers do not need a victim to be famous.

Security depends on systems and habits

Online safety does not require advanced technical training. Many common attacks rely on a person clicking a link, approving a login, reusing a password, or accepting a false story. Security tools still matter, but everyday decisions are part of the defense.

Useful habits include pausing before acting on an unexpected message, using a unique password or passkey for each account, enabling multifactor authentication, and recognizing the patterns common to scams. No one can eliminate all risk. The goal is to reduce preventable exposure and to recover quickly when prevention fails.

Try it: Make a quick inventory of the services connected to your primary email account. Which accounts use it for password recovery, purchase receipts, financial alerts, or stored photographs? This list will help identify which account protections deserve priority.

What this unit covers

The unit divides online safety into three practical areas:

  • Scams and social engineering: messages and calls designed to obtain money, information, or access.
  • Misinformation: false or misleading material, the systems that amplify it, and a routine for checking claims before believing or sharing them.
  • Accounts, privacy, and personal safety: authentication, data collection, harassment, and a step-by-step response when something goes wrong.

Each area includes actions that can be taken without becoming a security specialist. We will begin with the manipulation tactics used in scams.
