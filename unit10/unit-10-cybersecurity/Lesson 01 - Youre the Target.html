Lesson 1 - You're the Target (and That's OK)

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why ordinary people - not just celebrities and companies - are targets online.
  • Describe online safety as a set of habits rather than deep technical skill.
  • Name the three kinds of threat this unit will help you handle: scams, misinformation, and threats to your accounts and privacy.

Let's clear up the biggest myth about online safety right away: the idea that you're not important enough to be a target. "Why would anyone come after me? I'm not famous, I'm not rich, I've got nothing to hide." It feels reasonable. It's also exactly the thinking that gets people got.

Why you, specifically

Here's the thing: almost nobody is coming after you, specifically. That's the point. Modern online crime is a numbers game. A scammer doesn't hand-pick you and study your life - they fire the same fake text message at ten million phones and wait for the small percentage of people who are busy, distracted, or just unlucky enough to tap the link. You don't have to be interesting. You just have to be one of the ten million.

And you're worth more than you think. Your email account is a skeleton key - it can reset the passwords to everything else you own. Your debit card is cash. Your verified, real-looking social media account is a launchpad for scamming your friends and family, who trust messages that come from you. To the people running these schemes, a regular person with a phone is a perfectly good payday.

The good news: it's habits, not hacking

If that sounds exhausting, here's the part nobody tells you: staying safe online is much less about technology than you'd expect. You don't need to understand encryption math or be a "computer person." The overwhelming majority of real-world attacks succeed not by defeating some clever security system, but by fooling a human into opening the door - clicking the link, reusing the password, believing the story.

Which means the defenses are human too, and you already have most of them. A healthy pause before you click. A habit of not reusing passwords. Knowing the two or three tricks scammers lean on so you recognize them in the wild. It's a lot like locking your front door or not handing your wallet to a stranger who says they're a cop - you don't need a security degree, you need a few good reflexes. The goal isn't to be unhackable (nobody is). The goal is simply to be a harder target than the next person, because the numbers game moves on to easier marks.

Try it: take a quick mental inventory. If someone got into just your email account, what else could they reach - your bank? your shopping accounts? your other logins (through "forgot password")? your photos? Hold that list in mind; by the end of this unit you'll know how to put a real lock on it.

What this unit covers

We've split "protecting yourself online" into the threats you'll actually meet:

  • Scams & social engineering - the messages and calls engineered to trick you out of money or access, and how to spot them cold.
  • Misinformation - why your feed is full of stuff that's wrong (sometimes on purpose), and a simple toolkit for deciding what's actually true before you believe or share it.
  • Protecting your accounts, privacy, and self - passwords and two-factor that actually work, what you're giving away and how to claw some of it back, staying safe from harassment, and a calm step-by-step plan for when something goes wrong anyway.

None of it requires you to become a hacker. All of it is stuff you can start doing this week. Let's begin where the attackers do - with the art of the con.
