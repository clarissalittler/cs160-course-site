Lesson 1 - You're the Target (and That's OK)

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why ordinary people—not just celebrities and companies—encounter online attacks.
  • Describe online safety as a set of habits rather than deep technical skill.
  • Name the three kinds of threat this unit will help you handle: scams, misinformation, and threats to your accounts and privacy.

Here's a myth plenty of people believe about online safety: hackers and scammers only go after the rich, the famous, or the otherwise important. If you're none of those things, why would anybody bother with you?

The catch is that most attacks don't start with someone picking you in particular. They start with someone picking everyone.

Attacks at scale

Much of online crime is a numbers game. A scammer can send the same fake text to millions of phones and then just wait for the small fraction of people who happen to be busy, distracted, or unlucky that day. (It's the "one rule applied to everyone" idea from Unit 6, flipped around: a one-in-a-thousand success rate is great business when you're sending a million messages.) The attack is broad, but what happens to each person who falls for it is very personal.

Your ordinary accounts are worth something, too. Your email is probably where the password resets for everything else get sent, so whoever controls it can get into far more than your email. A payment account is money. And a social-media account your friends recognize is a great tool for scamming those friends, because they trust it. Nobody needs you to be famous for any of that to work.

Security depends on systems and habits

Here's the good news: staying reasonably safe online doesn't take advanced technical training. Many common attacks only work if a person clicks a link, approves a login, reuses a password, or believes a made-up story. Security software still matters! But the everyday decisions you make are part of the defense too.

The habits that help the most are simple ones: pause before acting on a message you weren't expecting, use a different password (or a passkey) for every account, turn on multifactor authentication, and get to know the patterns scams tend to follow. None of that makes you unhackable, and honestly, nobody is. The goal is to cut down on the risks you can prevent, and to recover quickly when something gets through anyway.

Try it: Pick either route. You can privately list the services connected to your main email account and decide which ones need protecting first. Or use this fictional case: Morgan's email gets bank alerts and purchase receipts, holds photo-sharing links, and is where Morgan's school account sends its password resets. Which account would you protect first, and why? Submit only your reasoning, not any account names or recovery details.

What this unit covers

We'll split online safety into three practical areas:

  • Scams and social engineering: messages and calls designed to obtain money, information, or access.
  • Misinformation: false or misleading material, the systems that amplify it, and a routine for checking claims before believing or sharing them.
  • Accounts, privacy, and personal safety: authentication, data collection, harassment, and a step-by-step response when something goes wrong.

Each one comes with things you can actually do, no security degree required. We'll start with the tricks scammers use to manipulate people.
