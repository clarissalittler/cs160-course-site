An Ancient Idea

We've spent the last few lessons talking about all the ways your data can be stolen or misused. Now let's talk about the main tool we have for keeping it safe: encryption. And the core idea is actually ancient—people have been hiding messages from prying eyes for thousands of years. The technology has changed, but the goal hasn't.

What Is Encryption?

Encryption is the process of taking readable data—called plaintext—and scrambling it into something unreadable—called ciphertext. Only someone with the right key can unscramble it back into the original message.

Think of it like a lockbox. Anyone can see the lockbox sitting on the table. They can pick it up, shake it, try to pry it open. But only someone with the key can actually open it and read what's inside.

That's the entire idea. Everything else in this lesson is details about how we do the scrambling and how we manage the keys. But the fundamental concept—make data unreadable to anyone who doesn't have the key—is the whole game.

The Caesar Cipher

Let's start with one of the oldest encryption methods we know about. Julius Caesar reportedly sent secret messages to his generals by shifting every letter in the alphabet by a fixed number of positions. If you shift by 3, then A becomes D, B becomes E, C becomes F, &c. The letter wraps around at the end—so X becomes A, Y becomes B, Z becomes C.

This is called the Caesar cipher, and the "key" is just the number of positions you shift. If you know the shift, you can decode the message. If you don't, you're stuck looking at gibberish—at least until you start trying different shifts (there are only 25 possibilities, so it's not exactly Fort Knox).

It's a toy by modern standards, but it illustrates the core concept perfectly: take readable text, apply a transformation with a key, get unreadable text. Apply the reverse transformation with the same key, get the original back.

Decode this message that was encrypted using a Caesar Cipher:

  • Once you figure out the shift for one letter, the rest all use the same shift.

Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Encryption

The Caesar cipher is an example of symmetric encryption—the same key is used to both encrypt and decrypt the message. You shift by 13 to scramble it, you shift by 13 (in the other direction) to unscramble it. Same key, both directions.

This is intuitive, and it works great—until you hit a problem. If you and I want to communicate using symmetric encryption, we both need the key. But how do I get the key to you safely? If I email it to you, someone could intercept it. If I text it, same problem. I could hand it to you in person, but that doesn't scale very well when you're trying to communicate securely with millions of people across the internet.

This is the key distribution problem, and for a long time, it was a really hard puzzle. The solution—one of the cleverest ideas in computer science—is asymmetric encryption, also called public key encryption.

Here's how it works: instead of one key, you have two. A public key and a private key. They're mathematically linked, but you can't figure out the private key from the public key (not in any reasonable amount of time, anyway).

  • The public key can encrypt data, but it can't decrypt it.
  • The private key can decrypt data that was encrypted with the matching public key.

Imagine a mailbox with a slot. Anyone walking by can drop a letter through the slot—that's the public key. But only you have the key to open the mailbox and read the letters inside—that's the private key. You can publish your public key to the entire world, and that's actually fine. It only lets people send you encrypted messages. Only your private key—which you never share with anyone—can read them.

This is what makes secure communication on the internet possible. When you visit a website and see that little lock icon in your browser's address bar, your computer and that website just performed a public-key handshake to set up an encrypted connection. All your data—passwords, credit card numbers, &c.—travels through that encrypted tunnel.

Why This Matters for You

You use encryption every single day, whether you realize it or not:

  • HTTPS — That "s" at the end of "https" stands for "secure." It means your connection to that website is encrypted. If you're entering a password or a credit card number on a site that's just "http" (no s, no lock icon)—don't. Your data is traveling without transport encryption, and attackers or intermediaries on the path could read or alter it.
  • End-to-end encrypted messaging — Apps like Signal use end-to-end encryption so that only you and the intended recipient can read the message contents in transit. Some other messaging apps offer this too, though the exact protections can depend on the app and its backup settings. (Not all messaging apps offer this by default—it's worth checking.)
  • Your phone's storage — Modern phones encrypt all the data on the device. If someone steals your phone but doesn't know your passcode, they can't read your files. The encryption does that for you, automatically.

Encryption isn't a silver bullet—it can't protect you from phishing, weak passwords, or giving your data to a company that doesn't protect it well. But it's one of the most important tools we have for keeping information private in a world where data is constantly moving across networks that we don't control.
