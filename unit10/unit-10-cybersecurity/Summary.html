Unit 10 Summary

Wrap up

This unit was all about practical online safety, and the big idea is that technology and habits work together. Encryption and passkeys do much of the work for you, but they can't stop you from typing your password into a fake page or passing along a made-up story, so most of what we practiced was the pause: noticing the pressure, checking through a channel you found yourself, and having a plan for when something goes wrong anyway. Nobody is unhackable, but these habits make the common problems less likely and easier to deal with.

What to carry forward

  • Most attacks aren't aimed at you in particular. They run at scale, because even an ordinary account is worth something to somebody, so good security is a mix of systems that protect you, habits you keep up, and a plan for recovering when something gets through.
  • Social engineering works on people instead of computers, using urgency, authority, fear, opportunity, or sympathy to rush you. That's why the pause matters so much: stop, and check through a phone number or website you found yourself rather than the one in the message.
  • Look closely at web addresses for mismatched or lookalike domains. And treat an unfamiliar QR code, an alert you weren't expecting, or a voice or video that could be synthetic as unverified until you've checked it another way.
  • Your feed isn't a neutral view of the world. It's ranked by engagement, which tends to favor emotional content and can spread misinformation, and generative tools now make it cheap to churn out endless variations. Before you trust or share something, try the SIFT moves: Stop, Investigate the source (by reading laterally), Find better coverage, and Trace claims and images back to the original.
  • Encryption keeps secrets safe while they travel. A Caesar cipher has only 26 keys, so you can just try them all, while a modern 128-bit key has so many possibilities that guessing is hopeless. Public-key cryptography solves the problem of sharing a key in the first place, digital signatures prove who sent something, and HTTPS protects the connection without promising anything about whether the site itself is honest.
  • For your accounts, use a different password for every service, keep them in a reputable password manager, and turn on the strongest MFA or passkey option you're offered. Passkeys and security keys hold up against phishing better than codes you type in yourself, since there's nothing for a fake page to collect.
  • Privacy is something you can manage on purpose. Every so often, look over your app permissions, your advertising and location settings, your browser controls, and what you're putting in public posts.
  • If you're harassed online, it isn't your fault, and your safety comes first. Save evidence when it's safe to, use the platform's controls, secure your accounts from a device you trust, and reach out to people you trust or to professional support.
  • Keep your software updated and keep a backup you know you can restore from. When something does go wrong, figure out what happened, save the evidence, use channels you've verified, and follow the steps that fit the account or data involved.

Looking back at the whole course

This is the last unit, so let's look back for a second.

Over ten weeks we've asked the same handful of questions in some very different places. How is this information represented, and what got left out? Where did the data come from? What rule is the program following, what does that rule assume, and who does it get wrong when it runs on everyone? What did the model learn from, and how would anyone know when it's wrong? And underneath all of it: who benefits, who's harmed, and who decides?

None of those questions are only about computers. They're questions you can ask about whatever computing is doing in your life and your field, long after this course is over.
