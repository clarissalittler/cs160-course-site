Unit 10 Summary

Wrap up

This unit was all about practical online safety, and the big idea is that technology and habits work together. Encryption and passkeys do a lot of the work for you, but they can't stop you from typing your password into a fake page or passing along a made-up story, so most of what we practiced was the pause: noticing the pressure, checking through a channel you found yourself, and having a plan for when something goes wrong anyway. Nobody is unhackable, but these habits make the common problems less likely and easier to deal with.

Summary

  • Many attacks operate at scale. Ordinary accounts have value, so effective security combines protective systems, careful habits, and a recovery plan.
  • Social engineering manipulates people. Urgency, authority, fear, opportunity, and sympathy can create pressure, so pause and verify through an independently chosen channel.
  • Inspect web addresses and requests. Look for mismatched or lookalike domains, and treat unfamiliar QR codes, unexpected alerts, and synthetic voices or video as unverified.
  • A feed is not a neutral view. Engagement-based ranking and emotional content can amplify misinformation, while generative tools reduce the cost of producing variations at scale.
  • Check before you trust or share using the SIFT moves: Stop, Investigate the source (read laterally), Find better coverage, and Trace claims and images to the original.
  • Encryption keeps secrets in transit. A Caesar cipher has only 26 keys and falls to brute force; modern 128-bit keys are hopeless to guess. Public-key cryptography solves the key-sharing problem, and digital signatures prove who sent something. HTTPS protects the connection, not the honesty of the site.
  • Protect accounts with a distinct password for every service, a reputable password manager, and the strongest available MFA or passkey option. Passkeys and security keys provide stronger phishing resistance than manually entered codes.
  • Manage privacy deliberately: review app permissions, advertising and location settings, browser controls, and the information included in public posts.
  • Respond to harassment with safety in mind. Being targeted is not the target's fault. Preserve evidence when safe, use platform controls, secure accounts from a safe device, and seek trusted or professional support.
  • Prepare for incidents. Keep software updated and maintain a recoverable backup. When something goes wrong, identify the incident, preserve evidence, use verified channels, and follow the response appropriate to the data or account involved.

Looking back at the whole course

This is the last unit, so let's look back for a second.

Over ten weeks we've asked the same handful of questions in a lot of very different places. How is this information represented, and what got left out? Where did the data come from? What rule is the program following, what does that rule assume, and who does it get wrong when it runs on everyone? What did the model learn from, and how would anyone know when it's wrong? And underneath all of it: who benefits, who's harmed, and who decides?

None of those questions are only about computers. They're questions you can ask about whatever computing is doing in your life and your field, long after this course is over.
