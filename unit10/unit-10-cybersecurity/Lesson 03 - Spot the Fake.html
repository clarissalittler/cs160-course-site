Lesson 3 - Spot the Fake

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Read a web address (URL) well enough to catch lookalike and fake domains.
  • Identify warning signs in a phishing message or fake login page without treating any single sign as proof.
  • Apply independent verification to QR-code scams and AI-generated voices or video.

Last lesson examined the psychology behind scams. We will now inspect the warning signs in messages and login pages. None of these signs is perfect by itself, but reading a web address and noticing pressure or an unexpected request provide useful evidence.

Learn to read a URL

A useful skill is reading the hostname in a URL, or web address. After https://, the hostname continues to the next slash, question mark, or end of the address. Within that hostname, the registered domain usually appears at the right, immediately before the public suffix such as .com or .org:

  • https://accounts.google.com/signin → the registered domain is google.com; accounts is a subdomain.
  • https://google.com.secure-login.ru/signin → the registered domain is secure-login.ru, not Google. The text google.com is only a subdomain chosen by the owner of secure-login.ru.
  • https://paypa1.com → the numeral 1 imitates a lowercase l. Lookalike domains also appear in sender addresses and link text.

On a computer, hover over a link without clicking to preview its destination. On many phones, press and hold the link. A mismatch between the visible label and destination is a warning; if an account may need attention, the safer response is to open the official app or enter a known address independently.

Phishing tells, and the fake login page

Other warning signs include urgency, an unexpected request for a password, verification code, payment, or gift card, and a destination that does not match the claimed sender. Because the page may closely copy a real sign-in screen, inspect the address bar before entering a password. QR codes and synthetic media

QR codes hide a destination until a device reads them. A fraudulent sticker placed over a legitimate code, for example, can lead to a fake payment page. Treat an unfamiliar QR code as an unfamiliar link and inspect the destination before continuing. Generative tools can also imitate a person's voice or appearance, so a familiar voice or video image is no longer sufficient proof of identity.

The most durable response is independent verification: when a message pressures you to act quickly, pause and make contact through a channel you already trust. Call a known number, open the official app, or use a verification phrase previously agreed upon with family. Leaving the channel initiated by the requester makes impersonation harder.

Try it: Select a promotional or alert email and, without opening its link, hover over or long-press the main button. Identify the hostname in the preview and decide whether it belongs to the organization named in the message.
