Lesson 3 - Spot the Fake

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Read a web address (URL) well enough to catch lookalike and fake domains.
  • List the concrete tells of a phishing message and a fake login page.
  • Recognize newer tricks - QR-code scams and AI-generated voices and video - and apply one rule that beats all of them.

Last lesson we met the psychology behind scams. Now let's get practical and train your eye on the tells - the little giveaways in a fake message that, once you know them, you can't un-see. The good news from the previous lesson still holds: you don't need to inspect anything technical. You mostly need to learn to read a web address and notice when a message is rushing you.

Learn to read a URL

The single most useful skill here is reading a URL (a web address) from right to left at the important part. The real destination is the bit just before the first single slash:

  • https://accounts.google.com/signin → really Google.
  • https://google.com.secure-login.ru/signin → not Google. The true domain is secure-login.ru; "google.com" is just dressed-up text in front of it.
  • https://paypaI.com → look closely - that's a capital I standing in for the lowercase l. Lookalike characters are a favorite trick.

On a computer you can hover over a link (without clicking) and the real destination shows in the corner of the screen - check that it matches the words you were promised. On a phone, press and hold the link to preview where it actually goes. If the visible text says one thing and the address says another, that's your answer.

Phishing tells, and the fake login page

Beyond the URL, the usual suspects from a phishing message: a sense of urgency ("act in 24 hours"), a generic greeting ("Dear Customer"), small spelling or formatting oddities, a request you wouldn't expect (passwords, gift cards, payment), and a link that doesn't go where it claims. The endgame of most phishing is a fake login page - a copy of a real sign-in screen that emails your password straight to the scammer. The page can look perfect; what gives it away is the address bar. Before you ever type a password, glance at the URL and ask: is this really the site I think it is? When in doubt, don't log in from a link at all - open a new tab and go to the site the way you normally would.

Newer tricks - and the one rule that beats them

Scammers have new tools. QR codes ("quishing") hide a destination you can't read at a glance - a sticker slapped over the real one on a parking meter can send you to a fake payment page, so treat a QR code like any other unknown link. And thanks to AI (remember Unit 9), scammers can now clone a voice from a few seconds of audio - the panicked "grandma, it's me, I'm in trouble, send money" call may not be a person you know at all - or even run a deepfake video on a call. Convincing fakery is now cheap.

You could spend forever memorizing new variations. Don't. There's one rule that handles nearly all of them: when something pressures you to act fast, stop and verify on a channel you choose yourself. Hang up and call the real number on the back of your card or your family member's actual phone. Don't reply to the text - open the app. The scam depends on you staying inside the conversation they started; stepping outside it is how you win.

Try it: grab the next promotional or "alert" email in your own inbox and, without clicking, hover over (or long-press) its main button or link. Does the address that pops up actually belong to the company it claims to be from? You'll be surprised how often it doesn't.
