Lesson 4 - Why You Can't Trust Your Feed

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain the difference between misinformation and disinformation
  • Describe how the "attention economy" tunes your feed for engagement rather than accuracy
  • Identify the main mechanisms that spread false information online - algorithms, clickbait, echo chambers, bots, and emotional manipulation
  • Explain how generative AI has lowered the cost of producing convincing fakes at scale

Here's an uncomfortable truth to start with: your feed isn't a window on the world. It's a slot machine built to keep you pulling the lever, and what keeps you pulling isn't what's true - it's what makes you feel something.

That's not a conspiracy theory. It's just how the business works. And once you understand how the machine is built, you stop being so easy to play. That's the whole point of this lesson and the next one.

Two words that get mixed up: misinformation and disinformation

People use these interchangeably, but the difference actually matters.

Misinformation is false stuff spread without the intent to deceive. Your aunt shares a post claiming that a certain spice cures the flu. She's not lying to you - she genuinely believes it and thinks she's helping. She's wrong, but she means well.

Disinformation is false stuff spread on purpose to deceive or manipulate. Someone invented that spice-cures-the-flu claim because they're selling the spice. They know it's false. They want you to believe it anyway.

The effect on you is identical - you end up believing something untrue. The difference is the motive behind it. And here's the kicker: disinformation often spreads through well-meaning people. The person who invents the lie only needs a few thousand aunts to do the sharing for free.

Why falsehood travels faster than truth

You'd hope that true things would naturally win out - that good information would crowd out the bad. It doesn't work that way, and the reason comes down to money.

Social platforms make their money from your attention. The longer you stay, the more ads you see, the more they earn. So everything about your feed is tuned toward one goal: keep you here, keep you scrolling, keep you reacting. That goal is called the attention economy, and accuracy is simply not part of it.

What keeps people scrolling? Not careful, balanced, true things. Those are often kind of boring. What keeps people scrolling is the stuff that's shocking, novel, outrageous, or scary. Researchers who studied millions of posts found that false news spreads faster and reaches more people than true news - largely because lies can be designed to be surprising and emotional, while the truth is stuck being whatever it actually is. Truth is often boring. A lie can be built to be irresistible.

The machinery, piece by piece

A handful of mechanisms do most of the work. None of them is exotic - you've felt all of them.

Engagement-driven algorithms. The feed watches what you react to and gives you more of it. Pause on an angry post, leave a heated comment, and the system learns "this person engages with outrage" and serves up more outrage. It isn't asking whether any of it is true. It's asking what'll keep you tapping.

Clickbait and misleading headlines. "You Won't BELIEVE What This Doctor Discovered." The headline is engineered to make you click before you think, and it often promises far more than the article delivers - if there even is an article. The headline did its job the moment you reacted to it.

Echo chambers and filter bubbles. Because the algorithm shows you more of what you already agree with, over time you mostly see one side of things. It starts to feel like "everyone" thinks the way you do, which makes a shaky claim feel obviously true. You're not seeing the world - you're seeing a room the algorithm built around you.

Bots and fake accounts. A single person can run hundreds of fake accounts, or pay for them, to make a message look way more popular than it is. When something seems to be everywhere at once, your brain reads "lots of people believe this, so it must be real" - even when it's really one operator pushing the same thing through a thousand puppets.

Emotional manipulation. Rage and fear are the most shareable emotions there are. A post that makes you furious or frightened gets reshared far more than one that makes you go "huh, interesting." So the stuff engineered to enrage or scare you gets a built-in boost - which is exactly why so much of what goes viral is designed to push those buttons.

The new accelerant: generative AI

Back in Unit 9 we looked at AI that can write essays and generate images. Here's the catch we didn't dwell on: the very same technology can mass-produce fakes.

It used to take real effort to flood the internet with lies. You had to write the fake articles, stage the fake photos, hire people to post the fake reviews. Now a generative model can crank out thousands of fake news stories, fake product reviews, fake "people" with fake profile photos, and convincing fake images and video - deepfakes - at nearly zero cost.

The phrase people use is "flooding the zone": burying the truth under so much plausible-looking nonsense that nobody can tell what's real anymore. AI didn't invent disinformation, but it dropped the price of producing it close to zero. That changes the scale of the problem entirely.

Who benefits? Who decides?

It's tempting to picture a lone villain behind all this, but the honest answer is messier. Scammers and propagandists benefit, sure. But so do the platforms that profit from every minute you spend enraged, and so does anyone with something to sell who's figured out that outrage moves product.

The thing to sit with is this: the feed you see was shaped by choices someone made - which posts to boost, which to bury, what counts as "engagement" worth rewarding. Those choices were made for someone's benefit, and that someone usually isn't you. Whenever a piece of content is working hard to make you feel something, it's worth asking the Computing & Society question we keep coming back to: who benefits if I believe this, and who benefits if I share it?

This isn't hopeless

I want to be clear that the answer is not "trust nothing and assume everyone's lying." That's its own kind of trap - if you can't believe anything, you'll just believe whatever feels good, which is right back where we started.

The answer is to understand how the machine works so you're not played by it. You can enjoy your feed and stay skeptical of it at the same time. Knowing that a headline is built to enrage you takes a lot of its power away. And once you know what to look for, you can actually check things - which is exactly where we're headed next.

Try it: Open your own feed and scroll for about two minutes. As you go, notice which posts are clearly engineered to make you angry or afraid - the all-caps headlines, the "they don't want you to know this," the things that make your jaw clench. For two or three of them, ask yourself: who benefits if I share this? You don't have to do anything about them. Just practice noticing the machinery while it's running.
