Lesson 4 - Why You Can't Trust Your Feed

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain the difference between misinformation and disinformation
  • Describe how the "attention economy" can prioritize engagement over accuracy
  • Identify the main mechanisms that spread false information online - algorithms, clickbait, echo chambers, bots, and emotional manipulation
  • Explain how generative AI has lowered the cost of producing convincing fakes at scale

It's easy to think of your feed as a window: you look through it and see what's going on in the world. But it's not a window. It's a selection, put together from the accounts you follow, the things other people share, paid promotion, and ranking systems whose job is to predict what will keep you scrolling.

Once you know how that selection gets made, it's much easier to make sense of what's in your feed, what's missing from it, and why some things keep showing up again and again. That's this lesson. The next one gives you a method for checking whether a claim is actually true.

Misinformation and disinformation

These two words get mixed up all the time. The difference between them is all about intent.

Misinformation is false or misleading information shared without meaning to deceive anybody. Your aunt passing along a health tip she honestly thinks will help people is spreading misinformation.

Disinformation is false or misleading information spread on purpose, to deceive or manipulate. Someone who makes up that same health tip so they can sell you supplements is spreading disinformation.

Either way, you end up believing something that isn't true. And here's the uncomfortable part: deliberate disinformation often gets most of its reach from sincere people like your aunt, who share it because they believe it.

Why false news spreads

You might hope that true information would just naturally win out over false information. It doesn't, and part of the reason is that the platform, the advertisers, the people making content, and you, the reader, don't all want the same thing.

Lots of social platforms make their money by selling your attention to advertisers. So their ranking systems pay close attention to signals like clicks, watch time, comments, and shares. That's the attention economy. Platforms may use other signals too, like quality and safety ones, but "lots of people engaged with this" and "this is accurate" are just not the same goal.

And what grabs attention? Things that are new, surprising, outrageous, or scary. A major study of millions of Twitter posts found that false news in its dataset spread farther and faster than true news, and novelty and emotional reactions were among the likely reasons. That doesn't mean every false claim goes viral! It does show that the truth doesn't get any built-in head start in a system built around sharing.

The machinery, piece by piece

A few different mechanisms feed into this. It helps to know them by name, because once you can name them you'll start spotting them everywhere:

Engagement-driven ranking. The system uses what you've watched and interacted with to predict what you'll open, watch, or comment on next. So if you keep engaging with stuff that makes you angry, you'll probably get more stuff that makes you angry, true or not.

Clickbait and misleading headlines. "You Won't BELIEVE What This Doctor Discovered" leaves out all the context and promises something dramatic. It can get the click even when the article underneath doesn't back it up at all.

Echo chambers. What you choose to follow, who your friends are, and what the ranking system serves up can all push you toward seeing the same views over and over. Then a claim can start to feel like something "everyone" believes, when really your feed is showing you a narrow slice of people.

Bots and coordinated accounts. Automated accounts, or lots of accounts run by the same group, can make a message look far more popular than it really is. Seeing something repeated over and over might mean many people agree, or it might just mean somebody organized it.

Emotional manipulation. Anger and fear make people share fast. Content built to make you angry or scared gets to ride that reaction, and it's usually traveled a long way before anyone stops to check it.

Generative AI

In Unit 9 we looked at systems that generate text and images. Unsurprisingly, the same tools are great at producing misleading stuff, too.

Generative models make it fast and cheap to crank out fake articles, fake reviews, fake profile pictures, and synthetic audio or video (including deepfakes). They can also spin out dozens of slightly different versions of the same message, each one aimed at a different audience.

One strategy, sometimes called "flooding the zone," is to bury reliable information under so many contradictory or plausible-looking claims that checking them all becomes exhausting, and people just give up. Generative AI didn't invent disinformation. People were doing this long before chatbots! But it can make the production much faster and bigger.

Who benefits? Who decides?

There isn't one villain here. Scammers and propagandists can benefit directly, content creators can get attention or sales, and platforms that run on advertising can make money from you staying on longer. Different companies make different choices, though, so it's better to look into the details than to assume.

Your feed is shaped by a bunch of choices people made: which posts to rank higher, which kinds of engagement count as "valuable," and what to hold back. So when something hits you with a strong emotion, it's a great time to ask our recurring Computing & Society questions. Who benefits if I believe this? Who benefits if I share it?

What to do about it

None of this means you should trust nothing and assume everybody is lying. That sounds savvy, but it throws away something important: the difference between strong evidence and weak evidence.

What we want instead is to understand how a feed gets put together, recognize when it's leaning on our emotions, and check the claims that matter. The next lesson gives you a routine for doing exactly that.

Try it: Compare these two fictional posts: “SHOCKING secret ban — share before it disappears!” and “Council minutes: proposed rule discussed; vote scheduled next month.” What about the first one pushes you to react quickly? Who benefits if people click or share it? Or, if you'd rather, look through a feed privately and describe the same features, without sharing anyone's posts or account details. (Keep in mind that tone alone doesn't tell you whether either claim is true!)
