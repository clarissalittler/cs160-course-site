Lesson 4 - Why You Can't Trust Your Feed

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain the difference between misinformation and disinformation
  • Describe how the "attention economy" can prioritize engagement over accuracy
  • Identify the main mechanisms that spread false information online - algorithms, clickbait, echo chambers, bots, and emotional manipulation
  • Explain how generative AI has lowered the cost of producing convincing fakes at scale

A social-media feed is not a neutral window on the world. It is a selection produced by accounts we follow, material other people share, paid promotion, and ranking systems that predict what will hold our attention.

Understanding that selection process helps us interpret what a feed includes, what it omits, and why particular material appears repeatedly. This lesson examines the process; the next develops a method for checking claims.

Misinformation and disinformation

The terms are related, but the difference concerns intent.

Misinformation is false or misleading information shared without an intention to deceive. A person might repeat an unsupported health claim while sincerely believing it will help others.

Disinformation is false or misleading information spread deliberately to deceive or manipulate. A seller might invent the same health claim in order to profit from it.

Both can leave an audience with a false belief, but the motive behind their creation or circulation differs. Deliberate disinformation often spreads farther through people who share it sincerely.

Why falsehood travels faster than truth

Accurate information does not automatically crowd out false information. One reason is that the goals of a platform, advertiser, content creator, and reader do not always align.

Many social platforms earn advertising revenue from users' attention. Ranking systems therefore consider signals such as clicks, viewing time, comments, and shares. This is part of the attention economy. Platforms may also use other quality and safety signals, but engagement and accuracy are not the same objective.

Novel, surprising, outrageous, or frightening material can attract attention. A major study of millions of Twitter posts found that false news in its dataset spread farther and faster than true news; novelty and emotional response were among the possible explanations. The result does not mean every false claim travels widely, but it shows why truth has no automatic advantage in a sharing system.

The machinery, piece by piece

Several mechanisms can contribute to this pattern:

Engagement-driven ranking. A system can use viewing and interaction history to predict what someone will open, watch, or discuss. Repeated engagement with outrage may therefore lead to more similar material, whether or not it is accurate.

Clickbait and misleading headlines. A headline such as "You Won't BELIEVE What This Doctor Discovered" withholds context and promises a dramatic result. It may attract a click or reaction even when the article does not support the implication.

Echo chambers and selective exposure. Personal choices, social networks, and ranking systems can all increase repeated exposure to familiar views. A claim may then seem broadly accepted even when the feed represents only a narrow group.

Bots and coordinated accounts. Automated or centrally managed accounts can make a message appear more popular than it is. Apparent repetition may reflect coordination rather than independent agreement.

Emotional manipulation. Anger and fear can motivate rapid sharing. Content designed to provoke those emotions may benefit from that reaction before readers investigate the claim.

The new accelerant: generative AI

Unit 9 introduced systems that generate text and images. The same tools can also produce misleading material.

Generative models reduce the time and cost required to produce false articles, reviews, profile images, and synthetic audio or video, including deepfakes. They can also generate many variations of a message for different audiences.

One strategy, sometimes called "flooding the zone," is to surround reliable information with so many contradictory or plausible-looking claims that verification becomes exhausting. Generative AI did not invent disinformation, but it can increase the speed and scale of production.

Who benefits? Who decides?

The incentives are distributed. Scammers and propagandists may benefit directly, content creators may gain attention or sales, and advertising-supported platforms may earn revenue from continued use. Different systems and organizations make different choices, so the details should be investigated rather than assumed.

A feed is shaped by choices about which posts to rank, which signals count as valuable engagement, and which material to limit. When content strongly provokes an emotion, ask the recurring Computing & Society questions: Who benefits if I believe this? Who benefits if I share it?

Skepticism without cynicism

The answer is not to trust nothing or assume that every source is lying. Total cynicism abandons the distinction between stronger and weaker evidence.

Instead, we can understand how a feed is assembled, recognize emotional pressure, and check important claims. The next lesson provides a repeatable method for doing so.

Try it: Review a feed for about two minutes and identify two or three posts that use anger, fear, all-capital headlines, or secret-knowledge framing. For each, ask who benefits if the post is opened or shared. The task is to notice the incentives and selection process.
