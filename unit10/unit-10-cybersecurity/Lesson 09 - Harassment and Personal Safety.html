Lesson 9 - Harassment and Personal Safety

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Recognize forms of online harassment, including doxxing, account takeover, stalkerware, cyberstalking, and impersonation
  • Take proactive steps to limit unnecessary public exposure
  • Follow a safety-conscious response plan if harassment is occurring
  • Identify sources of help when there is a threat to personal safety

Most of this unit has been about scammers and systems: phishing messages, fake websites, companies getting breached. Some online threats are much more personal than that. They can come from a former partner, one particular harasser, someone pretending to be you, or a group of people working together.

This happens to ordinary people, not just famous ones. So I want to say this clearly before anything else: if it happens to you, it is not your fault. And there are real, practical steps that can help you keep evidence, lock down your accounts, and get support.

What it can look like

Online harassment comes in a few different forms. Knowing the names helps, because it makes it easier to describe what's happening when you ask for help:

  • Doxxing - publishing someone's private information, such as a home address, workplace, or phone number, in order to expose, frighten, or threaten them.
  • Account takeover - gaining control of another person's social-media or email account, locking out its owner, or using it to impersonate them.
  • Stalkerware - software used to monitor a device's location, messages, calls, or other activity without the user's informed consent. An abusive person with physical or account access may install or enable it.
  • Cyberstalking and coordinated harassment - repeated unwanted contact from one person or a coordinated flood of insults, threats, and messages from many accounts.
  • Impersonation - using a false account or stolen account to appear to speak as another person.

Reduce your exposure before anything happens

No privacy setting can control what another person chooses to do. What settings can do is cut down on how much information about you is sitting out there, easy for someone to misuse.

  • Review your social privacy settings. Use the controls for who can see your posts and who can message, mention, or tag you. Pick settings that fit what the account is for.
  • Be careful with your location and routine. Skip public live-location posts, and look over photos for anything that gives away an address, a license plate, a workplace, or a recognizable landmark. If you share photos somewhere that doesn't strip out location data automatically, check whether it's still attached.
  • Separate your public and private identities. If you post publicly for work, a club, or a hobby, keep that separate from the account where your family photos and personal details live.
  • Search for yourself. Searching your name and the usernames you use shows you what a stranger could find. Ask for things to be removed where that makes sense, but don't post what you find as part of this exercise.
  • Check people-search sites. Many of them have an opt-out process. Getting removed from one site won't erase the original public records, and the information can show up again later. But it does take away one easy way to look you up.
  • Lock down your main email. Your email is usually how you get back into everything else, so protect it with its own unique password and the strongest multifactor or passkey option it offers.

Supplied case: A fictional public profile shows someone's home address, their weekly work schedule, and posts with their live location. Suggest a plan to cut down that unnecessary exposure, and name one place they could get support if harassment starts. You don't need to search for your own information or change any account for this.

Optional self-audit: Search your name, look at one of your main social accounts the way a stranger would see it, and change one public detail or privacy setting you don't need. Don't post your search results or any other personal information for the course. If changing a setting could alert someone who is monitoring you, use a safer device and get support before you change anything.

If it's already happening

If this isn't hypothetical for you, your immediate safety comes first. You don't have to do these steps in order. When you can, use a device and account that the person harassing you can't get into.

  • Don't retaliate. Responding can make the contact worse, or hand the harasser more material to use. You don't owe them a reply.
  • Save evidence, when it's safe to. Write down dates, usernames, URLs, messages, and what was going on, before any of it disappears. Some apps tell the other person when you take a screenshot, so using a second, safe device can be better.
  • Use the platform's tools. Once you've saved the evidence, report the harassment or impersonation, and block or restrict the accounts involved if that fits your safety plan.
  • Secure your accounts from a safe device. Check which devices are logged in and what your recovery information is, change any passwords that were compromised or reused, and turn on strong authentication. Don't make these changes on a device you think is being monitored.
  • Tell someone you trust. A friend, family member, counselor, advocate, or campus support office can help you keep track of what's happening and think through your options. You don't have to handle this alone.
  • If your safety is threatened, escalate. Contact the platform, your school, your workplace, a victim-support organization, or local emergency resources, whichever fits. If you're in immediate danger, contact emergency services where you are.

One special case needs extra care. If you think there's stalkerware on your phone, don't immediately delete the app or reset the phone. That can alert the person watching the device, and it can make things more dangerous. Instead, from a safer device, look at the FTC's stalkerware guidance or talk with a domestic-violence advocate, such as the National Domestic Violence Hotline, before you decide what to do.

Responsibility and support

I said it at the start, and I'll end on it too: being targeted is not your fault. It isn't because of something you posted, a photo you shared, or a setting you missed. The responsibility belongs to the person doing the harassing. Asking for help, whether from someone you trust, an advocate, the platform, your school, your workplace, or a public agency, is a completely reasonable thing to do to stay safe.
