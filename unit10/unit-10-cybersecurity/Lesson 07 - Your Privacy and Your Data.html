Lesson 7 - Your Privacy and Your Data

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what personally identifiable information (PII) is, and recognize the less-obvious data you give away just by living online
  • Describe who collects your data and why - including the "free" business model, app permissions, and data brokers
  • Discuss why privacy matters even if you feel you have "nothing to hide," and where surveillance creates a power imbalance between you and the organizations holding your data
  • Take concrete steps to reduce how much you leak - app permissions, ad settings, browser choices, and what you post

So far in this unit we've mostly talked about people attacking you - phishing, malware, scams, someone trying to get into your accounts. This lesson is different. This is about all the data you hand over when nobody's attacking you at all. You're just going about your day, using your phone, and a quiet, constant stream of information about you flows out into the world.

That's not a horror story. It's worth understanding clearly, because the more you know about what's happening, the better you can decide what you're actually okay with.

What you leak just by living online

Some of your data is the obvious stuff. Personally identifiable information (PII) is any information that can be used to identify you specifically - your name, birthday, home address, phone number, Social Security number, that kind of thing. It's the data you'd guard on a form.

But honestly, the obvious stuff is the least interesting part. The valuable part is everything you don't think of as "information about you":

  • Where you go - your phone logs your location all day long
  • What you search for, what you buy, what you almost bought and then didn't
  • Who you talk to, how often, and at what hours
  • How long you lingered on a post, a photo, a product - even the things you scrolled past slowly

Any one of those seems harmless. Stack thousands of them together and you get a startlingly detailed portrait of a person - their habits, health, politics, relationships, moods. You never sat down and wrote that profile. It got assembled out of the exhaust from your everyday life.

Who collects it, and why

Here's the thing worth sitting with: if you're not paying for the product, you often are the product - your attention and your data are what's being sold. That's not a conspiracy; it's the business model. The "free" app, the free email, the free social network - they make money by knowing enough about you to sell advertisers the chance to reach exactly you.

This is why app permissions are worth a second look. Why does a flashlight app want your contacts and your location? A flashlight needs the flash. When an app asks for far more than its job requires, the extra access usually isn't for you - it's data the app can use or sell. The permission screen is the one moment you get to say no, and most of us tap "Allow" without reading it.

And then there are the companies you've never heard of. Data brokers are businesses whose entire job is to buy, combine, package, and resell information about people. They pull from apps, loyalty cards, public records, and other brokers, then build profiles they sell to advertisers, insurers, employers, even debt collectors. You never signed up with them. You probably can't name a single one. They likely have a file on you anyway.

Two places this gets vivid: your face and your location

Most data collection is invisible. Two kinds make it concrete, because they track the physical, in-the-world you.

The first is facial recognition. Your face is a kind of permanent ID you can't change and can't leave at home. Cameras in stores, airports, and on the street can increasingly pick your face out of a crowd and match it to a name - no badge to swipe, no phone to check, no consent asked.

The second is location data. Your phone is, more or less, a continuous beacon broadcasting where you are. Carriers and apps can build a minute-by-minute map of your movements - where you sleep, work, worship, see a doctor. That's sensitive enough that the courts have gotten involved: in the U.S., the Supreme Court ruled in Carpenter v. United States (2018) that police generally need a warrant to get your historical cell phone location records.

Both of these are still being argued over - in courtrooms, in legislatures, around kitchen tables. That's a sign these are genuinely hard questions, not settled ones.

But I have nothing to hide

This is the most common response to all of this, and it deserves a real answer rather than an eye-roll. Privacy isn't really about hiding wrongdoing. It's about who gets to decide what's known about you and how it gets used.

A few reasons that matters even for someone with a completely boring, lawful life:

  • Data is basically forever. Something you share today can resurface years from now, in a world with different norms and different consequences.
  • Contexts collapse. Something perfectly fine in one setting - a joke among friends, a medical search, your address - can be damaging when it lands in front of a different audience.
  • Information drifts from its original purpose. Data collected for one reason (say, to suggest a route home) gets reused for another (building a profile, training a system, getting handed to a third party) without anyone asking you again.

You don't have to be a privacy absolutist. Plenty of people happily trade some data for genuine convenience, and that's a legitimate choice. The point isn't to hide - it's to draw your own line on purpose instead of having it drawn for you by whoever wrote the default settings.

What you can actually do

The good news: this isn't all-or-nothing, and you're not helpless. A handful of small moves meaningfully cut down what you leak.

  • Review your app permissions. Go through your phone and pull back access that doesn't match what the app does - location, microphone, contacts, photos. The flashlight does not need your contacts.
  • Turn off ad personalization and location history where your phone, browser, and accounts let you. These options are usually buried in settings, but they're there.
  • Use a privacy-respecting browser and block third-party cookies - those are the trackers that follow you site to site stitching your browsing together.
  • Skim a privacy policy before signing up for something that matters. You don't need to read every word; search for "collect," "share," and "sell" to get the gist fast.
  • Think before you post. Data is permanent. "Would I be fine with this in five years, in front of anyone?" is a decent gut check.
  • Consider other people, too. When you tag a friend, post a group photo, or upload your contacts, you're making a privacy decision on someone else's behalf. They didn't get a vote.

Try it: Open your phone's privacy or permissions settings (on iPhone: Settings → Privacy & Security; on Android: Settings → Privacy or Security → Permission manager). Scroll through and find one app that has a permission it clearly doesn't need to do its job - location, microphone, or contacts is a good place to look - and revoke it. Notice how many apps had access you'd forgotten about. Then take the bigger question to the Unit 10 - Privacy: Where Do You Draw the Line? discussion in D2L: where do you draw the line between convenience and privacy, and why? Post your answer - and anything surprising you found in your settings - there.

Computing & society: who benefits, who's harmed, who decides?

Step back and the same three questions from earlier units apply hard here. Who benefits from collecting all this data? Mostly the companies selling it and the advertisers buying it. Who's harmed when it leaks, gets resold, or gets used against someone - in a hiring decision, an insurance rate, a wrongful facial-recognition match? Usually the person being profiled, who often never knew the data existed.

And who decides? That's the uncomfortable part. There's a real asymmetry: the companies and governments holding your data know an enormous amount about you, while you know very little about them - what they've collected, who they've shared it with, how long they'll keep it. Closing that gap is partly personal (the settings above) and partly collective - it's why privacy laws, regulators, and public debates like the ones in those videos matter. You're one person against a system that runs on defaults, so the defaults & the rules are worth caring about, not just your own choices.
