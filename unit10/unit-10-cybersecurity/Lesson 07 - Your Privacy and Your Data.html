Lesson 7 - Your Privacy and Your Data

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain personally identifiable information (PII) and identify less-obvious data produced through online activity
  • Describe why organizations collect data through advertising models, app permissions, and data brokers
  • Discuss privacy as a question of control and power rather than simply having "nothing to hide"
  • Use app permissions, account settings, browser controls, and posting choices to limit unnecessary disclosure

So far, this unit has concentrated on phishing, malware, scams, and account compromise. This lesson concerns data collected during ordinary use, when no attack is taking place. Phones, applications, websites, and connected services can produce a continuing record of activity.

The goal is not alarm. It is to understand the collection well enough to make deliberate choices about convenience, disclosure, and control.

Data produced through ordinary use

Some data is easy to recognize as sensitive. Personally identifiable information (PII) is information that identifies or can be linked to a particular person, such as a name, home address, phone number, or Social Security number. The precise legal definition varies by context, but the underlying question is whether information can be connected to an individual.

Other data may seem less personal even though it can reveal a great deal when combined:

  • Location observations collected by an operating system, carrier, or application
  • Searches, purchases, and items placed in a cart but not purchased
  • Communication metadata, including who contacted whom and when
  • Interaction data, such as how long a post, photograph, or product remained on screen

Any one observation may reveal little. Combined across time and sources, however, the data can support inferences about habits, health, politics, relationships, and routines. The resulting profile is assembled from activity rather than written explicitly by the person it describes.

Who collects it, and why

Many services offered without a monetary fee earn revenue from advertising. They may use behavior and profile information to select audiences and sell advertisers access to those audiences. This does not always mean that a company literally sells a file containing a person's name, but the business model still assigns value to attention and data.

App permissions therefore deserve review. A flashlight application, for example, ordinarily has no functional reason to access contacts. A request for data unrelated to an application's purpose should prompt us to deny the permission, investigate the explanation, or choose a different application.

Data brokers collect, combine, analyze, and share or sell information from sources such as applications, loyalty programs, public records, and other brokers. Their products serve advertising, identity verification, people search, risk analysis, and other purposes. A person may appear in a broker's records without having dealt with that company directly.

Two places this gets vivid: your face and your location

Two categories make the connection to physical life especially clear.

The first is facial recognition. A face is a persistent biometric characteristic that is difficult to replace if the associated template is compromised. Depending on the system and setting, cameras may be used to compare a face with enrolled images without the explicit interaction required by a badge or phone.

The second is location data. Cellular networks, operating systems, and applications may collect different forms of location information. Across time, those records can reveal where a person sleeps, works, worships, or receives medical care. In the United States, the Supreme Court held in Carpenter v. United States (2018) that the government generally must obtain a warrant before acquiring historical cell-site location records of the kind at issue in that case.

The rules and norms governing both technologies continue to develop. The unresolved questions involve not only what is technically possible, but also what institutions should be allowed to do.

But I have nothing to hide

"I have nothing to hide" is a common response, and it deserves a direct answer. Privacy is not limited to concealing wrongdoing. It also concerns who decides what is known about a person, in which context, and for what purpose.

A few reasons that matters even for someone with a completely boring, lawful life:

  • Data can persist. Copies, archives, screenshots, and backups can cause material shared today to resurface years later.
  • Contexts collapse. Something perfectly fine in one setting - a joke among friends, a medical search, your address - can be damaging when it lands in front of a different audience.
  • Information drifts from its original purpose. Data collected for one reason (say, to suggest a route home) gets reused for another (building a profile, training a system, getting handed to a third party) without anyone asking you again.

Privacy need not be all or nothing. People may reasonably exchange some data for convenience. The point is to make that exchange deliberately rather than accept every default without review.

Practical controls

No setting eliminates data collection, but several controls can reduce unnecessary disclosure.

  • Review app permissions. Remove access to location, microphone, contacts, or photographs when it does not match the application's function. Where available, choose limited or one-time access.
  • Review advertising and location settings in the operating system, browser, and major accounts. Turning off personalization does not stop all collection, but it changes some uses.
  • Use browser privacy controls to limit cross-site tracking, including third-party cookies and other tracking technologies. Protections vary by browser and mode.
  • Review the privacy information before using a sensitive service. Look for what it collects, why it collects it, whom it shares data with, how long it retains data, and which controls are available.
  • Think before posting. Ask how a post might be copied, combined with other information, or viewed in a different context later.
  • Consider other people. Tagging a friend, posting a group photograph, or uploading contacts also makes a privacy choice about someone else.

Try it: Open the privacy or permissions settings on your phone. Menu names vary by device and operating-system version. Find one application with access to location, microphone, contacts, or photographs, and decide whether that access is necessary for its function. If not, revoke or limit it. Then explain where you draw the line between convenience and privacy.

Computing & society: who benefits, who's harmed, who decides?

The three questions from earlier units apply here. Who benefits from collecting the data? Benefits may go to the service receiving revenue, the advertiser reaching an audience, and the user receiving a feature. Who is harmed when data leaks, is repurposed, or contributes to a wrongful match or adverse decision? The costs may fall on a person who did not know the profile existed.

Who decides? Organizations holding data may know far more about an individual than the individual knows about their collection, sharing, retention, or decision systems. Personal settings address only part of that asymmetry. Product defaults, organizational policies, laws, regulators, and public debate also shape the available choices.
