Lesson 2 - Scams and Social Engineering

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what social engineering is and why most scams target the person, not the computer.
  • Name the emotional pressures scammers use to encourage action before reflection.
  • Recognize phishing in its different forms (email, text, phone, and targeted messages).
  • Apply a verification routine before clicking, calling, sharing information, or paying.

Social engineering targets people

Some attacks exploit software; others persuade a person to bypass a safeguard. A criminal may find it easier to request a verification code than to defeat a bank's technical security.

This tactic is called social engineering: manipulating someone into providing money, information, or access. It relies on psychology and context as well as technology.

Recognizing a tactic does not make anyone immune, especially when a message arrives at a stressful moment. It does make a deliberate verification routine easier to follow.

The emotional levers

Many scams use the same pressures to encourage a quick decision before the recipient verifies the story.

  • Urgency. "Act now or your account closes in 24 hours." A short deadline discourages independent checking.
  • Authority. A sender claims to represent a bank, government agency, police department, or supervisor in order to make compliance feel obligatory.
  • Fear. A warning about account fraud or illegal activity can shift attention from verification to immediate action.
  • Opportunity. A prize or unusually low price may distract from a suspicious website or payment method.
  • Sympathy or romance. Some scams build trust over time before introducing an emergency and a request for money.

The stories differ, but the pattern is consistent: strong emotion followed by pressure to act. That pressure is a cue to pause and verify.

Phishing: the workhorse of scams

The most common delivery method is phishing - a message that pretends to be from someone trustworthy to trick you into clicking a bad link, opening a bad attachment, or typing your password into a fake login page. (The name is a play on "fishing": they cast out lots of bait and see who bites.)

Phishing comes in a few flavors depending on how it reaches you:

  • Phishing - the classic, by email. The fake "your package couldn't be delivered" or "verify your account" message.
  • Smishing - the same trick by text message (SMS). Short, casual, and easy to tap without thinking.
  • Vishing - by voice: a phone call or voicemail, often a recorded "this is the IRS" or a fake fraud-department agent.
  • Spear phishing - a targeted message that uses real details, such as a name, employer, or recent transaction, to appear credible.

A quick field guide to common scams

The details change, but several recurring patterns are useful to recognize:

  • Fake bank / account alerts - "suspicious login detected, click here to secure your account."
  • Tech support - "your computer is infected! Call this number now" (often a pop-up that hijacks your screen).
  • Gift card demands - someone claiming official authority insists that a bill, fine, or emergency must be paid with retail gift cards. Legitimate government agencies and businesses do not require this form of payment for a debt.
  • Romance scams - an online relationship that gets serious fast, then needs money for a crisis.
  • Fake invoices / subscriptions - "your $499 renewal has been charged - call to dispute," to get you on the phone.
  • Job and check-overpayment scams - a too-easy work-from-home gig that "accidentally" overpays you and asks you to wire back the difference.
  • Marketplace and delivery-text scams - "your package is held, pay a small fee to release it," or a buyer who overpays for your couch on Craigslist.
  • Crypto and "investment" scams - guaranteed high returns, secret tips, or pressure to transfer money quickly. A promise of risk-free profit is a major warning sign.

A verification routine

A few habits reduce the chance that these tactics will succeed:

  • Slow down. Treat pressure and urgency as reasons to pause, not as proof that a request is legitimate.
  • Verify through a channel you find independently. Do not use the link or phone number in the unexpected message. Open the official app, use a number printed on a card or statement, or type a known address.
  • Protect secrets. Do not give anyone a password or one-time verification code. Banks and support staff do not need those values from you.
  • Avoid the supplied link. If an account might require attention, open the service directly and check for a matching notice.

Software filters help, but a pause and an independent check remain useful when a fraudulent message gets through.

Try it: Apply the verification routine to a suspicious message in a spam folder without opening its links or attachments. Ask: Who appears to be the sender? What pressure does the message create? What action does it request? How could the story be checked through an independent channel?
