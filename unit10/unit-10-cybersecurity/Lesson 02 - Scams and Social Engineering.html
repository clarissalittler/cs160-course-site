Lesson 2 - Scams and Social Engineering

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what social engineering is and why most scams target the person, not the computer.
  • Name the emotional pressures scammers use to encourage action before reflection.
  • Recognize phishing in its different forms (email, text, phone, and targeted messages).
  • Apply a verification routine before clicking, calling, sharing information, or paying.

Social engineering targets people

Some attacks go after software. Others go after you, by talking you into switching off a safeguard yourself. Think about it from a criminal's point of view: why spend weeks trying to break a bank's security when you can just call someone, pretend to be the bank, and ask for the code it texted them?

That's called social engineering: manipulating someone into handing over money, information, or access. It runs on psychology and timing as much as on technology.

Knowing the tricks doesn't make anyone immune, especially when a message shows up at a stressful moment. (Scammers are very good at stressful moments. That's kind of the whole job.) What it does do is make it easier to stick to a routine of checking before you act.

The emotional levers

The stories change all the time, but the pressures underneath them are surprisingly few. Each one is designed to get you to act before you check the story.

  • Urgency. "Act now or your account closes in 24 hours." A short deadline is there to keep you from stopping to check.
  • Authority. The sender claims to be your bank, a government agency, the police, or your boss, so that going along with it feels like something you have to do.
  • Fear. "Fraud on your account!" or "a warrant in your name!" When you're scared, your attention goes to fixing the problem right now instead of asking whether the problem is real.
  • Opportunity. A prize, or a price that's way too good, can distract you from a sketchy website or a weird way of paying.
  • Sympathy or romance. Some scams spend weeks or months building trust before there's suddenly an emergency, and the emergency needs money.

So the pattern is always the same: a strong feeling, then pressure to act fast. Once you know that, the pressure itself becomes your cue. If a message is pushing you to hurry, that's exactly when to slow down and check.

Phishing: the workhorse of scams

The most common delivery method is phishing - a message that pretends to be from someone trustworthy to trick you into clicking a bad link, opening a bad attachment, or typing your password into a fake login page. (The name is a play on "fishing": they cast out lots of bait and see who bites.)

Phishing comes in a few flavors depending on how it reaches you:

  • Phishing - the classic, by email. The fake "your package couldn't be delivered" or "verify your account" message.
  • Smishing - the same trick by text message (SMS). Short, casual, and easy to tap without thinking.
  • Vishing - by voice: a phone call or voicemail, often a recorded "this is the IRS" or a fake fraud-department agent.
  • Spear phishing - a targeted message that uses real details, such as a name, employer, or recent transaction, to appear credible.

A quick field guide to common scams

The details keep changing, but you'll see these patterns over and over:

  • Fake bank / account alerts - "suspicious login detected, click here to secure your account."
  • Tech support - "your computer is infected! Call this number now" (often a pop-up that hijacks your screen).
  • Gift card demands - someone claiming official authority insists that a bill, fine, or emergency has to be paid with retail gift cards. Real government agencies and businesses don't make you pay a debt with gift cards. Ever.
  • Romance scams - an online relationship that gets serious fast, then needs money for a crisis.
  • Fake invoices / subscriptions - "your $499 renewal has been charged - call to dispute," to get you on the phone.
  • Job and check-overpayment scams - a too-easy work-from-home gig that "accidentally" overpays you and asks you to wire back the difference.
  • Marketplace and delivery-text scams - "your package is held, pay a small fee to release it," or a buyer who overpays for your couch on Craigslist.
  • Crypto and "investment" scams - guaranteed high returns, secret tips, or pressure to move money quickly. If someone promises a profit with no risk, that's a huge red flag.

A verification routine

A few habits make all of these tricks much less likely to work on you:

  • Slow down. Pressure and urgency are reasons to pause. They are never proof that a request is real.
  • Check through a channel you find yourself. Don't use the link or the phone number in the message you weren't expecting. Open the official app, call the number printed on your card or statement, or type in an address you already know.
  • Keep your secrets secret. Don't give anyone a password or a one-time verification code. Your bank and real support staff never need those from you.
  • Skip the supplied link. If you think an account might really need attention, go to the service directly and see if there's a matching notice there.

Spam filters catch plenty. (As we saw in Unit 6, they also miss some and wrongly flag others!) So when a fake message does get through, your pause and your independent check are what's left.

Try it: Use one fictional message from the Spot the Phish exercise, or privately look at a suspicious message you've received, without opening any links or attachments. Ask yourself: Who does it claim to be from? What pressure is it putting on you? What does it want you to do? How could you check the story through a channel you found yourself? Use invented details in anything you post for the course.
