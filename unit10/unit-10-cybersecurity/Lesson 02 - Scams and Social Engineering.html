Lesson 2 - Scams and Social Engineering

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what social engineering is and why most scams target the person, not the computer.
  • Name the emotional levers scammers pull to make you act before you think.
  • Recognize phishing in its different forms (email, text, phone, and targeted messages).
  • Apply a few golden rules to slow down and verify before you click, call, or pay.

The scammer's real tool isn't code - it's you

When you picture a "hacker," you probably imagine someone in a hoodie typing furiously, breaking through firewalls with pure technical wizardry. That happens, sure. But the truth is way less cinematic: most online attacks don't break into the computer at all. They talk their way past the human sitting in front of it.

That's called social engineering - manipulating a person into handing over money, passwords, or access. The scammer doesn't need to crack your bank's security if they can just convince you to read them the code your bank texted you. Their real toolkit is psychology, not programming.

Here's the good news: once you can see the psychological tricks, they stop working on you. You don't need to be a security expert. You just need to recognize the pattern.

The emotional levers

Scams almost always run on the same handful of emotions. The goal is to spike your feelings high enough that you act before your slower, more skeptical brain catches up.

  • Urgency. "Act now or your account closes in 24 hours." Real urgency is rare; manufactured urgency is the scammer's favorite trick - because if you have time to think, you'll notice something's off.
  • Authority. They pretend to be someone you're inclined to obey - your bank, the IRS, the police, your boss emailing from "a different account." We're trained to listen to authority, so a uniform (even a fake one) gets the benefit of the doubt.
  • Fear. "There's a problem with your account" or "we've detected illegal activity in your name." Scared people stop asking questions and start doing what they're told.
  • Greed or opportunity. "You've won!" or "this deal expires tonight." A $900 espresso machine for $90 is exciting enough that you might not notice the website was registered last Tuesday.
  • Sympathy or romance. A new online sweetheart who suddenly has a medical emergency, or a stranger with a heartbreaking sob story. These play the long game, building trust before the ask.

Different stories, same engine: feel something strong, do something fast. The moment a message is pushing you to hurry, that's your cue to slow down.

Phishing: the workhorse of scams

The most common delivery method is phishing - a message that pretends to be from someone trustworthy to trick you into clicking a bad link, opening a bad attachment, or typing your password into a fake login page. (The name is a play on "fishing": they cast out lots of bait and see who bites.)

Phishing comes in a few flavors depending on how it reaches you:

  • Phishing - the classic, by email. The fake "your package couldn't be delivered" or "verify your account" message.
  • Smishing - the same trick by text message (SMS). Short, casual, and easy to tap without thinking.
  • Vishing - by voice: a phone call or voicemail, often a recorded "this is the IRS" or a fake fraud-department agent.
  • Spear phishing - a targeted version that uses real details about you (your name, your employer, a recent purchase) to seem legit. These are the scary-convincing ones, and they're getting easier to make as more of our info ends up online.

A quick field guide to common scams

You don't need to memorize every variety, but it helps to recognize the usual suspects when one shows up:

  • Fake bank / account alerts - "suspicious login detected, click here to secure your account."
  • Tech support - "your computer is infected! Call this number now" (often a pop-up that hijacks your screen).
  • Gift card demands - someone "official" insists you pay a bill or fine with iTunes or Amazon gift cards. No real agency does this, ever.
  • Romance scams - an online relationship that gets serious fast, then needs money for a crisis.
  • Fake invoices / subscriptions - "your $499 renewal has been charged - call to dispute," to get you on the phone.
  • Job and check-overpayment scams - a too-easy work-from-home gig that "accidentally" overpays you and asks you to wire back the difference.
  • Marketplace and delivery-text scams - "your package is held, pay a small fee to release it," or a buyer who overpays for your couch on Craigslist.
  • Crypto and "investment" scams - guaranteed returns, a hot tip, a friend-of-a-friend who got rich. If it's guaranteed, it's a lie.

The golden rules

Here's the empowering part. You can shut down the overwhelming majority of these with a few habits:

  • Slow down. Urgency is the tell. Almost nothing legitimate falls apart if you take ten minutes to think.
  • Verify through a channel you find yourself. Don't trust the phone number or link in the message - it goes straight to the scammer. Hang up and call the real number on the back of your card or the company's official website.
  • Remember what legit organizations never do. They don't demand gift cards, ask for your password, or insist on instant payment to avoid arrest. Your bank already knows your account number.
  • When in doubt, don't click. Skip the link entirely and go to the site directly by typing the address yourself.

That's it. A healthy pause and a habit of verifying on your own terms will protect you from more scams than any piece of software.

Try it: The next slightly-fishy message you get (and you will - check your spam folder if you're impatient), run it through the golden rules instead of reacting. Ask yourself three questions: Who is this really from? What emotion is it trying to push - urgency, fear, greed? And what does it want me to do fast? Just naming the trick out loud is often enough to break the spell.
