Lesson 2 - The Price of Convenience

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain privacy concerns around facial recognition and location data
  • Describe the tradeoff between convenience/security and privacy
  • Form your own position on where to draw the line

"Free" Isn't Free

Most of the services you use online are technically free. You don't pay for Google. You don't pay for Instagram. You don't pay for TikTok. But the business model isn't charity — it's data. You pay with information about yourself.

And that's not necessarily a terrible deal! You get an incredibly powerful search engine, a way to stay connected with friends, an endless stream of entertainment — and all it costs is... well, a detailed profile of who you are, what you like, where you go, and what you do. Whether that's a fair trade depends on who you ask, and it depends on what's being done with that information. In this lesson, we're going to look at two specific technologies — facial recognition and location tracking — where the convenience-vs.-privacy tradeoff gets really interesting.

Facial Recognition

Facial recognition technology does what it sounds like: it identifies or verifies a person by analyzing the features of their face. If you unlock your phone with your face, you've used it. If you've been auto-tagged in a photo on social media, you've encountered it. The basic idea is that software maps the geometry of your face — the distance between your eyes, the shape of your jawline, &c. — and converts that into a mathematical representation it can compare against a database.

If you remember our discussion of supervised learning from Unit 9, this is a direct application of that idea: the system has been trained on enormous datasets of labeled faces, and it's learned to identify patterns that distinguish one face from another.

Is Facial Recognition Invading Your Privacy?

The benefits are real. Unlocking your phone with your face is fast and convenient. Law enforcement has used facial recognition to find missing children and identify suspects. Airports use it to speed up boarding. These are genuinely useful applications.

But the concerns are also real. Facial recognition enables mass surveillance — the ability to track where people go and what they do on a scale that was never possible before. Governments can (and do) use it to monitor protests and public gatherings. And as we discussed in Unit 9's lesson on bias in AI, facial recognition systems have been shown to be significantly less accurate for people with darker skin, which means the technology can reinforce existing inequalities in policing and security.

Unlike a password, you can't change your face. If a database of facial recognition data gets breached, that biometric information is compromised permanently.

Think about it: Do you think the convenience of unlocking your phone with your face is worth the privacy tradeoff? What about the use of facial recognition in airports, stores, or by police?

Location Data

Your phone knows where you are. Always. Right now, as you're reading this, your phone is tracking your location — and depending on your settings, it may be sharing that information with dozens of apps and services.

Some of this is useful. You need your phone to know where you are for maps and navigation to work. Your weather app needs your location. Ride-sharing apps need it. But the question of who else gets that information — and what they can do with it — is where things get complicated.

In 2018, the Supreme Court took up this exact question in Carpenter v. United States. The case involved police obtaining 127 days of a suspect's cell phone location data from his wireless carrier — without a warrant. The court ruled, in a 5-4 decision, that accessing this kind of historical location data constitutes a search under the Fourth Amendment, meaning law enforcement generally needs a warrant to get it.

Carpenter v. United States — Location Data and the Fourth Amendment

This was a landmark ruling, but it only addresses one piece of the puzzle. It says the government needs a warrant to get your location history from your phone company. It doesn't say anything about what private companies can do with the location data you've already agreed to share with them (often by tapping "Allow" on a permissions popup without thinking about it). Data brokers — companies whose entire business is buying and selling personal data — can and do purchase location data and resell it.

Try this right now: Check your phone's location settings. How many apps have access to your location? How many of them are set to "Always" rather than "While Using"? Were any of them surprising?

Where Do You Draw the Line?

Here's the honest truth: there's no universally right answer here. Convenience vs. privacy is a genuine tradeoff, and reasonable people disagree about where to draw the line.

Some people are perfectly comfortable with targeted advertising — they'd rather see ads for things they actually want than random noise. Some people are happy to share their location with apps if it means getting better recommendations for restaurants or faster commute estimates. Others find the whole thing deeply unsettling and take active steps to minimize their digital footprint.

What matters is that you're making that decision consciously. The problem isn't that a tradeoff exists — it's that most people don't realize they're making one. They tap "Accept All Cookies" because the popup is annoying. They grant location access because the app won't work otherwise. They agree to terms of service they've never read because they just want to use the thing.

This is a conversation our society is actively having, and your generation gets to decide how it goes. Laws like the European Union's GDPR and California's CCPA are early attempts to give individuals more control over their data. But the technology moves fast, and the law is still catching up. The decisions being made right now — by legislators, by companies, and by individual users — will shape what digital privacy looks like for decades to come.

So it's worth thinking about: what are you comfortable sharing, and what aren't you? And are the defaults on your devices and apps actually aligned with those preferences?
