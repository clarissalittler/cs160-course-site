Lesson 9 - When Things Go Wrong

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain malware, including ransomware, and identify habits that reduce the risk of infection
  • Explain how software updates and recoverable backups reduce different forms of risk
  • Describe what HTTPS, VPNs, and end-to-end encryption do and do not protect
  • Choose appropriate first-response steps for a compromised account, malware, fraud, or a data breach

Prevention sometimes fails. A message may be convincing, a service may be breached, or a previously unknown software flaw may be exploited. A prepared response can limit the damage.

This final lesson has two parts: habits that protect devices and data, followed by response checklists for several common incidents.

Part 1: Protecting devices and data

Malware, in plain terms

Malware is short for malicious software: software designed to damage a system, steal information, monitor activity, or provide unauthorized access. Types include viruses that attach to other files, spyware that monitors activity, and ransomware that encrypts or otherwise denies access to data while demanding payment. Some ransomware also steals data, and payment does not guarantee recovery.

Useful defenses are routine: install software from trusted sources, treat unexpected attachments cautiously, keep built-in security protections enabled, and remove applications that are no longer supported or needed.

Updates repair known flaws

Software updates often repair security flaws as well as bugs. Once a flaw is known publicly, an unpatched device may remain exposed to attacks that the updated version prevents.

Enable automatic updates for the operating system, browser, and applications when practical. Restart the device when an update requires it, and replace software that no longer receives security support.

Backups provide a recovery path

A recoverable backup can limit data loss after ransomware, theft, physical damage, hardware failure, or accidental deletion. Restoration may still require cleaning or rebuilding a compromised device, and a backup is useful only if it contains an intact earlier copy.

Keep multiple copies of important files and make sure at least one is separated from the device—such as a versioned cloud-backup service or an external drive disconnected after backup. Ordinary file synchronization is not always a backup: deletion, corruption, or ransomware may synchronize to the other copy. Test occasionally that important files can be restored.

Networks and encrypted connections

Public Wi-Fi can expose devices to a malicious hotspot, local-network attacks, and any traffic that is not otherwise encrypted. Modern websites and applications usually encrypt traffic in transit, which reduces—but does not eliminate—the risk.

HTTPS encrypts the connection between the browser and the site named in the address bar. It helps prevent someone on the network from reading or changing that traffic, but it does not prove that the site itself is honest—a phishing site can also use HTTPS. A VPN encrypts traffic between the device and the VPN provider, which can be useful on an untrusted network or for access to an organizational network. It shifts trust to the VPN provider and does not make a malicious site safe.

Applications such as Signal use end-to-end encryption, which protects message content so that the service carrying it does not have the keys needed to read it in transit. It does not protect a message after an unlocked endpoint displays it, and services may still possess some metadata. Encryption protects a particular channel; it does not solve every privacy or security problem.

Part 2: Incident-response checklists

When something appears wrong, identify what happened before choosing a response. The steps differ depending on whether information was entered, software was installed, an account was taken over, or money was transferred.

"I entered my password or verification code on a phishing page."

  • Open the real service independently and change the affected password immediately. Change it anywhere else it was reused.
  • Sign out other sessions, review recovery information and recent activity, and remove unfamiliar connected applications or settings.
  • Enable the strongest available multifactor authentication or passkey. If a one-time code was disclosed, do not approve any unexpected login.

"I clicked a suspicious link or think I installed malware."

  • If suspicious software ran or the device is behaving as though it is compromised, disconnect it from networks to limit further communication.
  • Update the device's trusted security software and run a full scan. Remove only what the trusted tool identifies; a suspicious web page alone is not evidence that an application was installed.
  • From a different, clean device, change credentials that may have been captured and review important accounts.
  • If the problem persists, seek qualified support or erase and rebuild the device before restoring verified data from a clean backup.

"My account got hacked."

  • Use the service's official account-recovery process.
  • Change the password, sign out other sessions, and check recovery addresses, forwarding rules, connected applications, and recent activity.
  • Enable strong multifactor authentication or a passkey.
  • Tell contacts if the compromised account may have sent them messages.

"I got scammed out of money."

  • Contact the bank, card issuer, gift-card company, payment application, wire service, or other payment provider immediately and ask what recovery options remain.
  • In the United States, report the incident at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If personal information was exposed, use IdentityTheft.gov for a tailored recovery plan.
  • Preserve screenshots, names, account details, amounts, dates, and transaction records.
  • Expect possible follow-up "recovery" scams from people who promise to retrieve money for an upfront fee.

"My info showed up in a data breach."

  • Read the breach notice to determine which information was involved. If a current password was exposed, change it and every place it was reused.
  • Review affected accounts, enable strong authentication, and follow the organization's recovery instructions using contact information you verify independently.
  • If a Social Security number or information useful for identity theft was exposed, visit IdentityTheft.gov/databreach and consider a free credit freeze with each nationwide credit bureau.

The common principle is to act promptly without applying the same remedy to every incident. Preserve evidence, use a clean and independently verified channel, secure the affected system or account, notify the relevant institution, and follow a recovery plan appropriate to the information involved.

Try it: Confirm that automatic operating-system and application updates are enabled on one device. Then verify one backup: locate its settings, check the most recent successful run, and make sure it supports recovery of an earlier copy rather than only synchronization. If possible, test restoring one non-sensitive file.

This completes the unit's practical framework: recognize manipulation, verify claims and requests independently, use distinct and phishing-resistant authentication where possible, limit unnecessary data collection, maintain updates and recoverable backups, and respond according to the incident. No practice removes all risk, but these habits reduce common risks and make recovery more manageable. The Week 10 assignment brings the unit's ideas together in a hands-on exercise.
