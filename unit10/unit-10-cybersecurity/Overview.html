Unit 10 Overview

This unit is a practical guide to protecting yourself online. And the first thing to know is that most real threats look nothing like movie hacking, with someone in a hoodie typing really fast in front of green text. They look like a text message about a package. They're scams designed to manipulate people, false information built (or boosted) to grab attention, and attempts to get into your accounts or collect your personal data.

Security technology matters, but a small set of habits gets you a long way. We'll practice spotting phishing and checking claims before you share them, then look at how encryption keeps your messages secret, and use that to protect your accounts, manage your privacy, handle harassment, and recover when something goes wrong anyway.

Learning objectives

At the end of this learning module, you will be able to:

  1. Recognize scams and social-engineering tactics, and spot phishing in its common forms.
  2. Evaluate online information for misinformation using a simple, repeatable verification routine.
  3. Explain how encryption, public keys, and digital signatures keep communication private and prove who sent it.
  4. Protect your accounts with strong unique passwords, a password manager, and suitable multifactor or passkey authentication.
  5. Manage your privacy and personal data, and protect yourself from online harassment.
  6. Respond effectively when something goes wrong - malware, a hacked account, or a scam.

Practice without sharing personal information

Every lesson gives you a fictional case or a practice interactive to work with. Those are complete alternatives to poking around in your own accounts or devices, so you never have to use your own. In anything you turn in, use made-up examples, and don't submit passwords, codes, account details, private messages, or your personal search results.

Looking at your own settings is always optional. If you're on a shared or managed device, stick with the supplied cases instead of changing someone else's settings. And if changing something could alert a person who's monitoring you, use a safer device and get support before you change anything. Lesson 9 links to guidance on that.

Lesson map

The ten lessons in Unit 10
LessonCentral ideaQuestions to carry forward
1. You’re the Target (and That’s OK)Many attacks start by reaching a huge number of people, not by singling out one person. Both the systems we use and the choices we make can help protect us.Why would an attacker send the same message to thousands of people? What protection might help if someone clicks?
2. Scams and Social EngineeringScammers can use urgency, fear, or trust to get someone to act before checking a request. That's social engineering.What does this message want me to do? How could I verify the request through a channel I found myself?
3. Spot the FakeA link, login page, QR code, or piece of media can have clues that something is wrong. But a convincing appearance doesn't prove it's real.Where does this link actually lead? Can I reach the organization through a known route?
4. Why You Can’t Trust Your FeedWhat appears in a feed is shaped by platforms, incentives, and what people share. Those choices can help misleading information travel.Who benefits from this spreading? What evidence supports the claim outside my feed?
5. Checking What’s TrueFact-checkers often leave an unfamiliar page to find out what other sources say. The SIFT moves give us a way to do that kind of checking ourselves.Who is behind the claim? Can I find independent reporting or the original context before I share it?
6. How Encryption WorksEncryption uses keys to protect information. Public-key encryption helps people exchange secrets, while digital signatures help verify a message's origin and integrity.Who holds each key? What does encryption protect, and what can a signature tell us?
7. Passwords, Passkeys, and Two-FactorUnique passwords, password managers, passkeys, and two-factor authentication give us ways to make accounts harder to break into.What happens if one credential is exposed? What other step protects the account?
8. Your Privacy and Your DataApps and services can collect a trail of data as we go about our day. That data can reveal more about us than any single detail suggests.Who collects or receives this information? What choices do I have about sharing it?
9. Harassment and Personal SafetyHarassment can take different forms, and the safest response depends on the person's situation. Support and careful planning can help.What support and evidence might help? Could a change alert the person causing harm?
10. When Things Go WrongUpdates and backups help us prepare for trouble. If an account is compromised or a scam succeeds, a response plan can help limit the damage.What needs attention first? Who can help, and what information should be preserved?
