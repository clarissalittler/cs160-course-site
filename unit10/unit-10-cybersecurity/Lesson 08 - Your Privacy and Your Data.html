Lesson 8 - Your Privacy and Your Data

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain personally identifiable information (PII) and identify less-obvious data produced through online activity
  • Describe why organizations collect data through advertising models, app permissions, and data brokers
  • Discuss privacy as a question of control and power rather than simply having "nothing to hide"
  • Use app permissions, account settings, browser controls, and posting choices to limit unnecessary disclosure

Every lesson up to now has been about someone attacking you: phishing, malware, scams, a hijacked account. This one is different, because nobody is attacking anybody. It's about the data that gets collected while you're just going about your day. Your phone, your apps, the websites you visit, and the connected gadgets in your house can all keep a running record of what you do.

I'm not trying to scare you here! The goal is to understand what's being collected well enough that you can make your own choices about convenience, what you share, and how much control you want.

Data produced through ordinary use

Some data is obviously sensitive. Personally identifiable information (PII) is information that identifies you, or can be tied back to you, like your name, home address, phone number, or Social Security number. The exact legal definition depends on where you are and which law you're asking, but the basic question is always the same: can this be connected to a specific person?

Other data seems much less personal, until you start putting it together:

  • Location observations collected by an operating system, carrier, or application
  • Searches, purchases, and items placed in a cart but not purchased
  • Communication metadata, including who contacted whom and when
  • Interaction data, such as how long a post, photograph, or product remained on screen

Any one of those, by itself, doesn't say much. But collect them over months, from lots of sources, and they can reveal a surprising amount: your habits, your health, your politics, your relationships, your daily routine. And think about who wrote that profile of you. It wasn't you! It was assembled from what you did, not from anything you chose to say about yourself.

Who collects it, and why

So why is all this collected? Many of the services you use for free make their money from advertising. They can use what you do, and what they know about you, to pick out audiences, and then sell advertisers access to those audiences. That doesn't always mean a company is literally selling a file with your name on it. But the business is still built on your attention and your data being worth money. (There's an old saying about this: if you're not paying for the product, you might be the product.)

That's why app permissions are worth a look. Why would a flashlight app need your contacts? It doesn't! When an app asks for data that has nothing to do with what it's for, that's your cue to say no, look into why it's asking, or find a different app.

Then there are data brokers: companies that collect, combine, analyze, and share or sell information from all over the place, like apps, store loyalty programs, public records, and other data brokers. Their products get used for advertising, identity checks, people-search websites, risk scores, and more. Here's the strange part: you can be in a data broker's records without ever having heard of the company.

Two places this gets vivid: your face and your location

Two kinds of data make the connection between your online life and your physical life really obvious.

The first is facial recognition. Think about what makes a face different from a password: you can change a password if it leaks, but you can't exactly change your face! And depending on the system, a camera can compare your face against a set of stored images without you doing anything at all. With a badge or a phone, you at least have to tap something.

The second is location data. Your cell network, your phone's operating system, and your apps can each collect location information in different ways. Over time, those records can show where you sleep, where you work, where you worship, and where you go to the doctor. In the United States, the Supreme Court decided in Carpenter v. United States (2018) that the government generally needs a warrant to get historical cell-site location records like the ones in that case.

The rules for both of these are still being worked out. And the big open questions aren't only about what the technology can do. They're about what companies and governments should be allowed to do with it.

But I have nothing to hide

"I have nothing to hide" is a really common reaction to all this, and it deserves a real answer. Privacy isn't only about hiding things you did wrong. It's about who gets to decide what's known about you, in which setting, and for what purpose.

Here are a few reasons that matters even if your life is entirely boring and law-abiding:

  • Data sticks around. Copies, archives, screenshots, and backups mean that something you share today can come back years from now.
  • Contexts collapse. Something perfectly fine in one setting - a joke among friends, a medical search, your address - can be damaging when it lands in front of a different audience.
  • Information drifts from its original purpose. Data collected for one reason (say, to suggest a route home) gets reused for another (building a profile, training a system, getting handed to a third party) without anyone asking you again.

Privacy isn't all or nothing, either. It's completely reasonable to trade some of your data for something convenient, like turn-by-turn directions. The point is to make that trade on purpose, instead of accepting every default without looking.

Practical controls

No setting will make the collection stop entirely. But a few controls can cut down on how much you give away without meaning to:

  • Review app permissions. Take away access to your location, microphone, contacts, or photos when it doesn't match what the app does. If you can, choose limited or one-time access.
  • Review your advertising and location settings in your phone or computer's operating system, your browser, and your major accounts. Turning off personalized ads won't stop all collection, but it does change some of the ways your data gets used.
  • Use your browser's privacy controls to limit tracking from one site to the next, including third-party cookies and other tracking tricks. How much protection you get depends on the browser and the mode you're using.
  • Skim the privacy information before you use a sensitive service. Look for what it collects, why, who it shares your data with, how long it keeps it, and what controls you get.
  • Think before you post. How could this get copied, combined with other information, or seen by a totally different audience later?
  • Think about other people, too. When you tag a friend, post a group photo, or upload your contacts, you're making a privacy choice for somebody else.

Try it: Take this fictional list of permission requests: a map app wants your location while you're using it; a flashlight app wants your contacts; a voice recorder wants your microphone. Which ones make sense for what the app does? Which seem unnecessary? What would you look into before deciding? Or privately check one app on your own device and explain the same reasoning using made-up details. You don't have to change any permissions.

Computing & society: who benefits, who's harmed, who decides?

Our three questions fit this lesson really well. Who benefits from collecting the data? It might be the service making money, the advertiser reaching an audience, and you, getting a handy feature. Who's harmed when the data leaks, gets used for something else, or leads to a wrong match or a bad decision about someone? Often it's a person who never knew the profile existed in the first place.

And who decides? The organizations holding your data can know far more about you than you know about what they collect, who they share it with, how long they keep it, or what they use it to decide. Your own settings only fix a small part of that imbalance. The rest gets shaped by product defaults, company policies, laws, regulators, and public debate, which is one more reason to understand how this stuff works.
