Lesson 8 - Harassment and Personal Safety

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Recognize the main forms of online harassment - doxxing, account takeover, stalkerware, cyberstalking, and impersonation
  • Take proactive steps to reduce how exposed you are online
  • Follow a clear, concrete plan if harassment is already happening to you
  • Know where to turn for help when there's a threat to your safety

Most of this unit is about scammers and systems - phishing emails, sketchy websites, data breaches at companies you've never even heard of. But there's another kind of online threat, and it's a more personal one: sometimes the threat is a person. An ex who won't let go. A troll who's decided you're their entertainment for the week. A stranger who took something the wrong way. A whole pile of strangers who showed up at once.

This happens to ordinary people - not just celebrities or politicians. And if it happens to you, the first thing to know is that it isn't your fault - and there are concrete steps that genuinely help. Let's walk through them.

What it can look like

Online harassment isn't one thing. Here are the forms you're most likely to hear about, in plain terms:

  • Doxxing - publishing someone's private information (home address, workplace, phone number) to expose, scare, or threaten them. Example: someone digs up where you live and posts it on a forum so others can show up or call your job.
  • Account takeover (or hijacking) - someone gets into your social media or email account and either locks you out or impersonates you. Example: a hacker changes your password and starts messaging your contacts as "you."
  • Stalkerware - apps secretly installed on a phone to monitor location, messages, and calls. These are often installed by an abusive partner who has physical access to the device. Example: an app hidden on your phone quietly reporting your whereabouts to someone else.
  • Cyberstalking and coordinated harassment - repeated, unwanted contact from one person, or a "pile-on" where a crowd targets someone all at once with insults, threats, or floods of messages.
  • Impersonation - fake accounts pretending to be you, used to embarrass you or trick the people who know you. Example: a profile with your name and photo posting things you'd never say.

Reduce your exposure before anything happens

You can't control whether someone decides to be awful. But you can shrink the amount of information that's just sitting out there for them to use. Think of this as locking your doors - not because you expect a break-in, but because it's cheap insurance.

  • Lock down your social privacy settings. Most platforms let you control who can see your posts and who can message or tag you. Set these to friends-only or as restrictive as you're comfortable with.
  • Limit what reveals your location and routine. Don't post your live location. Be careful with photos that show your home, your car, your gym, or a recognizable landmark near where you live. Many phones tuck location data (metadata) into photos automatically - it's worth turning that off.
  • Separate your public and private identities. If you post publicly for work, a club, or a hobby, keep that separate from the account where your family photos and personal details live.
  • Google yourself. Search your own name and see what a stranger can find. It's the fastest way to spot something you'd rather take down.
  • Minimize what data brokers list about you. Remember those people-search sites from the privacy lesson? Opting out of them removes a big shortcut someone could use to find your address or phone number.
  • Secure the account that controls everything: your email. Whoever controls your email can reset the password on almost every other account you own. Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) there first - this is exactly the kind of thing we talked about in the passwords lesson.

Try it: Give yourself a 5-minute self-audit. (1) Search your own name and see what comes up. (2) Open your main social account and look at it the way a stranger would - what can someone who isn't your friend actually see? (3) Fix one thing right now: tighten a privacy setting, or remove one public detail (a phone number, an address, a tagged location). One small fix today is better than a perfect plan you never get to.

If it's already happening

Maybe you're reading this because it's not hypothetical. Okay. Take a breath - this is manageable, and here's a clear order of operations:

  • Don't engage or retaliate. It's a completely natural urge to fire back, but responding usually feeds the situation and rarely helps. You don't owe a harasser a reply.
  • Document everything first. Take screenshots that show dates, usernames, and URLs before anything gets deleted. This record matters if you later report to a platform, your school, or the police.
  • Block and report through the platform. Every major service has a reporting tool for harassment and impersonation. Use it - and block the accounts involved.
  • Tighten your privacy and change your passwords. Lock down your settings and update passwords on your important accounts, turning on 2FA where you can.
  • Tell someone you trust. A friend, family member, or counselor. You don't have to carry this alone, and a second person can help you document and decide what to do next.
  • Escalate when there's a threat to your safety. If anyone is threatening you or the people you love, contact the platform's safety team, your school, or your employer, and don't hesitate to involve law enforcement. Crisis lines and anti-stalking resources exist specifically for this, and reaching out to one is a normal, smart move - not an overreaction.

One important special case: if you suspect stalkerware on your phone, don't just delete the app. Removing it can tip off whoever installed it, which can make a dangerous situation worse. Instead, get help first - a domestic-violence hotline can talk you through the safest way to handle it.

Be kind to yourself

It's worth saying again, plainly: being targeted is not the target's fault. Not because of what you posted, what you wore in a photo, or which setting you forgot to flip. The responsibility sits entirely with the person doing the harassing. And asking for help - from a friend, a platform, a hotline, the police - isn't weakness or drama. It's the strong, sensible move, the same as calling a plumber when a pipe bursts. You're allowed to take up that space.
