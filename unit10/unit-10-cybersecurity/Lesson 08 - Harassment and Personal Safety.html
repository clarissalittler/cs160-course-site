Lesson 8 - Harassment and Personal Safety

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Recognize forms of online harassment, including doxxing, account takeover, stalkerware, cyberstalking, and impersonation
  • Take proactive steps to limit unnecessary public exposure
  • Follow a safety-conscious response plan if harassment is occurring
  • Identify sources of help when there is a threat to personal safety

Most of this unit has concerned scammers and systems: phishing messages, fraudulent websites, and organizational data breaches. Some online threats are more personal. They may come from a former partner, an individual harasser, an impersonator, or a coordinated group.

Online harassment affects people who are not public figures. Being targeted is not the target's fault, and several practical steps can preserve evidence, secure accounts, and help the person reach support.

What it can look like

Online harassment takes several forms:

  • Doxxing - publishing someone's private information, such as a home address, workplace, or phone number, in order to expose, frighten, or threaten them.
  • Account takeover - gaining control of another person's social-media or email account, locking out its owner, or using it to impersonate them.
  • Stalkerware - software used to monitor a device's location, messages, calls, or other activity without the user's informed consent. An abusive person with physical or account access may install or enable it.
  • Cyberstalking and coordinated harassment - repeated unwanted contact from one person or a coordinated flood of insults, threats, and messages from many accounts.
  • Impersonation - using a false account or stolen account to appear to speak as another person.

Reduce your exposure before anything happens

No privacy setting can control another person's behavior. It can, however, reduce the amount of information readily available for misuse.

  • Review social privacy settings. Use available controls for who can see posts and who can message, mention, or tag the account. Choose settings appropriate to the account's purpose.
  • Limit disclosure of location and routine. Avoid public live-location posts and review photographs for addresses, license plates, workplaces, or recognizable landmarks. Check whether location metadata is included when photographs are shared outside platforms that remove it.
  • Separate your public and private identities. If you post publicly for work, a club, or a hobby, keep that separate from the account where your family photos and personal details live.
  • Search for your public information. A search for your name and common usernames can reveal what a stranger can find. Request removal where appropriate, but do not publish the findings as part of this exercise.
  • Review people-search sites. Many provide an opt-out process. Removal from one site does not erase the original public records or prevent the information from reappearing, but it can remove an easy lookup path.
  • Secure primary email. Because email often supports account recovery, protect it with a unique credential and the strongest multifactor or passkey option available.

Optional self-audit: Search your name, review a main social account as a stranger would see it, and change one unnecessary public detail or privacy setting. Do not post the search results or other personal information for the course. If changing a setting could alert someone who is monitoring you, use a safer device and seek support before making changes.

If it's already happening

If the situation is not hypothetical, prioritize immediate safety over completing the list in a fixed order. When possible, use a device and account that the harasser cannot access.

  • Avoid retaliation. A response may intensify the contact or provide more material to misuse. A target does not owe the harasser a reply.
  • Preserve evidence when it is safe. Record dates, usernames, URLs, messages, and relevant context before content disappears. Some applications notify users about screenshots, so another safe device may be preferable.
  • Use platform controls. After preserving evidence, report harassment or impersonation and block or restrict the involved accounts when doing so fits the safety plan.
  • Secure accounts from a safe device. Review active sessions and recovery information, change compromised or reused credentials, and enable strong authentication. Do not make changes on a device suspected of being monitored.
  • Tell a trusted person. A friend, family member, counselor, advocate, or campus support office can help document the situation and consider options.
  • Escalate a threat to safety. Contact appropriate platform, school, workplace, victim-support, or local emergency resources. If danger is immediate, contact emergency services in your location.

One important special case: if you suspect stalkerware, do not immediately delete an application or reset the phone. A change can alert the person monitoring the device and increase danger. From a safer device, consult the FTC's stalkerware guidance or a domestic-violence advocate, such as the National Domestic Violence Hotline, before deciding what to do.

Responsibility and support

Being targeted is not the target's fault—not because of a post, photograph, or setting that was overlooked. Responsibility belongs to the person doing the harassing. Asking for help from a trusted person, advocate, platform, school, workplace, or appropriate public agency is a reasonable safety measure.
