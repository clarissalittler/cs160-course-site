Lesson 1 - Your Data, Their Business

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what personally identifiable information (PII) is and why it matters
  • Describe how apps and websites collect and use your data
  • Find and interpret a company's privacy policy

The Deal You're Already Making

Every time you use an app, visit a website, or tap "I agree" on a terms of service you didn't read (don't worry, nobody reads them), you're making a deal. You get a service — often for free. They get your data. Let's talk about what that deal actually looks like.

This is the fundamental bargain of the modern internet, and most of us make it dozens of times a day without thinking about it. You open Instagram, you scroll through TikTok, you search for something on Google — and in exchange for that free service, the company learns something about you. Sometimes a lot of somethings. The question isn't whether this is happening (it is), but whether you understand what you're giving up and whether you're okay with it.

What Is PII?

Personally Identifiable Information — PII for short — is any data that can be used to identify, locate, or contact a specific person. And it's way more than just your Social Security number.

PII includes the obvious stuff: your name, your address, your phone number, your date of birth. But it also includes things you might not think of as "personal information" — your browsing history, your purchase history, your location data, your biometric data (like your fingerprint or face scan), your IP address, &c. Some of this you hand over knowingly, like when you fill out a form to create an account. But a surprising amount is collected without you actively choosing to share it.

Here's the thing that makes PII tricky: individual pieces of it might seem harmless on their own. So a company knows your zip code — big deal, right? But combine your zip code with your date of birth and your gender, and research has shown that's often enough to uniquely identify you. Data gets powerful — and potentially dangerous — when it's combined.

How Data Gets Collected

So how do companies actually gather all this information about you? There are a few major mechanisms, and they're worth knowing about.

Cookies are small text files that websites store on your computer. Some cookies are genuinely useful — they're why you don't have to log into a website every single time you visit it. But tracking cookies follow you across different websites to build a profile of your browsing habits. When you search for rain boots and then every website you visit starts showing you ads for rain boots — that's cookies and tracking at work.

Location services let apps know where you physically are. Your weather app needs this to show you the right forecast. Your maps app needs this to give you directions. But your flashlight app? Probably doesn't need to know where you are — and yet many apps request location access anyway, because location data is valuable.

Browsing history is a record of every website you've visited. Search engines keep track of what you've searched for. This data gets used for targeted advertising, but it also builds a surprisingly detailed picture of who you are — your interests, your health concerns, your political leanings, what you're shopping for, what you're worried about.

Purchase tracking records what you buy, when you buy it, and how much you spend. If you've ever used a store loyalty card or rewards program, you've traded your purchase data for discounts. Retailers use this to predict what you'll want to buy next — and they're eerily good at it.

None of these mechanisms are inherently evil. But taken together, they create a remarkably complete picture of your daily life. And once that data exists, it can be sold, shared, leaked, or stolen.

Privacy Policies: The Deal in Writing

Many companies that collect your data publish a privacy policy — and in some places they are legally required to. A privacy policy explains what data they collect, how they use it, who they share it with, and what choices you have about it. In theory, this is your chance to understand the deal before you agree to it.

Privacy Policies: What Are We Really Agreeing To?

In practice? Nobody reads these, and companies know that. One study estimated that it would take the average person about 76 work days per year to actually read all the privacy policies they encounter. They're long, they're dense, and they're written in legal language that's designed to protect the company, not to inform you.

But understanding the basics is worth your time. You don't need to read every word — but you should know how to skim one and spot the important parts. Here's what to look for:

  • What data do they collect? Look for sections about "information we collect" or "data we gather." You'll often find it's a lot more than you expected.
  • How do they use it? Companies typically say things like "to improve our services" or "to personalize your experience" — which often means "to show you targeted ads."
  • Who do they share it with? Look for phrases like "third-party partners" or "service providers." This is where your data can end up in places you didn't anticipate.
  • What choices do you have? Some companies let you opt out of certain data collection. Many make it harder to opt out than to opt in.

The goal here isn't to make you paranoid — it's to make you informed. You might decide that the tradeoff is worth it for services you love. That's a perfectly reasonable choice. But it should be a choice you're making consciously, not one that's being made for you while you're not paying attention.
