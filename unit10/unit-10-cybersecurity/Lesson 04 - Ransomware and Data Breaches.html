Lesson 4 - Ransomware and Data Breaches

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how ransomware works and why it's become so common.
  • Describe what happens in a data breach and how it affects individuals.
  • Identify steps to take if your data is compromised.

The Threat That Makes the News

Of all the cybersecurity threats we'll talk about, ransomware is probably the one most likely to make the evening news. It's also the one that's grown the fastest in the last decade. What started as a niche annoyance has become a billion-dollar criminal industry—and the targets aren't just big corporations. Schools, hospitals, small businesses, and city governments have all been hit.

How Ransomware Works

The concept is brutally simple. Ransomware is malware—malicious software—that encrypts your files and demands payment to unlock them. The attacker holds your data hostage.

It usually starts with something mundane: a phishing email with a convincing-looking attachment, a link to a compromised website, or a vulnerability in outdated software. Someone clicks the wrong thing, and the malware quietly installs itself. Then it gets to work—encrypting every file it can reach. Documents, spreadsheets, databases, photos, backups if they're connected to the network. All of it, locked.

Imagine arriving at work one morning and every file on every computer in the building is locked. There's a message on every screen: "Pay $500,000 in Bitcoin within 72 hours or your files are gone forever." That's ransomware.

The attackers almost always demand payment in cryptocurrency—Bitcoin, usually—because it's harder (though not impossible) to trace. And the ransom amounts have skyrocketed over the years. Early ransomware might ask for a few hundred dollars. Modern attacks against organizations routinely demand millions.

Real-World Examples

This isn't hypothetical. Here are a few incidents that made headlines:

  • Colonial Pipeline (2021) — A ransomware attack shut down the largest fuel pipeline on the US East Coast for nearly a week. Gas stations ran dry across multiple states. The company paid roughly $4.4 million in ransom (the FBI later recovered most of it, but that's unusual).
  • Hospitals during COVID — Multiple hospitals were hit by ransomware during the pandemic, when they were already overwhelmed. In some cases, patient care was disrupted—surgeries delayed, ambulances rerouted. Attacking healthcare during a global health crisis tells you everything you need to know about the people behind these operations.
  • Schools and universities — Community colleges and K-12 school districts have been targeted repeatedly. They're attractive targets because they often have limited IT budgets but hold lots of sensitive student data.

The FBI recommends not paying ransoms. Paying funds more attacks, there's no guarantee you'll actually get your files back, and it marks you as someone willing to pay—making you a target for future attacks. That said, when a hospital can't access patient records or a business is bleeding money every hour, the pressure to pay is enormous. It's an ugly situation with no clean answers.

Data Breaches

Data breaches are a related but distinct problem. A breach happens when an attacker gains unauthorized access to a company's systems and steals user data. Sometimes breaches and ransomware happen together—attackers steal the data and encrypt it, so they can threaten to publish it if you don't pay—but a breach can happen without ransomware, and vice versa.

The scale of some breaches is staggering:

  • Equifax (2017) — Attackers stole Social Security numbers, birth dates, and addresses for 147 million people. Equifax is one of the three major credit bureaus, so this was deeply personal financial data—the kind of information that makes identity theft straightforward.
  • Yahoo (2013-2014, disclosed later) — All 3 billion user accounts were compromised. Every single one.
  • Countless smaller breaches — Retailers, restaurants, healthcare providers, fitness apps. They happen so frequently that most don't even make the news anymore.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: if you've been alive and had a credit history in the United States, your data has probably been part of at least one breach. Possibly several.

So what do attackers do with stolen data? A few things:

  • Identity theft — Opening credit cards, filing tax returns, or taking out loans in your name.
  • Credential stuffing — If they get your email and password from one breach, they'll try that same combination on dozens of other sites. (This is why reusing passwords is so dangerous—one breach can compromise all your accounts.)
  • Selling it — Stolen data is bought and sold on dark web marketplaces. A Social Security number might sell for a few dollars. A complete identity package—name, SSN, address, date of birth, mother's maiden name—goes for more.

What to Do if Your Data Is Breached

If you get a notification that your data was part of a breach, take it seriously. Don't ignore it. Don't assume it won't affect you. The steps below take a little time, but they can save you from much bigger headaches down the road.

Here's a practical checklist:

  1. Check haveibeenpwned.com — This free site (run by a respected security researcher) lets you enter your email address and see which known breaches included your data. It's sobering but useful.
  2. Change your passwords — Start with the breached service, then change any other account where you used the same password. (And stop reusing passwords—a password manager helps enormously here.)
  3. Enable two-factor authentication — If the breached service offers it, turn it on. Turn it on everywhere you can, actually. We covered this in an earlier lesson, and this is exactly why it matters.
  4. Freeze your credit — If sensitive data like your SSN was exposed, you can place a free credit freeze with each of the three major credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). This prevents anyone from opening new credit in your name. You can temporarily lift the freeze when you need to apply for credit yourself.
  5. Monitor your statements — Keep an eye on bank and credit card statements for charges you don't recognize. Set up alerts if your bank offers them.

None of this is glamorous, and it's frustrating that the burden falls on individuals when it's companies that failed to protect the data. But until the world gets better at preventing breaches, these are the best tools we have.

Try it yourself: Go to haveibeenpwned.com and enter your email address. Were you in any breaches? (Don't share the results—this is just for your own awareness. But if you find something, now you know what steps to take.)
