Lesson 5 - Checking What's True

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use a simple, repeatable routine (the SIFT moves) to decide whether to trust something online.
  • Investigate a source by "reading laterally" instead of staying on the page that's trying to convince you.
  • Check a suspicious image or claim using its source, context, and reverse image search rather than relying on visual "AI tells."

Last lesson explained why a feed may amplify false or misleading material. This lesson introduces a brief routine for deciding whether a claim deserves trust or further investigation. Professional fact-checkers often evaluate unfamiliar sources by leaving the original page and consulting independent evidence.

The four SIFT moves

Media-literacy researcher Mike Caulfield organizes this approach into four moves called SIFT. Not every claim requires all four, but stopping comes first:

  • Stop. Pause before reacting or sharing. Notice whether the post is producing a strong emotion or whether you already know enough about its source.
  • Investigate the source. Determine who is making the claim, what expertise or record the source has, and what its purpose may be.
  • Find better coverage. Open a new tab and search for reporting or analysis from sources equipped to evaluate the claim. Independent corroboration is stronger than repeated copies of one account.
  • Trace claims, quotations, and media to the original context. Find the study, speech, dataset, photograph, or video from which the item came and check what it actually shows.

When the claim is a chart or statistic, use the seven questions from Unit 8, Lesson 2. Ask where the data came from, what was omitted, whether the scale is appropriate, and whether the visual supports its stated conclusion. A precise-looking number still requires context.

Read laterally, not down

When people reach an unfamiliar website, they often read down the page and judge its logo or "About Us" statement. Those elements are controlled by the site itself. Lateral reading means leaving the page, opening new tabs, and checking what independent sources say about the organization, author, and claim. This outside context is usually more informative than the site's self-description.

Checking images and AI fakes

An image may be fabricated, but it may also be authentic and presented with a false date, location, or caption. Two checks are especially useful:

  • Reverse image search. Search the image using a browser or phone tool. Earlier appearances may reveal that a supposed breaking-news photograph is old, from a different location, or already investigated.
  • Check provenance and context. Look for the earliest available source, original caption, photographer or creator, and corroborating coverage. Visual artifacts such as distorted text or anatomy can prompt investigation, but generators improve quickly and authentic images can also contain odd details. Appearance alone is not a reliable detector.
