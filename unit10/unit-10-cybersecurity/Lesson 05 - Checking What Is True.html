Lesson 5 - Checking What's True

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use a simple, repeatable routine (the SIFT moves) to decide whether to trust something online.
  • Investigate a source by "reading laterally" instead of staying on the page that's trying to convince you.
  • Check a suspicious image or claim with reverse image search, and recognize signs of AI-generated fakes.

Last lesson explained why your feed is so full of nonsense. This one hands you the toolkit to deal with it - and the encouraging news is that you don't need to become a full-time fact-checker. You need a quick routine you can run in thirty seconds before you believe or reshare something. Professional fact-checkers don't read harder than you; they read differently, and you can copy their moves.

The four SIFT moves

A media-literacy researcher named Mike Caulfield boiled it down to four moves with the handy name SIFT. You don't do all four every time - often the first one is enough:

  • Stop. Before you react or share, just pause. Notice if the post is spiking your emotions - that feeling is exactly the moment to slow down, because outrage is what fake stuff is engineered to trigger.
  • Investigate the source. Who is telling you this, and why? A random account named "TruthBomb2024" is not the same as an established newsroom. Take five seconds to find out what the source actually is.
  • Find better coverage. Don't get stuck on one post. Open a new tab and search the claim itself. If something big really happened, multiple independent, trustworthy outlets will be covering it. If only one strange site has the story, that is the story.
  • Trace claims, quotes, and images to the original. Headlines distort, images get reused, quotes get faked. Follow it back to where it actually came from before you trust it.

Read laterally, not down

Here's the key trick inside "investigate the source." When most people land on an unfamiliar website, they read down the page - they study its slick logo and its "About Us" - to judge whether it's trustworthy. But a scam site can write itself a glowing About page. Fact-checkers instead read laterally: they leave the page almost immediately, open new tabs, and ask the rest of the web "hey, what is this site, and who's behind it?" You judge a source by what others say about it, not by what it says about itself.

Checking images and AI fakes

Images are the great manipulators - a real photo from the wrong time or place, or an outright fake, can do more than a thousand words of lies. Two cheap checks:

  • Reverse image search. Right-click an image (or use your phone's image-search tool) and search for it. You'll often discover the "breaking news" photo is actually ten years old, from a different country, or already debunked.
  • Look for AI tells. As we saw in Unit 9, anyone can now generate a convincing image or video for free. Look closely at hands and fingers, jumbled text on signs, warped backgrounds, jewelry or teeth that don't quite make sense, and an overall too-perfect, plasticky sheen. Be most suspicious of an image that is perfectly designed to make you furious at someone.
