Lesson 5 - Checking What's True

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use a simple, repeatable routine (the SIFT moves) to decide whether to trust something online.
  • Investigate a source by "reading laterally" instead of staying on the page that's trying to convince you.
  • Check a suspicious image or claim using its source, context, and reverse image search rather than relying on visual "AI tells."

Last lesson was about why your feed can end up amplifying false or misleading stuff. This lesson is the practical part: a quick routine for deciding whether a claim deserves your trust, or needs more checking first.

There's a fun fact about how professional fact-checkers work. When they hit an unfamiliar source, they mostly don't study the page carefully. They leave it, and go see what everyone else says about it. We'll see why in a minute.

The four SIFT moves

Media-literacy researcher Mike Caulfield boiled this down to four moves, called SIFT. You won't need all four for every claim, but the first one always comes first:

  • Stop. Pause before you react or share. Is this post making you feel something strongly? Do you actually know anything about where it came from?
  • Investigate the source. Who's making this claim? What do they know about it, what's their track record, and what might they want?
  • Find better coverage. Open a new tab and look for reporting or analysis from people who are in a good position to check the claim. Several independent sources agreeing is much stronger than a hundred copies of one post.
  • Trace claims, quotations, and media to the original context. Go find the actual study, speech, dataset, photo, or video it came from, and check what it really shows.

If the claim is a chart or a statistic, bring out the seven questions from Unit 8, Lesson 2. Where did the data come from? What got left out? Is the scale reasonable? Does the picture actually support the conclusion it's claiming? A very precise-looking number can still be very misleading without context.

Lateral reading

When most of us land on an unfamiliar website, we read down the page. We look at the logo, the design, the "About Us" section, and decide whether it seems legit. The problem is that the site controls every one of those things! Of course its "About Us" page makes it sound great.

Lateral reading means doing the opposite: leave the page, open some new tabs, and see what independent sources say about the organization, the author, and the claim. That outside view almost always tells you more than the site's description of itself. (This is the fact-checker trick from the start of the lesson. I love it because it's so simple, and it works on websites that look extremely professional.)

Checking images and AI fakes

A suspicious image might be completely fake. But just as often, it's a real photo with a false date, location, or caption attached. (A real photo of a flood from 2015, labeled as "this morning," is still misinformation!) Two checks are especially useful:

  • Reverse image search. Search using the image itself, with a browser or phone tool. If it turns up in older places, you might find out that the "breaking news" photo is years old, from somewhere else entirely, or already debunked.
  • Check where it came from. Look for the earliest version you can find, the original caption, who took or made it, and whether anyone else reported the same thing. Weird details like garbled text or an extra finger can be a reason to look closer, but image generators get better fast, and real photos have odd details too. You can't reliably tell a fake just by looking at it.
