Lesson 6 - Passwords, Passkeys, and Two-Factor

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how password reuse enables credential-stuffing attacks
  • Describe how uniqueness, length, and unpredictability contribute to password strength
  • Explain how a password manager generates and stores distinct passwords
  • Compare common forms of multifactor authentication with phishing-resistant passkeys

So far, this unit has concentrated on phishing, scams, and false information. We now turn to the authentication methods that protect accounts: passwords, password managers, multifactor authentication, and passkeys.

Email, financial services, photographs, health portals, shopping accounts, and many other services depend on authentication. A weakness in one account can sometimes affect several others, especially when credentials are reused.

Password reuse multiplies a breach

Short and predictable passwords such as password123, a pet's name, or a birthday are easy to guess. Reusing any password creates a second problem: one compromised service can expose other accounts.

Suppose an old forum is breached and an email address and password become available to attackers. Even if that forum was unimportant, the same credentials may unlock a more valuable account.

Attackers can test a leaked email-and-password pair automatically against many other services. This is called credential stuffing. It succeeds when the same credentials were used on more than one site.

The practical rule is therefore: every account gets a distinct password or passkey. A breach at one service should not provide credentials for another.

What actually makes a password good

Password strength depends on length and unpredictability, not merely on satisfying a pattern such as one capital letter, one number, and one symbol. Predictable substitutions do little to help a short password. A password manager can generate a long random value without requiring anyone to memorize it.

When a password must be memorized, a passphrase made from several randomly selected words can provide length in a memorable form. The words must be unpredictable, and the phrase must not be reused. A published example should never be copied as an actual password.

Memorizing a different strong password for every service is unrealistic. A password manager handles that task.

Password managers

A password manager can generate a long, random, unique password for each service, store the credentials in an encrypted vault, and fill them into the matching site or application. The user protects the manager with a strong account password, device authentication, or both.

This makes distinct passwords practical and limits the credential-stuffing risk from a breached site. Password managers are available as platform, browser, and standalone products; whichever type is chosen should come from a reputable provider and be kept updated.

A password manager concentrates sensitive information, so its security matters. Reputable products encrypt the vault and are designed so that stored credentials are not available as ordinary readable text to the provider. Protect the manager itself with a strong, unique account password and the best multifactor option it supports, and keep its recovery information current. For most people, this managed vault is a safer and more practical approach than reusing memorized passwords across many sites.

Two-factor authentication: a second lock

Two-factor authentication, or 2FA, combines two different kinds of evidence—for example, something you know, such as a password, with something you possess, such as an enrolled device or security key. MFA is the broader term for multifactor authentication. A stolen password alone is then insufficient for login.

The second factor might be a one-time code, an approval tied to a specific login, a hardware security key, or device-based biometric confirmation.

The methods are not equally resistant to attack. Text-message codes can be redirected through phone-account attacks, and manually entered codes from either text messages or authenticator apps can be relayed by a phishing site. Unsolicited push approvals can also be abused, so never approve a login you did not initiate. A passkey or hardware security key tied to the real domain provides stronger phishing resistance. If a code is the only second factor offered, it still adds protection beyond a password alone.

Passkeys: the version with no password at all

A passkey replaces a shared password with a cryptographic key pair. The device proves possession of the private key, usually after local confirmation with a fingerprint, face scan, or device PIN.

The private key is not sent to the service, and the credential is bound to the site's domain. A correctly implemented passkey is therefore resistant to credential phishing and does not expose a reusable password if the service's credential database is breached. Account recovery and the security of devices or synchronization accounts still matter. When a trusted service offers a passkey, it is generally a strong option.

Data breaches happen - here's how to cope

Organizations sometimes experience data breaches, and an individual cannot prevent a service from being compromised. The response depends on what information the notice says was exposed.

Have I Been Pwned can report whether an email address appears in its database of known breaches and what categories of data those incidents exposed. An email search does not reveal a particular password. Follow the affected service's notice, change any current or reused credential associated with the incident, review account activity, and enable stronger authentication.

Try it: Review the security settings for your primary email account, which is often used to recover other accounts. Confirm that the recovery address and phone number are current. Then enable the strongest multifactor or passkey option available to you and review the password manager provided by your device, browser, or a reputable standalone service.
