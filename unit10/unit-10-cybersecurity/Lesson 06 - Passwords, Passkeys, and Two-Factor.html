Lesson 6 - Passwords, Passkeys, and Two-Factor

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why reusing the same password across sites is the single most dangerous habit, and what "credential stuffing" is
  • Describe what actually makes a password strong (hint: length, not weird symbols)
  • Explain what a password manager does and why it's the highest-impact security habit you can adopt
  • Describe two-factor authentication and passkeys, and turn on protections for your most important accounts

So far this unit has been about the bad guys - phishing, scams, the people trying to get in. The rest of it is about you, and the surprisingly small set of habits that lock the door. We'll start with the most important one: your passwords.

Here's the thing. Your passwords are the keys to your entire life now. Your email, your bank, your photos, your medical records, the place you order tacos - all of it sits behind a password. And most people guard those keys terribly.

The real problem isn't weak passwords - it's reused ones

We all know we're "supposed" to use strong passwords, and lots of people don't - password123, your dog's name, your birthday. That's bad. But it's not the thing that actually gets people hacked.

The thing that gets people hacked is using the same password everywhere.

Picture it: you use FluffyTheCat99 on some random forum you signed up for in 2018. That forum gets breached - and big sites get breached constantly, more on that in a minute - and now your email address and FluffyTheCat99 are sitting in a giant list that attackers buy and trade.

Here's what they do with it. They take your email + FluffyTheCat99 and try it on Gmail. Then your bank. Then Amazon. Then your work login. Automatically, by the millions, across every site they can think of. This is called credential stuffing, and it works depressingly often - because so many people use one password for everything. One leak from a site you forgot about can unlock your whole life.

So the rule that matters most: every account gets its own password. A breach should cost you one account, not all of them.

What actually makes a password good

Forget most of what those annoying "must contain a capital letter, a number, and a hieroglyph" rules taught you. The single biggest factor in a password's strength is length. A long password is hard to crack; a short one is easy, no matter how many $ and ! symbols you cram in.

The friendly version of this is a passphrase - four or five random words strung together, like correct-otter-battery-stapler. It's long, it's genuinely hard to guess or crack, and you can actually remember it. Long beats complicated, every time.

But - and this is the catch - you can't remember a hundred strong, unique passphrases. And you shouldn't try. That's a job for a piece of software, not your brain.

Password managers: the one habit that changes everything

A password manager is an app that generates a long, random, unique password for every single site you use, stores them all safely, and fills them in for you. You remember exactly one password - the master password that unlocks the manager - and it remembers the other two hundred.

This quietly solves the whole problem. Every account gets a different strong password without you lifting a finger or memorizing anything. If a site gets breached, the damage stops at that one account. There are good ones built right into your phone and browser, and good standalone ones too.

Now, the worry that always comes up: "isn't putting all my passwords in one app a single point of failure? If someone gets into that, they get everything." It's a fair question, and the honest answer is that the trade-off is hugely in your favor. Reputable password managers encrypt your data so thoroughly that even the company running it can't read your passwords - only your master password unlocks them. Compare the real risk: with a manager, you have one well-defended vault. Without one, you have the same weak password scattered across fifty sites, any one of which could leak it tomorrow. The vault is far safer than the scattering.

Two-factor authentication: a second lock

Two-factor authentication (you'll see it as 2FA, or MFA for "multi-factor") adds a second step beyond your password. The idea is to combine something you know (your password) with something you have (your phone). So even if someone steals your password, they're stuck at the door - they don't have your phone to finish logging in.

In practice it looks like this: you type your password, and then the site asks for a second thing - a six-digit code, or a tap on your phone to approve the login.

One nuance worth knowing: getting that code from an authenticator app (or a tap-to-approve prompt) is stronger than getting it by text message. Text-message codes can be intercepted or redirected by a determined attacker, so app-based codes are the better choice when a site offers them. That said - any two-factor is dramatically better than none. If text message is all a site supports, turn it on anyway.

Passkeys: the version with no password at all

The newest evolution is the passkey, and it's lovely because it gets rid of the password entirely. Instead of typing anything, your device proves it's really you - usually with your fingerprint, your face, or your device PIN - and logs you in.

Because there's no password being sent anywhere, there's nothing to phish, nothing to guess, and nothing to steal in a breach. Big sites are increasingly offering passkeys, and they're genuinely easier to use than passwords once they're set up. When a site offers one, take it.

Data breaches happen - here's how to cope

I keep mentioning breaches because they're just a fact of online life now. Major companies get breached on a regular basis, and when they do, your info can end up in big public dumps that float around the internet. This isn't your fault and it isn't something you can prevent - but you can respond to it.

You can actually check whether your email has shown up in known breaches at a free site called Have I Been Pwned (haveibeenpwned.com). Type in your email and it'll tell you which breaches it appeared in. If a password of yours has been exposed, the fix is simple: change it on that site, and - you guessed it - never reuse it anywhere else. (A password manager makes this painless, because each one is already unique.)

Try it: Pick your single most important account - your email, because it's the master key to everything else (every "forgot my password?" reset gets sent there). Go into its security settings and turn on two-factor authentication today. While you're at it, take a look at installing a password manager - even just the one built into your phone or browser is a huge step up.
