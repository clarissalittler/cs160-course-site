Unit 2 Summary

Wrap up

Step back and here's the shape of the whole unit. The internet is a distributed network of networks that nobody owns - built with no center on purpose. Splitting messages into packets and building in redundant paths lets it route around damage and keep working when pieces fail; that same distribution is a big part of why it's so hard to fully censor.

But over time we've funneled enormous chunks of it through a few clouds, content delivery networks, and platforms - because that's cheaper, faster, and easier - and in doing so we've quietly rebuilt the very single points of failure the design was meant to avoid. So the modern internet is a paradox: engineered to be resilient, yet increasingly brittle at the center, with real consequences for who gets access and who holds power.

Summary

  • The internet is a distributed "network of networks" - no single owner. It works because everyone follows the same open protocols.
  • Messages are split into packets that can take different paths and are reassembled at the destination.
  • Redundancy - many possible paths - lets traffic reroute around a failed cable, router, or node, so the network survives damage (and resists censorship).
  • Every device has an IP address; DNS is the "phone book" that turns names like pcc.edu into those addresses.
  • Protocols are layered, each doing one job - which let the internet grow and change without being rebuilt. The web (HTTP) is just one protocol at the top; alternatives like Gopher and Gemini exist.
  • The internet's foundation is nicknamed TCP/IP: IP gives every device an address and routes packets, while TCP splits a message into packets and makes sure they all arrive, in order.
  • We've re-centralized much of the internet onto a few clouds, CDNs, and platforms: convenient and usually reliable, but now a single company's glitch can take down large parts of the web (Google 2020, Fastly 2021, Cloudflare).
  • The social stakes: the digital divide decides who's included, and control over the pipes - ISPs and net neutrality, infrastructure companies, tracking, and censorship - decides who holds power.
