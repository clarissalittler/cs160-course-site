Lesson 1 - What the Internet Is (and Isn't)

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain, at a high level, what the internet is - a distributed "network of networks."
  • Describe the central tension this unit is about: distribution versus centralization.

The first surprise: nobody owns it

When you type a web address and a page appears a half-second later, it's easy to picture "the internet" as one giant machine humming away somewhere. It isn't. Here's the first surprise: the internet isn't one thing, and nobody owns it. It's a network of networks - your phone carrier's network, your college's network, Netflix's network, a hospital's network, and hundreds of thousands of others - all agreeing to pass each other's messages along using the same shared rules. No single company or government runs the whole thing. It works because everyone plays by the same protocols (agreed-upon rules), which is genuinely remarkable when you stop to think about it.

So what actually happens when you type in a web address? Roughly: your message hops from your device to your router, out across a chain of networks that hand it off to one another, until it reaches a server - some computer, maybe on the other side of the planet, that has the page you asked for. Then the answer makes the whole trip back to you. All of it in the blink of an eye.

Distributed on purpose

Here's the idea that runs through this whole unit, so it's worth planting a flag on it now. The internet was designed to be distributed - spread out, with no center, no single headquarters that everything flows through. That wasn't an accident or a happy side effect. It was the whole point. A network with no center is one that's very hard to break: knock out any one piece and the messages just flow around the gap. (You'll see exactly how next lesson.) The people who built it wanted a network that could survive damage, and "no center to attack" is how you get that.

The tension to watch for. But here's the twist we'll keep circling back to. Even though the internet is designed to have no center, over the last couple of decades we've quietly funneled enormous chunks of it through a small handful of giant companies - a few cloud providers, a few "content delivery" networks, a few platforms. Why? Because centralizing is cheaper, faster, and more convenient. The catch is that every time we do it, we hand back a little of the resilience that being distributed gave us in the first place. Distributed versus centralized - efficient but fragile versus messy but tough - is the push and pull at the heart of this unit.

Where we're headed

With that tension in mind, here's the arc of the unit:

  • First, how the distributed design actually works - packets, redundancy, addresses, and the layered agreements that hold it all together (Lessons 2–4).
  • Then, the pull toward the center - why we centralize onto the cloud and big platforms, and the dramatic ways that bites us when it breaks (Lessons 5–6).
  • Finally, who this all affects - who gets access and who doesn't, and who ends up controlling the pipes everyone depends on (Lessons 7–8).

You don't need to become a network engineer. The goal is to understand the internet well enough to be a thoughtful citizen of it - to know why it works, why it sometimes fails spectacularly, and who wins and loses from how it's built.
