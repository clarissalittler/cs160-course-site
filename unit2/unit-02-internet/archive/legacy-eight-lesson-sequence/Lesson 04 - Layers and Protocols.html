Lesson 4 - Layers, Protocols, and the Roads Not Taken

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what a protocol is, and why the internet is built as a stack of layers.
  • Name the layers of the TCP/IP model at a high level, and say what TCP and IP each do.
  • Explain why that layered design let the internet grow and change without being rebuilt.
  • Recognize that the web is one choice among possible alternatives - like Gopher and Gemini.

A protocol is just an agreement

You built one last lesson without me even calling it that: your Battleship rules were a protocol - an agreed-upon set of rules for how to exchange information. The internet runs on protocols. When two machines have never met and may be running completely different hardware, software, and languages, a shared protocol is what lets them cooperate anyway. It's the reason a fifteen-year-old phone and a brand-new data center can still talk to each other.

The big idea: layers, each doing one job

Here's the part worth actually remembering - and it's one idea, not a list to memorize. The internet isn't built as one giant, do-everything protocol. It's built as a stack of layers, where each layer handles one job and trusts the layer below it to handle the rest.

Mailing a birthday card works the same way:

  • You write the message (you don't think about trucks).
  • You put it in an envelope with an address (you don't think about which roads).
  • The postal service routes it (it doesn't care what the card says).
  • Trucks and planes physically carry it (they don't read addresses).

Each layer only has to do its own job and hand off to the next. The internet uses the same shape, and computer scientists give the layers names - you'll hear the whole scheme called the TCP/IP model (after its two star protocols, coming up in a moment). You do not need to memorize this. Just notice the shape: four jobs, stacked, each trusting the one below.

Read it top to bottom and it's the birthday-card story again: you use an app, which hands your message down to be split into packets, addressed, and physically carried - and the same happens in reverse at the other end. You order a video; you never once think about the transport layer or the fiber under the ocean.

TCP/IP: the two that do the heavy lifting

Two of those protocols are so central, and work so hand-in-glove, that people fuse their names into one - TCP/IP - and use it as shorthand for the internet's whole foundation. You've actually met both halves already; here's how they split the work:

  • IP (Internet Protocol) is the addressing and routing layer from Lesson 3. It stamps each packet with a destination address and gets it hopped across networks toward that address. IP is a bit of a gambler - it fires each packet off on its own and doesn't lose sleep if one goes missing.
  • TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) sits one layer up and plays reliability manager. It's what actually chops your message into the numbered packets from Lesson 2, hands them to IP, and then - the important part - checks that every piece arrived, asks for any lost ones to be re-sent, and reassembles them in order at the far end.

So the division of labor is simple: IP delivers individual packets; TCP makes sure the whole message shows up complete and in the right order. Together they turn "a pile of addressed packets bouncing around a redundant network" into "your email actually arrived, intact" - which is why "TCP/IP" gets used as a nickname for the entire foundation of the internet.

Why bother with layers? Because you can replace one layer without touching the others. When wifi was invented, the physical layer changed completely - radio instead of wires - and everything above it kept working, because the upper layers never cared how the bits moved, only that they did. That modularity is a huge part of why the internet could grow from a few hundred machines to billions without being torn down and rebuilt. It's the distributed philosophy again, this time in the design itself: no single layer has to know or control everything.

The web is one layer - and one choice

The thing you probably think of as the internet - websites, links, browsers - is really just one protocol sitting at the top application layer: HTTP, the HyperText Transfer Protocol. Your browser sends an HTTP request ("please send me this page"), a server sends the page back, and your browser draws it. The web is enormous and wonderful, but it's worth realizing it's not the only thing that could sit up there.

Just for fun: the roads not taken. Because protocols are just agreements, people have proposed and built alternatives to the web - some before it, some in reaction to it.

  • Gopher (1991, University of Minnesota) was a text-and-menus system for browsing documents across the internet - and for a moment in the early '90s it was neck-and-neck with the young Web. It faded after the graphical web took off (a licensing-fee scare in 1993 didn't help), but a small Gopher community still quietly runs today.
  • Gemini (2019, created by a pseudonymous developer called Solderpunk - not Google's AI, which just happens to share the name) is a deliberately tiny modern protocol, sitting somewhere between Gopher and the web. Its whole pitch is a reaction against today's bloated, ad-choked, tracker-laden web: no cookies, no JavaScript, no ads, no way to hide surveillance in the page. Pages are plain text with a dash of formatting. "Geminispace" is small, but it's a real, living community.

The point isn't that you should switch to Gemini. It's that the web you use every day was a choice, not a law of nature - and the fact that it grew huge, commercial, and centralized while these simpler cousins stayed small is itself a story about the pull toward the center, which is exactly where we're headed next.
