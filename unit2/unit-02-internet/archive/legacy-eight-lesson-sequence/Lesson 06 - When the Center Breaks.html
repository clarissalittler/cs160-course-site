Lesson 6 - When the Center Breaks

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe real outages where one company's glitch took down large parts of the internet.
  • Explain the paradox: a network built to survive damage, brought down by its own centralization.
  • Weigh who benefits, who's harmed, and who decides when so much depends on so few.

Last lesson we saw the internet quietly re-centralize onto a few clouds, CDNs, and platforms - for good reasons. This lesson is the bill coming due. Here's the strange thing about the outages below: none of them was the internet "breaking" in the old sense. No cables were cut, no war, no disaster. Each was a single company's software glitch - which cascaded across half the web precisely because so much of the web now leans on that one company.

December 2020: a school district cancels class

On a Monday morning in December 2020 - deep in pandemic remote learning - Google's login system failed for about an hour. Because so many services check in with that one system, Gmail, YouTube, Meet, and Google Classroom all went dark at once. Students couldn't sign in to their Chromebooks. Across the country, schools that ran their whole day on those tools had to scramble - delaying online classes and hunting for another way to reach students who suddenly couldn't log in. One Michigan district said the outage had simply knocked out its ability to hold virtual classes at all; there was nothing to do but wait for Google to come back.

Sit with that: a single company's authentication bug, and a morning of school across the country stalled. That's not a story about a fragile internet - the internet was fine. It's a story about what happens when a whole system funnels through one login.

June 2021: one setting takes down the web

In June 2021, huge swaths of the internet vanished at once - Amazon, Reddit, the New York Times, the Guardian, PayPal, CNN, even the UK government's gov.uk. The cause? A single configuration change at a CDN company called Fastly, tripped when one customer updated their settings, exposed a hidden bug that knocked out Fastly's whole network. The outage lasted under an hour - Amazon alone reportedly lost around $34 million in that window - and it hit sites that have nothing to do with each other, except that they all sat behind the same middleman you've never heard of.

And again... and again: Cloudflare

Then there's Cloudflare, one of the biggest CDNs of all. More than once, a bug inside Cloudflare - a bad update to its own bot-fighting system, for instance - has cascaded and taken down a chunk of the internet with it: X, Spotify, ChatGPT, Shopify, Dropbox, Coinbase, even a transit agency's schedule app, all stumbling at the same moment. Each time, the same headline writes itself: large parts of the internet went down today. Each time, it's the same root shape - too much depending on one provider.

The paradox, stated plainly. The internet was engineered to be the most resilient network humans ever built - distributed, redundant, able to route around a nuclear strike. And yet a school district lost a day of class over a login bug, and the web blinked out over one company's typo-sized config change. How? Because we took that gloriously distributed network and, layer by layer, for convenience and cost, re-concentrated it onto a handful of companies. The resilience is still down there in the wires. We just keep building new single points of failure on top of it.

So what do we do about it?

There's no clean villain here, and no easy fix. These companies are, most of the time, extremely good at keeping things up - probably better than the scattered mom-and-pop servers they replaced. The convenience is real and the outages are rare. But "rare" isn't "never," and when the failure comes, it comes for everyone at once - hospitals, banks, airlines, schools - instead of one site at a time. That's the essence of centralized fragility: fewer failures, but far bigger ones.

It's our three questions again, and they're sharp here. Who benefits from centralizing onto a few providers? (All of us, most days - it's cheaper, faster, easier.) Who's harmed when it breaks? (Whoever couldn't do the thing they needed - see class, catch a flight, reach a doctor - through no fault of their own.) And who decides how much of society's plumbing should run through three or four private companies? (Right now: mostly the market, and mostly nobody on purpose.)
