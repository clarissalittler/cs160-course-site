Lesson 8 - Who Controls the Pipes

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify who actually holds power over how the internet works, even though "no one owns it."
  • Explain net neutrality, internet censorship, and online tracking as questions of control.
  • Tie the unit together: a distributed network with quietly concentrated power.

We started this unit with a happy fact: nobody owns the internet. That's still true - and it's still remarkable. But "no single owner" is not the same as "no one has power over it." As the internet became the thing everything runs on, the question of who gets to make the rules got very real. Here's a tour of the people and companies with their hands on the controls.

The rule-makers: open standards

Start with the good news. The core rules of the internet - the protocols - are open standards, not owned by any company. They're hammered out by volunteer bodies like the IETF (the Internet Engineering Task Force), which is famous for making decisions by rough consensus rather than by vote: if a proposal is controversial enough to need a vote, the thinking goes, it should be reworked until the objections are answered. Anyone with the expertise can join the mailing list. A separate body, ICANN, coordinates the naming and address system so two sites can't grab the same name. For decades the U.S. government held formal oversight of that naming system; that oversight finally passed to ICANN's global community in 2016 (though critics still argue the U.S. holds outsized sway). This open, cooperative layer is a real achievement worth defending - it's a big reason the internet grew the way it did.

The gatekeepers: your ISP and net neutrality

To reach any of that openness, though, you go through your Internet Service Provider - your cable or phone company. That's a chokepoint, and it raises a decades-old fight: net neutrality, the principle that your ISP should treat all traffic equally - not secretly slow down a competitor's video, or charge extra to let certain sites load fast. Without that rule, the company you pay for access could quietly shape what you actually see.

The (exhausting) U.S. net-neutrality timeline

In the U.S. this has been a twenty-year tug-of-war. The FCC adopted strong net-neutrality rules in 2015 (treating broadband like a utility), repealed them in 2017, then voted to restore them in April 2024 - only to have a federal appeals court strike them down in January 2025, holding that reclassifying broadband is Congress's call, not the agency's. So as of now there are no federal net-neutrality rules; some states (notably California) enforce their own, and any lasting federal rule would have to come from Congress passing an actual law. Translation: whether your ISP can play favorites is still, genuinely, up for grabs.

The new chokepoints: infrastructure companies

Remember the clouds and CDNs from the centralization lessons? They don't just create fragility - they create power. When a huge fraction of the web sits behind a few infrastructure companies, those companies can, in effect, switch a site off. It has happened: a CDN deciding it no longer wants to serve a particular customer can knock that site off the internet, no government or court involved. Sometimes that's a company refusing to shield something genuinely vile; sometimes it's a handful of private firms quietly deciding what gets to exist online. Either way, it's real power that the internet's "no one's in charge" design never anticipated.

The watchers: tracking and surveillance

Control isn't only about what you can reach - it's about who's watching you reach it. Remember from the addresses lesson: your IP address locates you and ties your activity together, and the sites and networks you pass through can log where you go. Two big groups care a lot about that trail:

  • Advertisers and data brokers, who assemble startlingly detailed profiles of your habits to target ads - the quiet business model of much of the "free" web.
  • Governments. In 2013, Edward Snowden revealed that the U.S. National Security Agency was scooping up internet traffic on a mass scale. The disclosures pushed the whole industry toward encrypting traffic by default and kicked off a public argument about surveillance that flares up with every new technology.

The censors

Finally, control can mean simply blocking. Internet censorship - a government or network deciding what people can reach - runs from the clearly-defensible (blocking child sexual abuse material) to the clearly-repressive (silencing dissent and journalism). And it leans directly on the plumbing you learned: the most common way to block a site is at the DNS level, making its name un-look-up-able so your device never even learns the address. The distributed design fights back - traffic and tools route around blocks - but when access funnels through a few providers, censorship gets easier.

Pulling the unit together. "No one owns the internet" is true and worth cherishing - but power over it is real, and it's quietly concentrated: standards bodies write the rules, ISPs guard the on-ramps, a few infrastructure giants hold the chokepoints, advertisers and governments watch the traffic, and censors decide what's reachable. That's this whole unit in one breath: a network designed to be distributed and ungoverned, steadily pulled toward the center - which makes it more convenient, more fragile, and easier to control all at once. The point of learning how it works isn't to become an engineer. It's so that when you hear "net neutrality" or "outage" or "they're tracking you," you know what's actually at stake, and you get a say. Who benefits, who's harmed, who decides? On the internet, now more than ever, those are the questions.

Discussion: pick a lever

Choose one of the forms of control in this lesson - your ISP and net neutrality, an infrastructure company that can switch sites off, advertiser/government tracking, or outright censorship. In the discussion:

  • Explain in your own words how it lets someone shape your experience of the internet.
  • Give a real or realistic example of it helping and an example of it harming.
  • Who do you think should get to decide the rules for it - the company, the government, an open body like the IETF, users? Why?

That's the end of Unit 2! You can now explain the internet as a distributed "network of networks" that survives damage through packets and redundancy - and you can explain the constant pull toward the center that makes it convenient, fragile, and contested.
