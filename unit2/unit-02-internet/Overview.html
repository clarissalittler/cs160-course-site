Unit 2: The Internet

Every tap on a link crosses a chain of devices, networks, protocols, companies, and public decisions. This unit follows that chain. We start with the internet as a physical “network of networks,” then look at how packets, routers, addresses, names, ports, and layered protocols let independently operated systems cooperate. With those ideas in hand, we'll trace a web request through DNS, HTTPS, servers, clouds, and content delivery networks.

The same architecture raises social questions. A distributed protocol can end up supporting very centralized platforms. A redundant backbone can coexist with a household that has exactly one affordable on-ramp. Encryption can protect a page in transit while the destination platform records every visit. An open standard can invite anyone to participate while time, expertise, language, disability, and institutional resources decide whose voice actually carries weight.

How to use these lessons: Each page contains a complete text explanation and text-based practice. Diagrams come with meaningful alt text and, where needed, a full text equivalent such as an edge list or a sequence. Nothing in the unit depends on seeing or manipulating an embedded visual; all required information is in the prose too.

A recurring lens: connection, consequence, and power

Each lesson comes back to three questions:

  • Who benefits?
  • Who might be harmed or left out?
  • Who decides, and who should have a voice?

These questions don't replace technical analysis — they sharpen it. Knowing the layer and the organization involved tells you which people have the power to fix, restrict, fund, or redesign the thing in front of you.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the internet from Wi-Fi, the web, cloud services, and individual apps, and trace an ordinary interaction across local, provider, and destination networks.
  2. Explain packet switching, routing, headers and payloads, loss and retransmission, congestion, latency, throughput, and the practical limits of redundancy.
  3. Explain how IP addresses, ports, DNS, layering, encapsulation, and shared protocols let unfamiliar systems communicate.
  4. Trace an HTTPS request and response and explain what transport encryption protects and what it does not.
  5. Compare self-hosting, cloud, CDN, and platform dependencies, including convenience, scale, correlated failure, environmental costs, and control.
  6. Analyze an access or governance problem by identifying relevant stakeholders, layers, evidence, and both technical and institutional responses.

The five lessons in Unit 2
LessonCentral ideaQuestions to carry forward
1. A Network of NetworksIndependently operated physical networks cooperate to carry exchanges between clients and servers.Which parts are local, which are remote, what still works, and who operates each link?
2. Packets, Routing, and ResiliencePacket switching shares links; routers forward one step at a time; redundancy creates options rather than guarantees.Where can data wait, drop, or reroute? Who has an affordable backup?
3. Addresses, Names, and ProtocolsLayered agreements use addresses, ports, and names to turn bits into coordinated communication.What does an identifier actually identify? Which agreement or lookup failed?
4. The Web, Cloud, and Platform StackWeb requests cross an encrypted protocol stack; shared infrastructure offers scale while creating dependencies.What is protected, who can still observe, and what fails together? What can you publish yourself?
5. Access, Power, and GovernanceStandards bodies, providers, platforms, governments, communities, and users govern different layers of connection.Who can connect, watch, block, appeal, or redesign? What technical and institutional response fits?
