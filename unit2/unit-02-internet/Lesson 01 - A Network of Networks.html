Lesson 1: A Network of Networks

Big question

When an app says it's “connecting,” what is it connecting through — and which people and organizations make that connection possible?

"Connecting" is doing a lot of work in that little spinning message! In this lesson we'll pull it apart. We'll sort out the difference between the internet, the web, Wi-Fi, the cloud, and the apps you use, follow one ordinary request across several networks, and find the places where a failure (or somebody's decision) could stop that request from getting through.

Begin with an ordinary moment

You tap a link in D2L. A page appears. That tiny action may have involved your phone or laptop, a Wi-Fi access point, a router, Portland Community College's network, one or more commercial carriers, a hosting company, and a server humming away in another city. The reply crosses all of those networks in the other direction, often in less time than it takes to blink.

It feels like one smooth action ("the internet loaded the page"), and honestly it's kind of amazing that it works at all. Machines owned by completely different organizations, connected by completely different kinds of cables and radios, cooperate because they all follow the same technical agreements. And once you can see the pieces underneath, a lot of things start to make sense: why your connection fails the ways it does, who can watch or block what you're doing, and why "nobody owns the internet" is true but leaves out an awful lot.

Five things people call “the internet”

Everyday speech mushes several different things together under one word. We're going to pull them apart now, because the rest of the unit leans on the differences.

Related terms that are not interchangeable
TermWhat it meansConcrete example
InternetA worldwide system of interconnected computer networks that exchange data using Internet protocols.PCC's network exchanging packets with a web host's network.
Wi-FiOne way for a device to reach a nearby local network by radio.Your laptop connecting to a home router. Wi-Fi can be working fine even when the router has lost its internet connection.
WebA service that uses the internet to retrieve linked resources, usually with HTTP or HTTPS.Opening a Neocities page in a browser. Email and online games use the internet too, but they're not necessarily the web.
CloudSomeone else's computing and storage resources, rented or offered over a network.A school storing files in Google Drive rather than on a computer in the classroom.
App or platformA particular service built on top of internet connections.D2L, TikTok, a banking app, or an online game. If one app is down, the internet may still be fine.

So why be this fussy about words? Because it really helps the moment something breaks. If your browser announces "no internet," the first thing to ask is what still works. Can another device get on the Wi-Fi? Does a different site open? Does the site load if you switch your phone to cellular? Each of those little tests changes which piece is the likely culprit, and which company or person would have to fix it.

How networks connect

A network is just a group of devices that can send data to each other. Your home network might be a phone, a laptop, a game console, a printer, and a router. PCC runs much bigger networks that connect classrooms, offices, and campus services. Your mobile carrier runs another network, and a cloud company runs yet another. The internet is what you get when all of these separately run networks connect to each other and agree to pass each other's traffic along.

Your home network usually reaches the rest of the internet through an internet service provider, or ISP, which is the company you pay for internet service, like a cable or fiber company. A college might buy connections from several providers at once. Those providers then connect to other networks, sometimes directly and sometimes by paying bigger transit networks to carry their traffic farther, and the really big networks meet up at exchange points where they hand traffic back and forth.

There's a detail in here that I find really elegant: no packet needs a map of the entire internet. A router only needs enough information to pick a useful next step. One network accepts the packet, moves it toward another, and hands it off. It's less like one nationwide postal company and more like a chain of delivery services that have all agreed to pass each other's parcels along.

A text trace from a student to a course page

  1. Student device: a laptop creates a request for a course page.
  2. Local link: Wi-Fi carries the request to a nearby access point or router.
  3. Campus or home network: local networking equipment moves it toward an internet connection.
  4. ISP and transit: one or more provider networks carry it toward the network hosting the course site.
  5. Destination network: that network delivers the request to a server.
  6. Return trip: the server sends a response back through networks to the laptop. The return path need not be identical.

In real life the companies, the number of handoffs, and the physical route all vary. So think of this trace as a simplified model. It's detailed enough to show you who's responsible for each part of the trip, without pretending that every connection takes the same path.

The physical internet

Words like "wireless" and "the cloud" make the internet sound weightless. It is very much not! The Wi-Fi and cellular signals near you travel by radio, but most long-distance traffic eventually ends up in cables: copper wires, fiber-optic strands carrying pulses of light, and even cables lying on the ocean floor between continents. On top of that there are routers in equipment rooms and servers in data centers, and all of it needs land, electricity, cooling, and people to build it and keep it running.

And physical things have physical problems. A backhoe can cut a fiber line. Ice can bring down cables. A storm can knock out a neighborhood's power. Then there are the decisions people make: a landlord can decide whether a provider gets to wire their building at all, and a company can decide that running fiber out to a rural area just isn't worth the money. So "can this person get online?" is partly a technical question, but it's also a question about money, geography, and politics.

Clients and servers

A lot of what happens online is one program asking and another program answering. The one asking is the client, and the one answering is the server. When your browser asks for a web page, it's the client, and the program sending back the page is the server. By the way, "server" doesn't have to mean a giant special machine in a basement somewhere. One computer can run lots of server programs at once, and the same program can be a client in one conversation and a server in another.

Why set things up this way? Because one server can answer lots of people at once, and keep shared information, like everyone's grades, all in one place. But that also means everybody depends on it. If an entire class relies on one login server, that server can become a bottleneck even while every cable and router along the way is working fine. Later in the unit we'll separate "the internet is down" from "one service on the internet is down." They look identical when you're sitting on the couch, but they're very different problems.

A history with more than one motive

The internet didn't spring from one inventor or one goal. In the 1960s, U.S. government-funded researchers were exploring ways to let geographically separated computers — and the research groups using them — share scarce computing resources and communicate. ARPANET's first four nodes came online in 1969. Packet switching helped computers share communication links efficiently, and it meant a single failed node didn't automatically end every exchange.

Later work had to connect networks with very different personalities, including packet radio and satellite networks. Mobile military communication made it especially important that things kept working through interference, lost connections, and changing conditions. TCP/IP developed as a way for these independent networks to interoperate, and ARPANET switched over to TCP/IP in 1983. From there the modern internet grew through sustained public research, universities, standards communities, private networks, commercial providers, and millions of people who built services and communities on top.

You might have heard that ARPANET was built to survive a nuclear war. That story is catchy, but it isn't really right! Sharing resources and communicating were the main goals from early on, and being able to survive failures became important across several different lines of research. If you'd like to read more, the DARPA history of ARPANET and the Internet Society's history, written by several of the internet's pioneers, both tell the longer story.

Local failure, service failure, or internet failure?

Imagine D2L won't load. At least four different things could be going on:

  • Device or local-link problem: your laptop disconnected from Wi-Fi.
  • Local-network or ISP problem: your home router works, but your provider's connection is down.
  • Path problem: some networks can reach D2L's host while others can't, because of a routing or cable problem.
  • Service problem: the destination network is reachable, but D2L's application or login system is failing.

So how do you tell which one it is? Look at how far the failure spreads. If one device fails, suspect that device. If every device in the building fails, suspect the building's network or its provider. If one service is failing for people everywhere, suspect that service, or some shared company it depends on behind the scenes. Most of diagnosing a network problem is asking two questions over and over: what still works, and what do the broken things have in common?

And once you have a guess, there's usually something you can do about it. If it's your device, try turning its Wi-Fi off and back on. If it's every device at home, restarting the router and modem fixes a surprising number of problems, and if that doesn't work, your provider probably has an outage page you can check from your phone. If it's one service, look for that service's status page, or a site that tracks outages, to see whether it's down for everyone. If it is, the only thing to do is wait for someone else to fix it.

Everyday life and social impact

Nobody owns the whole network of networks, but every single piece of it is run by somebody. Your landlord might control what wiring gets into your building. Your ISP picks the prices and decides when to upgrade. The college sets rules for its own network, a cloud company decides where to build its data centers, governments fund infrastructure and regulate the companies, and standards groups write the agreements that let all of it work together.

  • Who benefits? Shared protocols mean a student, a neighborhood group, or a small business can publish something that devices around the world can reach — without negotiating with every network in between.
  • Who might be harmed or excluded? People can face no service, unaffordable service, an unsuitable device, inaccessible sites, or a connection controlled by an employer, school, landlord, or government.
  • Who decides? Lots of different people and organizations, each in charge of one piece of the chain. So even though nobody owns the whole internet, there are plenty of places along the way where someone can say no.

The Week 2 Neocities activity leans on one of the internet's most hopeful features: you can make a page and hand people its address, without asking anybody's permission to be a publisher. You don't have to settle for being a reader of somebody else's feed. Over the next few lessons we'll keep asking what makes that freedom possible, and what a person still needs in order to actually use it.

Try it: trace an everyday connection

Pick something you did online today, like opening a course page or checking when the next bus comes. Write a text trace with at least five stops. Include:

  1. your device and its local connection;
  2. the local network and ISP;
  3. at least one other network or service provider;
  4. the destination server or service; and
  5. one realistic failure at the device, local-network, ISP, or destination level.

Then explain one test that would help distinguish your chosen failure from the other three levels.

See one possible answer

A phone sends a request by Wi-Fi to a home router, through a fiber ISP, through a transit network, into the bus agency's hosting provider, and to its schedule server. If the bus site fails but other sites load on both the phone and a laptop, the local Wi-Fi and ISP probably work; the shared destination service is a stronger suspect.

Lesson takeaway

The internet is a network of networks, made of physical stuff and run by lots of different organizations. Wi-Fi is one way of getting onto it, and the web and cloud services run on top of it. Shared agreements let all those separately run networks cooperate, but every piece is still built and controlled by somebody. That's why the same picture of the pieces helps you figure out both why something broke and who could fix it.
