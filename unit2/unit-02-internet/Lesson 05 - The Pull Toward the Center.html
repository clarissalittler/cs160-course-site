Lesson 5 - The Pull Toward the Center

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what "the cloud" is, and why it isn't the same thing as the internet.
  • Describe how the cloud, content delivery networks, and big platforms concentrate the internet onto a few companies.
  • Explain the genuine benefits of centralizing - and the trade-off it creates.

So far the internet has looked wonderfully distributed: no owner, no center, packets routing around damage. All true. And yet, in practice, an enormous share of what you do online runs through a surprisingly small number of companies. This lesson is about why that happened - because the reasons are genuinely good ones - and it sets up the price we pay for it.

"The cloud" is just someone else's computer

What is the cloud, exactly? Is it the same as the internet? Not quite. In the early days, computers were huge and expensive, so people walked to a shared computer center to get anything done. Then computers got small and cheap and everyone had their own. But lately the pendulum has swung back: some jobs (search, voice recognition, streaming, training AI) need so much horsepower that it makes sense to send them off to gigantic warehouses filled with tens of thousands of machines. Those warehouses, taken together, are what we call the cloud.

That sticker says it best: "the cloud" is just someone else's computer. When your photos are "in the cloud," they're on a hard drive in a building owned by Apple, Google, or Amazon. You've been using the cloud all through this course - your Google Docs live there. And here's the concentration: the vast majority of the world's cloud computing is rented from just three companies - Amazon (AWS), Microsoft (Azure), and Google. Countless apps, banks, governments, and startups don't own servers at all; they rent from those three. A staggering slice of "the internet" physically sits in a few companies' warehouses.

The middlemen you've never heard of

There's a second layer of concentration most people never notice. To make websites fast and to shield them from attacks, most big sites don't serve you directly. They sit behind a content delivery network (CDN) - companies like Cloudflare, Fastly, and Akamai that keep cached copies of websites in hundreds of locations, so a page loads from a server near you instead of from across the world. It's a genuinely clever, distributed-sounding idea.

But a handful of these CDNs now sit in front of a huge fraction of all the websites you visit - one company, Cloudflare, reportedly helps serve something like a fifth of the entire web. When you load a news site, a shopping site, and a game, there's a decent chance all three quietly passed through the same middleman. You never see its name, but it's on the path.

And a few places where everyone spends their time

Add one more layer: platforms. The open web has billions of pages, but most people spend most of their time inside a few walled gardens - a handful of search engines, social apps, video sites, and stores. The distributed network is still under there, but our attention has centralized onto a short list of companies that increasingly decide what we see.

Why we did it - and the catch

It's tempting to treat this as a villain story, but centralizing happened for real, good reasons:

  • It's cheaper. Renting a slice of Amazon's cloud beats buying and babysitting your own servers.
  • It's easier. A tiny team can launch a global app in a weekend without owning a single machine.
  • It's usually more reliable. These companies employ world-class engineers and keep things running smoothly the vast majority of the time.
  • It's faster and safer, day to day. CDNs speed up the web and absorb attacks that would flatten a small site.

Here's the catch, and it's the heart of this unit. The internet was designed to have no center so it could survive losing any piece. But every time we funnel more of it through a few clouds, a few CDNs, a few platforms, we quietly rebuild exactly the kind of single points of failure the design was meant to avoid. We've traded some of the messy-but-tough resilience of a distributed network for the smooth-but-fragile convenience of a centralized one. Most days you get the convenience for free. On the rare day one of those companies has a bad morning, you find out what the trade really cost - which is exactly the next lesson.
