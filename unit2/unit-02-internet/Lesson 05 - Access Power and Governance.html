Lesson 5: Access, Power, and Governance

Big question

If no one owns the whole internet, who can still connect, watch, slow, block, or govern what people do through it?

This closing lesson has ambitions: identifying several kinds of internet power, explaining digital access as more than the presence of a signal, describing open-standards governance and net neutrality, distinguishing observation and control at different layers, and proposing both technical and institutional responses to an internet problem.

No single owner does not mean no power

The internet joins independently operated networks. That architecture keeps any one organization from being the universal owner — but every connection still passes through equipment, software, names, contracts, and institutions that somebody controls.

No row has total control. A platform can remove a post without removing its author from the internet. An ISP can interrupt a connection without deleting the destination server. A registrar can suspend a domain name while the server stays reachable by other identifiers. A government may compel several actors at once. To understand any particular dispute, find the specific layer and the specific lever.

Open standards are a form of governance

The core protocols aren't simply handed down by a corporation. The Internet Engineering Task Force, or IETF, develops many internet standards through working groups, public drafts, review, implementation experience, and what it calls rough consensus and running code. Rough consensus doesn't mean every participant prefers the final choice — it asks the chairs to determine that serious technical objections have been considered and the group can move forward.

The IETF's process is open: anyone can write an Internet-Draft, discussion happens on mailing lists and at meetings, and published RFCs are free for anyone to read. That openness supports interoperability and makes it much harder for one vendor to redefine a core protocol for everyone else single-handedly.

Formal openness is not the same as equal influence, though. Following long technical discussions takes time. Travel, language, employment, disability access, and specialized expertise all shape who can participate repeatedly. A large company can pay engineers to spend years in standards work; a community affected by a technical choice may never learn the discussion exists. So good governance asks two questions, not one: “Can anyone enter?” and “Who can realistically stay, be heard, and implement the result?” The IETF's guide to its standards process describes the path from a draft through working-group and broader review to an RFC.

Names and numbers need coordination

If two unrelated organizations both treated pcc.edu as their own unique global domain, nobody would know which one a link meant. If public IP addresses were assigned inconsistently, routers could deliver packets to the wrong network. ICANN, working with the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority functions, regional registries, registries, registrars, and a multistakeholder community, coordinates parts of these unique-identifier systems.

ICANN's role is important but limited: it coordinates the DNS root zone and identifier policies. It does not operate every name server, provide everyone's internet service, or decide the acceptability of all online content. The ICANN overview describes individuals, businesses, civil society, governments, and technologists as participants in its policy process.

Between them, the IETF and ICANN show that “governance” doesn't have to mean one government ministry running a network. Rules can emerge from standards, contracts, technical coordination, markets, courts, public agencies, and communities. The result can be broad collaboration — and also some truly frustrating gaps in accountability.

Access is not one yes-or-no question

The digital divide is the unequal ability to benefit from networked technology, and a coverage map showing a nearby connection is only the beginning of it. Meaningful access includes:

  • Availability: reliable infrastructure actually serves the location.
  • Affordability: service, fees, repair, and suitable devices fit a household's budget over time.
  • Quality: throughput, latency, data allowances, and reliability support the required task.
  • Devices: a person has more than a phone when a full keyboard, large display, local file management, or specialized software is needed.
  • Skills and support: people can use services, recognize risks, recover accounts, and get help without shame.
  • Accessibility: hardware, software, documents, and interactions work with the person's senses, movement, cognition, language, and assistive technology.
  • Safety, trust, and relevance: using the connection does not impose unacceptable surveillance, harassment, language barriers, or risk.

You can see these distinctions in the data. In the U.S. National Telecommunications and Information Administration's November 2023 survey, 12 percent of people lived in households with no internet connection. Among households earning at least $100,000, 80 percent had both fixed and mobile connections; among households earning under $25,000, only 54 percent did. Smartphone-only use also varied substantially across demographic groups. The NTIA's June 2024 summary emphasizes that overall use improved while the quality and experience of connection stayed unequal.

These figures are dated snapshots — later surveys should replace them when this lesson is updated. The conceptual point doesn't expire, though: counting everyone with some internet use as equally connected conceals differences that matter.

Why a phone is not always enough

A smartphone can be a powerful, essential computer. It can also be a lousy tool for comparing documents, writing a long paper, wrangling several downloaded files, programming, or using a website whose controls assume a large screen. A person can have “internet access” and still be unable to complete a task that was designed around a laptop with reliable home broadband.

This matters in a community-college course. Requiring locally installed software can exclude students working on a shared, borrowed, employer-controlled, or locked-down computer. Requiring a file to be downloaded, renamed, found, and uploaded can end up measuring file-management experience as much as the intended concept. Browser-based editors and shareable links don't solve every access problem, but they can remove installation privileges and file-transfer steps that were never the learning goal in the first place.

Accessibility is likewise part of access, not a bonus feature bolted on later. An image without useful alt text makes a fast connection deliver nothing to a blind reader. An activity requiring precise mouse movement can fail a student who navigates by keyboard. A video without accurate captions excludes content even when it streams flawlessly. Being included in the network means both reaching the bits and being able to use what they represent.

What different parties can observe

Surveillance gets much easier to reason about once you separate the participants:

  • Your device can see content before it is encrypted and after it is decrypted.
  • A local network and ISP carry packets and can see addressing, size, and timing. With ordinary unencrypted HTTP or DNS, they may see more content or names.
  • A DNS resolver sees queries sent to it and the network address making them, unless privacy techniques divide or hide that information.
  • The destination service receives the request content and can connect it to an account, cookie, IP address, device information, and prior activity.
  • A platform can observe activity within its own application even though HTTPS protects that activity from ordinary intermediaries.

Encryption changes who can read content on the path; it doesn't make data collection disappear. Swapping an ISP's DNS resolver for another provider hides queries from the ISP — and reveals them to the resolver you chose. A VPN moves some visibility and trust from the local ISP to a VPN provider. Privacy tools have real uses, but every one of them has a threat model: whom it protects against, what stays visible, and which new intermediary you now have to trust.

Blocking and moderation happen at different layers

A network can block a DNS answer, an IP address, a port, or recognizable unencrypted traffic. A platform can remove a post or an account while the rest of the internet stays reachable. An app store can prevent ordinary installation of software on devices it controls. A cloud or CDN provider can stop serving a customer. A government may order one or more of these actions.

And not all control serves the same purpose. A college may block malicious traffic to protect accounts. A platform may remove targeted harassment so a community can actually participate. A court may order action against unlawful material. The very same mechanisms can be used to suppress journalism, queer health information, organizing, or dissent. “No moderation” is itself a governance choice — one that can let powerful participants drive everyone else away.

Evaluating a control decision therefore takes more than sorting it into “censorship” or “safety.” Ask:

  • What exact content, account, protocol, or destination is affected?
  • Who made the decision under what authority and evidence?
  • Is the response targeted and proportionate, or does it block many uninvolved people?
  • Are the rule, explanation, appeal, and error-correction process accessible?
  • Can affected people communicate elsewhere, or does this actor control a practical bottleneck?

Net neutrality: power at the on-ramp

Net neutrality is the principle that an ISP providing access shouldn't block, throttle, or sell preferential delivery for lawful internet traffic based on its source, destination, service, or commercial affiliation — subject to reasonable network management. The issue is not whether routers may ever prioritize anything; networks have to manage congestion, emergencies, attacks, and services with different technical needs. The dispute is whether the access provider should use its gatekeeping position to choose winners for business or other nontechnical reasons.

Supporters argue that neutral access protects speech and innovation: a new site or a small creator can reach users without paying every ISP for a fast lane. Opponents of particular regulations argue that broad common-carrier rules can discourage investment, impose mismatched rules on changing technologies, or move too much authority to regulators. Both technical management and market power are real, and a policy has to distinguish them.

U.S. federal policy has swung back and forth. The FCC adopted rules in 2015, reversed its classification in 2018, and adopted another order in 2024. On January 2, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit set aside the 2024 order, holding that the FCC lacked statutory authority to impose those rules through the Communications Act classifications it used. As of this lesson's July 2026 revision, no federal net-neutrality rules are in effect, while state rules — including the California Internet Consumer Protection and Net Neutrality Act — can still apply. Elections, legislation, litigation, and agency action can all change this status, so this dated legal paragraph must be checked each term.

From diagnosis to response

Internet problems often need two kinds of response:

Pairing technical and institutional responses
ProblemTechnical responseInstitutional response
One fiber cut disconnects a communityBuild a physically separate backup path and tested failover.Fund underserved routes, require accurate resilience reporting, or support a community network.
A shared cloud outage stops classKeep an independent status page, offline materials, exports, and a fallback communication channel.Procure for portability and resilience; assign responsibility for continuity planning.
A harmful DNS block hides unrelated sitesUse more precise detection and limit the scope and duration of the rule.Publish criteria, notice, evidence, appeal, oversight, and error correction.
Students cannot use a required toolOffer a browser-based, keyboard-accessible, low-bandwidth route with equivalent text.Budget for devices and support; test requirements with affected students rather than assuming access.

A technical workaround can help an individual right now while leaving the underlying inequality untouched. A policy without a workable technical implementation can be purely symbolic. Strong responses connect both.

Everyday life and social impact: keep asking three questions

  • Who benefits? Open standards, public investment, reliable providers, encryption, accessible design, and well-governed communities can widen participation.
  • Who might be harmed or excluded? Costs, device assumptions, inaccessible interfaces, surveillance, arbitrary blocking, harassment, and concentrated infrastructure can turn an open protocol into an unequal experience.
  • Who decides? There is rarely one answer. Name the standards body, operator, company, government, institution, community, or user with the relevant lever — and ask how that actor is accountable to the people affected.

“The internet” is too broad to be the actor in a sentence like “the internet blocked me.” Precision is empowering here — it reveals exactly where a decision can be challenged or redesigned.

Synthesis studio: diagnose an internet incident

Choose one scenario:

  1. A course site opens by IP address but not by domain name on one campus network.
  2. A neighborhood has one advertised provider, but the monthly cost and installation fee are unaffordable to many residents.
  3. Several unrelated news sites return errors at the same time while email and small independent sites work.
  4. A creator's platform page disappears after a moderation decision, but the creator's personal site remains available.

Write a short incident map that includes:

  1. a text path showing the relevant layers and organizations;
  2. what still works and what does not;
  3. the most likely failing layer or decision-maker and one alternative explanation;
  4. who benefits, who is harmed or excluded, and who decides;
  5. one technical mitigation and one institutional response; and
  6. one new fact you would seek before acting.
See a model for scenario 3

Packets can reach other services, so the entire internet and the user's local link are not down. The news sites may share a CDN whose edge servers return errors; a shared DNS provider is another possibility. Readers and publishers are harmed, while the shared provider ordinarily benefits both groups through performance and security. A technical mitigation could use a second delivery path or a minimal origin-hosted fallback page. An institutional response could require dependency inventories, tested continuity plans, and prompt public incident reports. Before deciding, check the affected sites' DNS records, response headers, status pages, and whether the failures share a provider.

Connection to the Week 2 activity discussion

Publishing a Neocities page exercises a real freedom that open web standards created: you can make a resource, give it a URL, link to other people, and let them link back. In your discussion, look at the whole access chain. Free hosting lowers one barrier, but meaningful participation still requires a device, a connection, a usable editor, an accessible page, time, skills, and a host whose rules permit the work. The point is neither cynicism nor cheerleading. It's valuing a real, empowering capability while noticing what it takes to make that capability available to everyone.

Lesson takeaway

The internet has no single owner, but it's governed at every layer. Standards groups define shared protocols; identifier organizations coordinate names and numbers; ISPs control the on-ramps; infrastructure companies and platforms control important services; governments make law and investments; users and communities publish, organize, and build alternatives. Meaningful access takes affordability, quality, devices, skills, accessibility, safety, and voice — not merely a signal. And understanding the technical layer is what reveals who has the power to improve or restrict it.
