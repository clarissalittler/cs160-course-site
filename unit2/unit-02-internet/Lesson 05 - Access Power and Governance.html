Lesson 5: Access, Power, and Governance

Big question

If no one owns the whole internet, who can still connect, watch, slow, block, or govern what people do through it?

This last lesson pulls the unit together. We'll look at who has what kind of power over the internet, what it really takes to "have internet access" (it's a lot more than a signal), how the internet's rules get made, who can watch your traffic and who can block it, and what kinds of fixes are possible when something about the internet isn't working for people.

Who has power on the internet?

Remember that the internet is lots of separately run networks joined together. That's why no one organization owns the whole thing. But every connection still passes through equipment, software, names, contracts, and institutions that somebody controls.

Several actors and the power each can exercise

Standards communityShared protocol specificationsWhich technical features independent systems can implement compatibly

Notice that nobody in this table has total control. A platform can take down your post without kicking you off the internet. An ISP can cut off your connection without deleting the website you were trying to reach. A registrar can suspend a domain name while the server behind it is still up and reachable some other way. A government might force several of these at once. So when you hear about a fight over something online, ask which layer it's happening at, and whose hand is on that particular lever.

Open standards as governance

So who writes the internet's rules? The core protocols aren't just handed down by some corporation. A lot of them come from the Internet Engineering Task Force, or IETF, where people work in groups, publish drafts publicly, review each other's work, and try things out in real software. Their motto for making decisions is rough consensus and running code. "Rough consensus" doesn't mean everybody loves the final choice. It means the people running the group have to be satisfied that every serious technical objection has been heard and dealt with, so the group can move on.

The process is open: anyone can write a draft, the discussions happen on public mailing lists and at meetings, and the finished RFCs are free for anyone to read. That openness is a big part of why software from different companies works together, and it makes it much harder for any one company to quietly change a core protocol for everybody else.

But "anyone can join" isn't the same as "everyone has an equal say." Following long technical discussions takes a lot of time. Things like travel costs, language, your job, disability access, and specialized expertise all shape who can keep showing up, year after year. A big company can pay engineers to spend years working on standards, while a community that's affected by a technical choice might never even hear that the discussion is happening. So for any process that calls itself open, there are really two questions: can anyone get in? And who can realistically stick around, be heard, and actually build what gets decided? (If you're curious, the IETF's guide to its standards process walks through how a draft becomes an RFC.)

Coordinating names and numbers

Some things on the internet need someone to coordinate them. If two unrelated organizations both thought pcc.edu was theirs, nobody would know which one a link meant. If public IP addresses got handed out inconsistently, routers could deliver packets to the wrong network. So ICANN coordinates parts of these naming and numbering systems, working with a whole web of other organizations: the regional groups that hand out IP addresses, the registries and registrars that handle domain names, and a big community of businesses, governments, and individuals.

ICANN's job is important, but it's narrower than people sometimes think. It coordinates the very top of the DNS system and the rules for handing out names and numbers. It doesn't run every name server, it doesn't provide anyone's internet service, and it doesn't decide what content is acceptable online. The ICANN overview describes who takes part in making its policies.

So the IETF and ICANN show that "governing" the internet doesn't have to mean one government office running everything. Rules come out of standards, contracts, technical coordination, markets, courts, government agencies, and communities. Sometimes that leads to amazing collaboration, and sometimes it leaves really frustrating gaps where nobody seems to be responsible for anything.

What access means

The digital divide is the gap between people who can really make use of the internet and people who can't. And a coverage map showing that a connection is available nearby is only the very beginning of the story. Here's what it actually takes:

  • Availability: there's actually reliable service where you live.
  • Affordability: you can keep paying for the service, the fees, the repairs, and a decent device, month after month.
  • Quality: the connection is fast enough, responsive enough, reliable enough, and has a big enough data allowance for what you need to do.
  • Devices: you have more than a phone when the task needs a keyboard, a big screen, a way to manage files, or special software.
  • Skills and support: you know how to use the services, spot the risks, get back into a locked account, and get help without being made to feel dumb.
  • Accessibility: the hardware, software, and websites work with how you see, hear, move, think, and read, and with any assistive technology you use.
  • Safety, trust, and relevance: going online doesn't expose you to surveillance, harassment, or risks you can't accept, and there's something there for you in a language you read.

You can see these distinctions in the data. In the U.S. National Telecommunications and Information Administration's November 2023 survey, 12 percent of people lived in households with no internet connection. Among households earning at least $100,000, 80 percent had both fixed and mobile connections; among households earning under $25,000, only 54 percent did. Smartphone-only use also varied substantially across demographic groups. The NTIA's June 2024 summary emphasizes that overall use improved while the quality and experience of connection stayed unequal.

Those numbers are snapshots from a particular year, so they should be updated when newer surveys come out. But the point behind them doesn't expire: if you count everyone who uses the internet at all as equally connected, you hide differences that really matter.

Why a phone is not always enough

A smartphone can be a powerful and essential computer. It can also be a lousy tool for comparing two documents, writing a long paper, keeping track of a bunch of downloaded files, programming, or using a website that was obviously designed for a big screen. So a person can have "internet access" and still be unable to do something that was designed around a laptop with good home internet.

This matters a lot in a community-college course! If a class requires installing software, it can shut out students working on a shared, borrowed, or work computer that won't let them install anything. If an assignment requires downloading a file, renaming it, finding it again, and uploading it, it can end up testing how comfortable you are managing files as much as it tests the actual topic. That's part of why this class uses things like Colab and shareable links. They don't solve every access problem, but they skip some installing and file-shuffling steps that were never what you were supposed to be learning.

Accessibility belongs on this list too. If an image doesn't have useful alt text, the fastest connection in the world delivers nothing to a blind reader. If an activity needs precise mouse movements, it can shut out a student who navigates with the keyboard. A video without accurate captions leaves people out even when it streams flawlessly. In every one of these cases, the bits arrived just fine, and the person still couldn't use them.

What different parties can observe

Who can see what you do online? It gets a lot easier to think about once you separate out everybody involved:

  • Your device can see content before it is encrypted and after it is decrypted.
  • A local network and ISP carry packets and can see addressing, size, and timing. With ordinary unencrypted HTTP or DNS, they may see more content or names.
  • A DNS resolver sees queries sent to it and the network address making them, unless privacy techniques divide or hide that information.
  • The destination service receives the request content and can connect it to an account, cookie, IP address, device information, and prior activity.
  • A platform can observe activity within its own application even though HTTPS protects that activity from ordinary intermediaries.

So encryption changes who along the way can read what you send, but it doesn't make data collection go away. And every privacy tool just moves the trust around. Switching from your ISP's DNS resolver to a different one hides your lookups from your ISP, but now the new resolver sees them instead. A VPN hides your traffic from your local network and ISP, but now you have to trust the VPN company. These tools really are useful! You just want to know, for each one, who it protects you from, what's still visible, and who you're trusting now instead.

Blocking and moderation happen at different layers

Blocking can happen at almost every layer we've talked about. A network can block a DNS answer, an IP address, a port, or unencrypted traffic it recognizes. A platform can remove a post or an account while the rest of the internet stays reachable. An app store can stop software from being installed on the phones it controls. A cloud provider or CDN can stop serving a customer. And a government can order any of these.

But not all of it is for the same purpose. A college might block malicious traffic to keep accounts safe. A platform might remove targeted harassment so that the people being harassed can stick around. A court might order action against illegal material. And the very same tools can be used to suppress journalism, queer health information, organizing, or protest. Even "no moderation at all" is a choice about how to govern a space, and it's one that can let the loudest and most powerful people drive everyone else away.

So how do you tell whether a particular block or removal is reasonable? Just sorting it into "censorship" or "safety" usually doesn't get you very far. Here are some better questions to ask:

  • What exact content, account, protocol, or destination is affected?
  • Who made the decision under what authority and evidence?
  • Is the response targeted and proportionate, or does it block many uninvolved people?
  • Are the rule, explanation, appeal, and error-correction process accessible?
  • Can affected people communicate elsewhere, or does this actor control a practical bottleneck?

Net neutrality

Net neutrality is the idea that the company you pay for internet access shouldn't block your legal traffic, slow it down, or sell faster delivery to some sites over others, based on where the traffic comes from, where it's going, or which business it belongs to. (There's an exception for reasonable network management.) Now, nobody's arguing that networks can never prioritize anything. They have to deal with congestion, emergencies, attacks, and services with different needs, like video calls versus file downloads. The fight is about whether your ISP should be allowed to use its position as your on-ramp to pick winners and losers for business reasons.

Supporters say net neutrality protects free speech and new ideas, because a new site or a small creator can reach people without having to pay every ISP for a fast lane. People who oppose particular net neutrality rules say that heavy regulation can discourage companies from investing in their networks, apply old rules badly to new technology, or hand too much power to government regulators. Both sides are pointing at something real: networks do need managing, and an ISP really can use its position to pick winners. The hard part of writing a good policy is telling those two apart.

U.S. federal policy has swung back and forth. The FCC adopted rules in 2015, reversed its classification in 2018, and adopted another order in 2024. On January 2, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit set aside the 2024 order, holding that the FCC lacked statutory authority to impose those rules through the Communications Act classifications it used. As of this lesson's July 2026 revision, no federal net-neutrality rules are in effect, while state rules — including the California Internet Consumer Protection and Net Neutrality Act — can still apply. Elections, legislation, litigation, and agency action can all change this status, so this dated legal paragraph must be checked each term.

From diagnosis to response

When something about the internet isn't working for people, there are usually two kinds of fixes, a technical one and an institutional one, meaning a change in how some organization makes its decisions:

Pairing technical and institutional responses
ProblemTechnical responseInstitutional response
One fiber cut disconnects a communityBuild a physically separate backup path and tested failover.Fund underserved routes, require accurate resilience reporting, or support a community network.
A shared cloud outage stops classKeep an independent status page, offline materials, exports, and a fallback communication channel.Procure for portability and resilience; assign responsibility for continuity planning.
A harmful DNS block hides unrelated sitesUse more precise detection and limit the scope and duration of the rule.Publish criteria, notice, evidence, appeal, oversight, and error correction.
Students cannot use a required toolOffer a browser-based, keyboard-accessible, low-bandwidth route with equivalent text.Budget for devices and support; test requirements with affected students rather than assuming access.

Why both? Because a technical workaround can help one person right now while leaving the bigger problem exactly where it was, and a new policy with no realistic way to actually carry it out doesn't change much of anything. That's why every row of the table has both columns filled in.

Everyday life and social impact: keep asking three questions

  • Who benefits? Open standards, public investment, reliable providers, encryption, accessible design, and well-governed communities can widen participation.
  • Who might be harmed or excluded? Costs, device assumptions, inaccessible interfaces, surveillance, arbitrary blocking, harassment, and concentrated infrastructure can turn an open protocol into an unequal experience.
  • Who decides? There is rarely one answer. Name the standards body, operator, company, government, institution, community, or user with the relevant lever — and ask how that actor is accountable to the people affected.

"The internet" is too big and vague to be the one doing something, in a sentence like "the internet blocked me." Was it your school's DNS resolver? The platform's moderators? A court order? Once you know which one, you know where to push back, or what would have to be redesigned.

Practice: diagnosing an internet incident

Choose one scenario:

  1. A course site opens by IP address but not by domain name on one campus network.
  2. A neighborhood has one advertised provider, but the monthly cost and installation fee are unaffordable to many residents.
  3. Several unrelated news sites return errors at the same time while email and small independent sites work.
  4. A creator's platform page disappears after a moderation decision, but the creator's personal site remains available.

Write a short incident map that includes:

  1. a text path showing the relevant layers and organizations;
  2. what still works and what does not;
  3. the most likely failing layer or decision-maker and one alternative explanation;
  4. who benefits, who is harmed or excluded, and who decides;
  5. one technical mitigation and one institutional response; and
  6. one new fact you would seek before acting.
See a model for scenario 3

Packets can reach other services, so the entire internet and the user's local link are not down. The news sites may share a CDN whose edge servers return errors; a shared DNS provider is another possibility. Readers and publishers are harmed, while the shared provider ordinarily benefits both groups through performance and security. A technical mitigation could use a second delivery path or a minimal origin-hosted fallback page. An institutional response could require dependency inventories, tested continuity plans, and prompt public incident reports. Before deciding, check the affected sites' DNS records, response headers, status pages, and whether the failures share a provider.

Connection to the Week 2 activity discussion

Publishing a Neocities page uses a real freedom that open web standards made possible: you can make something, give it an address, link to other people, and let them link back to you. In your discussion, think about the whole chain it takes to get there. Free hosting removes one barrier, but you still need a device, a connection, an editor you can use, a page that other people can access, time, skills, and a host whose rules allow what you want to make. None of this makes the activity less worthwhile! Making a page is a real and empowering thing to be able to do, and part of appreciating it is noticing what it takes for everyone to be able to do it.

Lesson takeaway

Nobody owns the whole internet, but somebody is in charge at every layer of it. Standards groups write the shared protocols, and organizations like ICANN keep names and numbers unique. ISPs control the on-ramps, and the big infrastructure companies and platforms control a lot of the important services. Governments make laws and pay for infrastructure, and users and communities publish, organize, and build alternatives. Real access takes a lot more than a signal: it takes affordable service, a good enough connection, the right device, skills, accessibility, safety, and a voice. And knowing how the technical layers work is how you figure out who has the power to improve, or restrict, any particular piece of it.
