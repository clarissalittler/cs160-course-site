Lesson 3 - Addresses and Names

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why every device on the internet needs an address.
  • Describe how a human-friendly name like pcc.edu gets turned into a numeric address.
  • See why addresses and names are also how you get located, tracked, and blocked.

Packets can only reroute around trouble if they know where they're headed. So the distributed design needs one more thing: a way to address every device, and a way to turn the names humans use into the numbers machines use. Let's discover the addressing part by playing a game.

Warm-up: Battleship without talking

You're going to play a slightly unhinged version of Battleship - not one game, but several at once against several people. Watch the two short videos for the setup and the rules, then play a few rounds with friends or family, paying close attention to what you actually have to say to keep the game going.

Group Activity

With your study group, design an efficient protocol to play a 4-person game of Battleship with no talking - using only 0's and 1's. Post it to Unit 2 Lesson 3 - Battleship Protocol. Wrestle with the questions that make it a real protocol:

  • How do you say who a message is for and who it's from - do you use names, or numbers? (Numbers are shorter and never ambiguous.)
  • How does the receiver know where one address ends and the next begins?
  • What else does every message need - the target square, hit or miss?
  • Show what you'd send, in 0's and 1's, for: "player 1 to player 3, fire on A2," and "player 3 to player 1, miss on A2." Explain what each bit means.

One post per team, with all names. Then compare with other groups - did anyone find a cleverer encoding?

Here's the punchline: you just reinvented the core of how the internet addresses messages. You needed a standard way to say who's sending, who's receiving, and where one address stops and the next starts. Real computers face exactly this, and solve it with IP addresses.

IP addresses: a number for every device

The Internet Protocol (IP) gives every device that connects to the internet a unique address. An old-style IPv4 address looks like 192.168.1.1 - four numbers (each 0–255) separated by dots. Routers all along the way read the destination address on each packet and pass it toward that address, hop by hop, the same way the post office reads the address on an envelope. That's really all you need to know about it for now: every device has an address, and that's how packets find their way.

IP Addresses & DNS

DNS: the internet's phone book

But you don't type 172.217.4.206 to visit a website - you type google.com. Something has to translate the friendly name into the numeric address, because humans remember names and machines route by numbers. That something is DNS, the Domain Name System - basically a giant, distributed phone book for the internet.

Why not just keep one master list on your computer? Because addresses change constantly - restart your router or walk into a coffee shop and your device may get a brand-new address. With billions of devices coming and going, no single list could keep up. So DNS is spread across many servers that look names up for you on the fly - itself a nicely distributed solution.

The quiet "so what." Addresses and names are how the internet finds you - which means they're also how you get found. Your IP address reveals roughly where you are and ties your activity together, which is the raw material of online tracking. And DNS is a favorite place to block things: the most common way a country or a school network censors a site is simply to make its name un-look-up-able, so your device never learns the address. Keep that in your back pocket - we'll come back to tracking and censorship in the unit's final lessons: who benefits from being able to locate and block, and who's harmed?
