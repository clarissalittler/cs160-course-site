# Week 8 Assignment: Quantitative Reasoning (Data)

> **Note for instructors:** This document is the canonical source for Week 8 Assignment and supersedes "Week 8 Assignment - Quantitative Reasoning (QR) v 2025.pdf". The old PDF said in one place that a dataset "will be provided" and in another that students should find their own — this version resolves that: students find their own dataset via Data Is Plural.

## Purpose

In this assignment you'll find a dataset, ask a question of it, and pull some actual insight out of the numbers — then step back and think about what your analysis means for real people. This is quantitative reasoning: the same skill you use when a news article throws a chart at you and you have to decide whether to believe it.

The process is the one we practiced in Unit 8: start with a question the dataset can answer, figure out which columns you need, represent the data with charts, and interpret what you see.

After completing this assignment, you will be able to:

- Draw conclusions from data by using quantitative reasoning, including reading charts, graphs, or tables.
- Explain the societal impact of a data analysis using insights and information obtained from the dataset.

## Task

Find your own dataset and perform some analysis on it. This is meant to be an act of creative exploration: you can pick a topic that is serious or silly. The site we suggest for finding a dataset — the archives of the excellent newsletter *Data Is Plural* (https://www.data-is-plural.com/archive/) — has everything from cups of coffee consumed in the 1990s show *Twin Peaks*, to coral reef health in the Pacific, to data that was used to track down the fate of migrant children placed in the homes of strangers.

A tip: look for a dataset with a .csv file you can download — that's the easiest form of data to open in Google Colab (or Sheets, or Excel, or whatever tool you like).

**Work through the six steps below, answering every prompt in your document.**

### 1. State the Problem or Question

Find a dataset via the Data Is Plural archive and paste the URL of the dataset into your document.

What dataset did you choose? State an interesting question you'd like to answer using this dataset, and **predict what you think the answer will be** — you'll check your prediction in step 5.

### 2. Determine Information Needed

Answer questions a–d:

a. What information will you need to answer your question? Which columns of data are relevant?
b. Explain why this information is important.
c. Is the data quantitative (and if so, is it discrete or continuous) or categorical?
d. What type of charts (bar chart, histogram, scatter plot, or crosstab) will you use to visualize the data, and why is that type a good fit?

### 3. Representation

Open the dataset in a Google Colab notebook (or another application of your choosing) and use the charting tools to make the chart(s) you described in 2d. Every chart needs:

- a title,
- labeled x- and y-axes (with units!),
- a legend, if there's more than one series.

Paste your chart(s) into your document.

### 4. Interpretation

What patterns in your charts from step 3 address your question from step 1? In your response, make sure to:

a. Use mathematical terms (percents, counts, averages, ranges, &c.)
b. Use units of measurement correctly (percents, counts, money, prediction scores, &c.)
c. Identify any assumptions you've made in your interpretation

### 5. Conclusions

Does your interpretation in step 4 actually answer your question from step 1? Consider these limitations:

a. Does the data align with your prediction from step 1? Why or why not?
b. **Correlation vs. causation.** If you found a pattern — say, two things rising and falling together — that's a *correlation*. It does not, by itself, mean one causes the other. For your pattern: what are at least two different explanations? Could A cause B? Could B cause A? Could some third thing (a *confounder*) be driving both? Could it be coincidence? Which explanation do you find most plausible, and — importantly — what would you need to know to actually tell?
c. Discuss any inconclusive or limited results. Would modifying your question (or getting more/different data) lead to a more conclusive result?

### 6. Social Impact

Data analysis isn't neutral — someone collected this data, someone is described by it, and conclusions drawn from it can change how people are treated. Answer all three:

a. **Who benefits?** Who could use your conclusions, and to do what? (Set policy, sell something, win an argument, allocate money?)
b. **Who's affected?** What communities are described by or impacted by this data? How might they react to your analysis — and would they say the data represents them fairly? Is anyone *missing* from the dataset who should be in it?
c. **Who decides?** Who collected this data and chose what to measure? How might those choices have shaped what your charts can and can't show?

## Requirements

Your submitted document must include:

1. The URL of a dataset from the Data Is Plural archive, plus your question and prediction (step 1)
2. Written answers to every lettered prompt in steps 2, 4, 5, and 6
3. At least one chart, embedded in the document, with a title and labeled axes (step 3)
4. An interpretation that uses actual numbers from your data, not just "it went up"
5. A genuine discussion of correlation vs. causation for the pattern you found (step 5b)
6. Answers to all three social-impact questions (step 6)

## What to Submit

Put everything — your answers and your embedded chart(s) — into a single document (Google Doc exported to PDF, Word, or PDF all work) and upload it to the Week 8 Assignment dropbox in D2L. Make sure the charts are actually embedded in the document, not links to somewhere else.
