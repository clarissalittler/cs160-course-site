# Week 10 Activity — Investigate a Scam

## What's this all about?

This unit is about cybersecurity, and here's the unglamorous truth at the center of it: the biggest threat to most people online isn't some hooded genius cracking your encryption. It's a *con*. A trick. Someone talking you — or your grandmother, or your coworker — into clicking a link, sending a gift card, or typing your password into a page that just *looks* like your bank. The fancy term is **social engineering**, but it's the oldest crime there is, wearing a new outfit.

The single best defense is knowing the playbook. A scam you can *recognize* is a scam that can't touch you. So this week you're going to become the class's resident expert on **one** specific internet scam. You'll pick one, dig into how it actually works, and write it up so clearly that a classmate (or your own family) could spot it coming.

> **No coding this week.** This is research and writing — investigate, then teach.

## Step 1: Pick a scam

Choose **one** scam to investigate. Pick something real and specific — not "phishing" in general, but a particular flavor of it. Some good ones to choose from:

- **The "grandparent" / family-emergency scam** — a panicked call or text: "Grandma, it's me, I'm in jail, please don't tell Mom, I need bail money."
- **Romance scams** — a months-long fake relationship that ends in a request for money.
- **The fake invoice / tech-support pop-up** — "Your computer has a virus! Call this number!" or "Your Geek Squad subscription renewed for $399."
- **Gift-card scams** — "Pay your overdue taxes / utility bill / boss with Apple gift cards." (Why always gift cards? That's a great thing to dig into.)
- **Phishing emails** that impersonate a bank, the IRS, Amazon, Netflix, or a delivery service.
- **The "pig butchering" crypto investment scam** — a long con that lures victims into a fake investment platform.
- **Fake job offers** that "accidentally" overpay you with a bad check and ask you to wire the difference back.
- **Marketplace / Venmo / Zelle scams** when buying or selling secondhand goods.
- **QR-code scams** ("quishing") — stickers slapped over real parking-meter or menu QR codes.
- **SIM-swapping** — taking over your phone number to defeat text-message security codes.

Or propose your own — anything that runs through the internet, phones, or email counts. (Run an unusual choice by me if you're not sure.)

## Step 2: Investigate it

Find **at least two trustworthy sources** about your scam and learn how it really works. Good places to look:

- The **FTC's consumer site** ([consumer.ftc.gov](https://consumer.ftc.gov)) — searchable, plain-language, excellent.
- The **FBI's IC3** (Internet Crime Complaint Center) and its annual reports.
- The **AARP Fraud Watch Network** (great on scams that target older adults).
- Your **bank's or a major bank's security pages** (they document the scams they see).
- Reputable news investigations and consumer-protection nonprofits.

> **Be a critical reader** (it's the whole unit): be a little suspicious of random blog posts, anything trying to *sell* you "protection" software, and AI chatbot answers you can't trace to a source. Note *where* you got each fact. A scam writeup built on a scammy source would be ironic.

As you read, hunt for these specifics:

- **The hook:** how does the scam first reach the victim? (Email? Text? A call? A dating app? A pop-up?)
- **The pressure:** what emotion does it lean on — fear, urgency, greed, loneliness, helpfulness? Scams almost always rush you and isolate you. *Where* does yours do that?
- **The ask:** what does the scammer ultimately want — money, a password, a code, remote access to a device?
- **The tell:** what are the red flags? What's the one detail that gives it away?
- **The defense:** what should someone do instead? What's the safe move?

## Step 3: Write it up as a post

Write a clear, friendly **post** (roughly 300–500 words) that teaches your scam to a non-expert. Imagine you're writing the thing you'd want your least-techy relative to read. Structure it however you like, but make sure it covers:

1. **What it's called and how it works** — walk through the con step by step.
2. **A realistic example** — a short "here's how it'd play out" story. (Make one up, clearly labeled as an example. Don't use a real person's details.)
3. **The red flags** — the specific things that should make someone's alarm bells ring.
4. **What to do instead** — the safe move, and where to report it ([reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov) is the U.S. one).
5. **Your sources** — list the two-plus places you learned this from.

Write in your own voice. The goal isn't a dry report — it's something a person would actually *read and remember* when the scam shows up in their inbox six months from now.

## Reflection

Include a short reflection at the end of your post (a few sentences):

1. Almost every scam works by hijacking an *emotion* — fear, urgency, love, greed, or just the desire to be helpful. Why is going after feelings so much more effective for criminals than going after the technology itself?
2. Who do scams like yours tend to target, and why *them*? (Think about who's isolated, who's trusting, who's under financial pressure, who's less familiar with the tech.) Connect this to our thread: who's most harmed, and who's responsible for protecting them?

## What to turn in

Post your full writeup on the **Week 10 discussion thread:**

1. Your 300–500 word scam post (the five parts above).
2. Your short reflection.

Then **read at least two classmates' posts** on *different* scams from yours, and reply. In your reply, say one thing you didn't know and one way you'd help someone you love avoid that particular scam. By the end of the week, as a class, we'll have a genuine field guide to internet cons — keep it; share it with your family.
