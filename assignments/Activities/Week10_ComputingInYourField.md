# Week 10 Activity — Computing in Your Field

## What's this all about?

In the first week of the course you introduced yourself, and most of you said something about what you're studying or what you'd like to do: nursing, art, accounting, welding, teaching, music, biology, undecided, &c. Ten weeks later, I'd like you to go back to that field and find out how computing is being used in it *right now*.

Why "right now," and why do I want *you* to find out instead of telling you? Because this stuff changes *fast*. Job titles and tools in computing appear and disappear within a year or two. (Remember when "prompt engineer" was going to be the hot new job? Yeah.) Anything I wrote down about them would probably be out of date before the next term starts. So you're the researcher this time. You'll find one real, current example of computing at work in your field, and then look at it using the ideas from this course.

The second part is the real point. Over ten weeks we've looked at how information is represented, how networks move it around, how programs make decisions, what happens when one rule runs on everyone, how data can mislead, and how a model learns from examples. None of that was only about computers in the abstract! It's a set of questions you can ask about anything a computer does, including whatever it's doing in your field.

> **No coding this week.** This is a short piece of research and writing. Keep it small, since it's the last week and you have the scam investigation too.

Any field counts: your major, a job you have now, a job you'd like someday, or (if you're undecided, or you'd rather not share your plans) any field or hobby you care about.

## Step 1: Pick one specific use of computing

Choose one specific thing, not a whole area. "AI in healthcare" is too big to say anything useful about in a few hundred words. Some examples of the right size:

- **Healthcare:** software that flags hospital patients who might be getting sicker, or that reads medical images to point out spots a radiologist should look at.
- **Retail or food service:** scheduling software that decides who works which shifts.
- **Education:** tools that check student writing for plagiarism or for AI-generated text.
- **Agriculture:** soil-moisture sensors that decide when and how much to irrigate.
- **Art, design, or music:** generative tools for images or audio, or the recommendation systems that decide whose work gets seen and heard.
- **Finance or accounting:** fraud detection that freezes a transaction, or software that sorts receipts and expenses automatically.
- **Trades and manufacturing:** machines controlled by programs (like CNC cutting), or sensors that predict when equipment will need repair.
- **Law, government, or social work:** risk scores, eligibility checks, or automated screening of applications.

Or anything else, as long as it's real and you can find out how it's used.

## Step 2: Find out how it works today

Find **at least two sources**, and try to make at least one of them come from inside the field itself. That could be a professional association, a trade publication, a union or industry newsletter, or a conversation with someone who does the work. (If you talk to someone, ask their permission first, and don't name them or their employer unless they're happy with that.)

Some cautions:

- **Check the date on everything.** For this activity, a source from five years ago is describing a different world. Prefer sources from the last year or two, and note the date of each one.
- **Be suspicious of sales pitches.** A company selling the product will describe it at its best. Look for at least one source that doesn't make money from it.
- **Don't rely on an AI chatbot's answer** unless you can trace it to a real source you've checked yourself.
- **Don't share anything confidential** about a workplace, and don't post anyone's personal information.

As you read, look at your example through **at least three** of these lenses from the course:

- **Data (Units 1, 5, and 8):** What information does it use? Where does that information come from, who collected it, and did the people it describes agree to that? What gets left out?
- **The network (Unit 2):** Does it depend on the internet, a cloud service, or one particular company? What happens to the work if that goes down?
- **Rules at scale (Units 4 and 6):** Is it applying a rule, score, or threshold to many people or things at once? What does the rule assume? Which is worse here, something wrongly flagged or something that slips through, and who bears each kind of mistake?
- **Cost and limits (Unit 7):** Is there anything it can't do, or that's too slow or expensive to do well?
- **Learning from examples (Unit 9):** Is there a trained model involved? What was it trained on, and how would anyone know when it's wrong?
- **Security and privacy (Unit 10):** What could go wrong if it were attacked, or if its data leaked?

You won't find answers to all of these, and that's fine! Sometimes "the company doesn't say what data it uses" is the most interesting thing you'll find.

## Step 3: Write it up as a post

Write a post of roughly **250–400 words** that someone outside your field could follow. Cover:

1. **Your field and your example.** What is the tool or system, and what does it do in your field?
2. **How it works,** as far as you could find out, using at least three of the lenses above.
3. **Who benefits, who's harmed, who decides?** Who chose to use it, who gains from it, and who carries the risk when it gets something wrong?
4. **One question you'd want to ask** the people who built it or the people who use it every day.
5. **Your sources,** with the date of each one.

## Reflection

End your post with a few sentences on one of these:

- Which idea from the course turned out to be most useful for making sense of your example, and why?
- Did anything in this course change how you see your field, or the technology people in it use?
