# Legacy Unit Discussion Prompts

Archived July 2026 while the weekly creative activities became the course's primary discussion experiences.

The prompts below previously appeared inside lesson pages, unit overviews, and the course schedule. They are preserved here in readable form so their questions, examples, and activity ideas can be reused later. Formatting and D2L-specific navigation language have been normalized; the source path for each prompt is recorded.

The Course Lobby, Ask Questions forum, introductory post, and study-group organization are not part of this archive because they remain general course communication rather than unit-content discussions.

## Week 1 — Digital Information

### Circle Square Patterns

**Former source:** `unit1/unit-01-digital-information/Lesson 02 - Circle Squares and Binary Numbers.html`

Students posted a link to their circle-and-square Google Slide deck and explained:

- How counting in their circle-and-square system was similar to ordinary counting
- How it was different

After posting, students compared their rules with classmates' rules and discussed why independently invented systems did or did not use the same conventions.

### Black and White Digital Images

**Former source:** `unit1/unit-01-digital-information/Lesson 05 - Black and White Images.html`

Students uploaded the two digital images they created with the Pixelation Widget and answered:

- How many bits did each image take, including metadata?
- How long did each image take to build?
- Which image was a better representation of the original, and why?
- What are the advantages and disadvantages of sampling an image more frequently?

Students could then view and reply to classmates' images.

### Intellectual Property

**Former source:** `unit1/unit-01-digital-information/Lesson 09 - Intellectual Property.html`

Students chose one source:

- The article “Is Fortnite stealing black dance culture?” about copying, credit, and profit
- A recent news story about a creator and generative AI

They then answered:

- What was digitized?
- What was the goal or purpose of digitizing it?
- Who benefited?
- Who was harmed?
- Were the impacts intended or unintended? How could they tell?

## Week 2 — The Internet

### Battleship Protocol

**Former source:** `unit2/unit-02-internet/Lesson 03 - Addresses and Names.html`

Study groups designed an efficient binary protocol for a four-player Battleship game with no spoken communication. They considered:

- How a message identifies its sender and recipient
- How a receiver knows where one address ends and another begins
- How to encode a target square and a hit-or-miss response
- A full binary example for “player 1 to player 3, fire on A2”
- A full binary example for “player 3 to player 1, miss on A2”

Each team made one post with all names and compared its encoding with other groups.

### Your Own Outage

**Former source:** `unit2/unit-02-internet/Lesson 06 - When the Center Breaks.html`

Students recalled an outage or found a recent outage in the news, then discussed:

- What broke and what people could not do as a result
- Whether “the Internet” failed or one heavily depended-upon company failed
- Who was harmed and whether they had any say in that dependency
- What a person, school, or city could reasonably do to reduce dependence on one provider

### The Divide Near You

**Former source:** `unit2/unit-02-internet/Lesson 07 - Who's On, Who's Not.html`

Students considered the digital divide in their own community and shared:

- Something in their life that now effectively requires Internet access
- Who might struggle to do it and whether the barrier was cost, location, devices, or experience
- Whether reliable Internet should be treated more like a paid product or a public utility

### Pick a Lever

**Former source:** `unit2/unit-02-internet/Lesson 08 - Who Controls the Pipes.html`

Students chose one form of control—an ISP and network neutrality, an infrastructure company, advertising or government tracking, or censorship—and explained:

- How it could shape someone's Internet experience
- One realistic example of it helping and one of it harming
- Who should make the rules: a company, government, open standards body, users, or someone else

## Week 3 — Introduction to Programming

### Lego Algorithms

**Former source:** `unit3/unit-03-python-introduction/Lesson 01 - Algorithms and Pseudocode.html`

Students wrote a precise algorithm for building a small Lego structure. A classmate followed the instructions exactly and tried to recreate it. The comparison illustrated the gap between what an algorithm's author meant and what the instructions actually specified.

### Tax Rate Program

**Former source:** `unit3/unit-03-python-introduction/Lesson 04 - Arithmetic.html`

With a partner, students wrote a program that asked for a purchase amount, calculated tax, and displayed a readable result. The program was posted to the Unit 3 Tax Rate Program discussion.

## Week 4 — Decisions and Loops

### Practice Program

**Former source:** `unit4/unit-04-python-decisions-and-loops/Lesson 02 - Making Decisions with if, elif, and else.html`

Partners designed and coded a small program of their choice. They began with a sample run and pseudocode, included at least one input and output, and used an `if`, `if-else`, or `if-elif-else` structure. They posted the program with both names, then offered a classmate suggestions or kudos.

### Tuition Program

**Former source:** `unit4/unit-04-python-decisions-and-loops/Lesson 05 - for Loops and range.html`

Students wrote a `for` loop displaying projected tuition for several years. The default scenario began at $8,000 per semester and increased 3 percent annually for five years. Students could choose another rate or duration if they explained it, then posted their code and responded to two classmates.

## Week 5 — Collections

### Budget Program

**Former source:** `unit5/unit-05-python-collections/Lesson 04 - Loop Patterns.html`

Students wrote a program that asked for a monthly budget, repeatedly collected expenses with a sentinel value, maintained a running total, and reported whether the user stayed within budget. They posted the `.py` file and pseudocode and gave two classmates generous suggestions and kudos.

## Week 6 — Working with Real Data

### Hobbies Program

**Former sources and references:** `unit5/unit-05-python-collections/Lesson 03 - Looping Over Lists.html`, `unit6/unit-06-python-real-data/Overview.html`, and `unit0/course-information/Course Schedule.html`

Partners built an output string while looping through a list of hobbies, posted the code with both names to the “Unit 6 - Hobbies Program” discussion, and left a classmate feedback and kudos. ## Week 7 — Algorithms

### Conway's Game of Life

**Former source:** `unit7/unit-07-Algorithms and Algorithm Efficiency/Lesson 06 - Conways Game of Life.html`

After spending ten minutes experimenting with a Game of Life simulator and watching “Life in Life,” students posted:

- A pattern they found interesting, including what it did over time
- Their honest reaction to the fact that three local rules can, in principle, support universal computation

The prompt explicitly welcomed agreement, disagreement, and uncertainty.

## Week 8 — Data

### Test Your Data Literacy

**Former source:** `unit8/unit-08-data/Lesson 02 - Reading Charts Without Getting Fooled.html`

Students found a chart in a news article, social feed, advertisement, sports broadcast, or product page. They posted the chart or a link and explained:

- What the chart claimed
- One misleading technique it used, or one such technique it deliberately avoided
- One question they would ask before trusting it

Students then compared their reading with at least one classmate's interpretation.

### Beatles Part 1

**Former source:** `unit8/unit-08-data/Lesson 07 - Cleaning Data.html`

Partners opened a Beatles dataset, checked whether it was clean, brainstormed questions answerable with one column, completed Part 1 of a Colab notebook, shared the notebook by link, and posted it to the discussion.

### Beatles Part 2

**Former source:** `unit8/unit-08-data/Lesson 10 - Choosing a Chart.html`

Partners used the Beatles dataset to brainstorm questions involving two columns, completed Part 2 of the Colab notebook, shared the notebook by link, and posted it to the discussion.

## Week 9 — Artificial Intelligence

### AI, Bias, and Society

**Former source:** `unit9/unit-09-artificial-intelligence/Lesson 10 - AI, Bias, and Society.html`

Students selected an AI system they relied on—a recommendation feed, navigation app, autocomplete, chatbot, photo tagging, or another system—and analyzed:

- Who benefited and profited
- Who could be harmed by errors, bias, or use at scale
- Whether harm was distributed evenly
- Who decided how the system behaved and who was likely absent from that decision

### AI in Your Field

**Former source:** `unit9/unit-09-artificial-intelligence/Lesson 11 - AI in Everyday Life and Careers.html`

Partners compared their majors or possible careers. For each field, they identified two ways AI could genuinely help and one way it could fail or be misused, then posted their comparison together.

## Week 10 — Cybersecurity

### Scams in the Wild

**Former source:** `unit10/unit-10-cybersecurity/Lesson 02 - Scams and Social Engineering.html`

Students examined a scam attempt they or someone they knew had received. They described what it looked like, identified the emotional lever it used—such as urgency, fear, authority, or greed—and considered what it wanted the target to do quickly.

### Privacy: Where Do You Draw the Line?

**Former source:** `unit10/unit-10-cybersecurity/Lesson 07 - Your Privacy and Your Data.html`

Students inspected their phone's privacy or permission settings, found an app with a questionable permission, and optionally revoked it. They then explained where they drew the line between convenience and privacy and described anything surprising they found.
