Part 1: Follow a Web Page

Pick a website you actually use, like a news site, a store, or a game's page. (Pick one whose address you can type, rather than an app.)

1A. Look up its address

Go to Google's free DNS lookup tool, Dig. Type in the site's domain name with nothing in front of it (so example.com, not https://example.com/page), make sure the record type is A, and run it. You're doing by hand the same lookup your browser does every time you visit a site.

  1. Which site did you look up, and what is one IP address that came back?
  2. Did you get one IP address or several? Big sites often hand out more than one. Why might a site want its name to point at more than one address? (Lessons 2 and 4 will help here.)

1B. Trace the trip

Now describe what happens between typing that site's address and seeing the page, as a list of at least five steps in order. For each step, say what it accomplishes and who or what does the work (your browser, a DNS server, your internet provider, the website's server, &c.). Optional vocabulary bank
  • Browser: the client program requesting and displaying the page
  • DNS: the name-lookup system that connects a domain name to records such as an IP address
  • IP address and routing: information and decisions used to move packets toward a destination network
  • ISP: the provider connecting a home or institution to other internet networks
  • TLS/HTTPS: protection for the web exchange while it travels between client and server
  • HTTP request and response: the browser's request for a resource and the server's reply

Part 2: Design a Wordle Protocol

Imagine you're building an online game like Wordle. Each round, the player sends a five-letter guess to the server. The server replies with how many turns the player has left and what it found out about each letter. Your job is to decide exactly what bits go in those messages.

Bit reminder: With n bits, you can make 2n patterns. Find the smallest number of bits that gives you enough patterns for all the possibilities you need.

2A. Send the guess

The English alphabet has 26 letters. If every letter uses the same number of bits, how many bits does it take to send one five-letter guess? Explain how you know your field for each letter is big enough.

2B. Send the result

The server's reply has to include:

  • the number of turns left, which can be anything from 0 through 5; and
  • one of three results for each of the five letters: not in the word, in the word but in a different spot, or in the right spot.

Design a reply. Say what fields it has, in what order, how many bits each one uses, and what each pattern means. Then add up the total number of bits. There's more than one good design here, so explaining your choices counts as much as the total.

2C. Try it out

Now use your design for real. The secret word is PLANT, the player guesses CRANE, and after this guess they have 4 turns left. Write out the server's whole reply as bits, using the format you designed in 2B, and then label which bits belong to which field. (If you find that your design can't actually say something it needs to, that's good to discover! Fix your design in 2B and explain what you changed.)

Part 3: Take Apart a Real Outage

Unit 2 kept coming back to the idea that the internet is built to be spread out, but a lot of it now runs through a few big companies, and when one of them has a bad day, a lot of things break at once. Here are three times that really happened. Choose one, read the short summary, and then skim the company's own explanation. You don't need to understand every word of those write-ups; they're written for engineers. Read them for the main story.

  1. Facebook disappears (October 4, 2021). For about six hours, Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp were unreachable everywhere in the world. During routine maintenance, a command accidentally disconnected the network that links all of Facebook's data centers. Facebook's DNS servers were still running, but they stopped announcing themselves to the rest of the internet, so nobody could even look up where Facebook was. Engineers had to go to the data centers in person to fix it. Read Facebook's explanation, and, if you'd like an outside view, Cloudflare's account of watching it happen.
  2. Amazon's cloud stumbles (October 19–20, 2025). For most of a day, a long list of popular apps and websites that run on Amazon Web Services had errors or stopped working, many of them with no obvious connection to Amazon at all. The trigger was a bug in the automated system that keeps the DNS records for one of Amazon's database services up to date: it left the record for that service empty, so other systems couldn't find it, and the problem spread from there. Read Amazon's summary (the first few paragraphs are the important part).
  3. Cloudflare's error pages (November 18, 2025). For about three hours, people trying to reach a huge number of websites got a Cloudflare error page instead. Cloudflare sits in front of a big share of the web to speed sites up and protect them from attacks. A change to the permissions on one of its databases made a settings file for its bot-detection system double in size, and the software on Cloudflare's servers couldn't handle a file that big. At first the company thought it was under attack. Read Cloudflare's explanation (again, the opening section is enough).

3A. What broke, and how could people tell?

Which outage did you choose? Which piece of the internet from Unit 2 is at the center of it, like DNS, routing, a cloud provider, or a CDN, and how did that one failure end up breaking so many things? Then name one clue about what kept working, or what failed and what didn't, and explain what that clue tells you.

3B. Who was affected, and who could fix it?

Think about the people involved. Who normally benefits from so many services relying on this one company? Who was hurt by the outage, and was the harm spread evenly? (Think about who might have had a backup, and who didn't.) And who actually had the power to fix it, or to make it less likely to happen again?

3C. Could it have been less bad?

Propose two things that could make an outage like this one less damaging next time: one technical idea, and one about how companies, schools, or governments decide what to depend on. For each, explain how it would help and what it might cost. Then say whether your outage makes you think the internet is more fragile, less fragile, or about as fragile as you thought before this unit, and why.
