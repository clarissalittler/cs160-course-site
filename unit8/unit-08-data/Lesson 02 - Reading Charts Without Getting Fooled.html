Lesson 2 - Reading Charts Without Getting Fooled

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what data literacy means and why it matters in everyday life.
  • Recognize five common ways charts mislead: truncated axes, cherry-picked time windows, counts without denominators, dual y-axes, and 3-D pie charts.
  • List the questions worth asking before you trust any chart you see in a feed, a news story, or an ad.

Data literacy is a survival skill

You already know how to read words critically. If a stranger's post says "everyone agrees this is the best restaurant in Portland," some reflex in you asks: everyone? who counted? do they own the restaurant? That reflex is literacy - not just decoding the words, but questioning them.

Data literacy is the same reflex applied to numbers and charts: the ability to read data, understand where it came from, and question what it actually shows. It matters well beyond jobs in analytics, because charts show up every day in feeds, news stories, ads, and policy debates. Somebody made every one of those charts, and that somebody chose what to include, what to leave out, and where the axis starts. Sometimes those choices are reasonable. Sometimes they're the whole point.

And there is a lot of this stuff. For a sense of the sheer volume of data the world generates while you microwave a burrito, check out Domo's Data Never Sleeps 12.0 infographic - millions of messages, searches, and streams per minute. Every chart you meet is somebody's summary of some slice of that flood. The question is always: is it an honest summary?

Check the arithmetic: $500 million divided among 327 million people is about $1.53 each, not $1 million each (PolitiFact's fact-check has the details). Nobody's number sense is automatic, including yours and mine. Checking is a habit, and charts make the habit especially important because the visual impression arrives before the arithmetic does.

The chart-trick catalog

Here are five classics. None of them needs fake data, and that's what makes them dangerous: every one can be built from true numbers and still leave you believing something false.

1. The truncated y-axis

A bar chart works because bar height means amount. Start the y-axis somewhere other than zero and that promise breaks. Say two food carts each sold about a thousand orders last month: Cart A sold 1,000 and Cart B sold 1,020, a 2% difference. Now draw the y-axis starting at 990: Cart A's bar is 10 units tall and Cart B's is 30 units tall. Cart B's bar is three times the height for a 2% difference. Nothing was made up. The chart just cropped away the context. So whenever bar heights look dramatic, first check where the axis starts.

In the wild: In 2012, Fox News showed what would happen to the top income-tax rate if the Bush-era tax cuts expired: it would go from 35% to 39.6%. The bars started at 34% instead of zero, so the second bar looked about five times taller than the first, for a change of 4.6 percentage points. (Storytelling with Data redraws it with a zero baseline, and the dramatic jump mostly disappears.)

2. The cherry-picked time window

A changing line can support very different stories depending on where the chart starts and stops. A gym that charts membership from January 2 to January 31 may show rapid growth; the same line drawn over three years may show a recurring winter bump followed by a spring decline. Same data, different context. When a chart shows a trend, ask: Why this window? What happens if we zoom out or shift the window?

This trick shows up in some very high-stakes arguments. For years, people arguing that global warming had "paused" liked to draw temperature charts that started in 1998. That year was unusually hot because of a strong El Niño, a natural warming swing in the Pacific Ocean. Start a chart at a spike and the years right after it look flat by comparison, even though the long-term climb kept going. Zoom out to several decades and the trend is unmistakable. Same data; the starting point did all the work. (Inside Climate News has a good summary of why researchers rejected the "pause.")

3. Counts without denominators

"25 dog breeds have a maximum life span of 15 years!" Is that a lot? You literally cannot know - 25 out of what? Out of 30 breeds it's a dominant pattern; out of 105 (the actual dataset you'll meet in a few lessons) it's about 24%; out of 10,000 it's a rounding error. A raw count with no denominator isn't telling you what it seems to be telling you. This one is everywhere in the news: "City A had 500 burglaries, City B only 50" sounds damning until you learn City A has 20 times the population - making it the safer city per person. Whenever you see a count, ask for the rate: out of how many?

4. The dual y-axis

Some charts plot two lines with two different y-axes - one scale on the left, another on the right. Because those scales are chosen independently, a designer can make the lines appear to cross, converge, or track each other closely. Plot a company's revenue (left axis, in millions) against complaints (right axis, in dozens), and changing either scale changes the apparent relationship. Two lines moving together on a dual-axis chart prove nothing until both scales have been checked.

In the wild: At a 2015 congressional hearing, a member of Congress presented a chart about Planned Parenthood with two lines, one for cancer screenings and one for abortions, that appeared to cross dramatically. The two lines were drawn on different scales (one in the millions, one in the hundreds of thousands), and neither axis was labeled. The numbers themselves were real, but the crossing was an illusion. PolitiFact rated the chart "Pants on Fire," and a data-visualization expert it interviewed called it deeply misleading.

5. The 3-D pie chart

Pie charts are already hard to read because people compare lengths more accurately than angles. Tilt one into 3-D perspective and the slice toward the viewer is drawn physically larger, so a 20% slice in front can look bigger than a 30% slice in back. So a 3-D effect that's supposed to be decoration changes what the chart appears to say, even when every label is correct.

In the wild: When Steve Jobs presented Apple's share of the U.S. smartphone market in 2008, the slide was a 3-D pie. Apple's 19.5% slice sat at the front, and critics pointed out that it looked bigger than the 21.2% "Other" slice behind it (Daring Fireball collects the back-and-forth). The numbers were right there on the slide. The perspective did the persuading.

Bonus: the upside-down axis

One more, because it's such a famous example. In 2014, a Reuters graphic about gun deaths in Florida, before and after the state's "Stand Your Ground" law, drew its y-axis with 0 at the top and the larger numbers toward the bottom. So when the line went up, deaths were going down, and when deaths rose sharply after 2005, the line appeared to drop. Glance at it the normal way and you'd conclude the opposite of what the data said. (Pacific Standard explains the problem.) Always read the axis labels, not just the shape of the line.

Notice that these examples come from TV news, a congressional hearing, a product launch, and a wire service. Chart crimes don't belong to any one side of any argument! If you want to see lots more, Wikipedia's article on misleading graphs has a whole gallery of them, with explanations of what went wrong in each one.

One more thing to file away

There's a sixth trap, big enough to get its own lesson: two things moving together doesn't mean one causes the other. When we get to scatter plots in Lesson 5, we'll see charts practically begging you to conclude "A causes B" - and we'll work out the full list of other explanations. For now, just let the phrase "correlation is not causation" start rattling around in your head.

The questions to ask

Rolling all of this up, here's your pocket checklist for any chart in the wild:

  • Who made this, and what do they want me to believe?
  • Where does the y-axis start?
  • Why this time window and not a wider one?
  • Counts or rates - and out of how many?
  • Are there two y-axes? (Check both scales.)
  • Is it 3-D or otherwise decorated in ways that distort?
  • Where did the data come from, and what got left out?

A chart that survives all seven questions has earned some trust. A chart that fails one isn't necessarily lying - but now you know exactly where to poke.

This same reflex reaches well past charts. In Unit 10 we'll point it at your whole feed - fake images, doctored quotes, and headlines engineered to be reshared - and build a routine for checking a claim before you pass it along. Misleading charts are one small part of a much bigger world of online persuasion.
