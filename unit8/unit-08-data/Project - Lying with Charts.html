Small Weird Project — Lying with Charts

This project is entirely optional and is not graded. It's here because the best way to learn to spot a misleading chart is to make one yourself. Think of it as training your immune system.

What you'll do: Take a simple dataset and deliberately make the most misleading chart you can. Then explain exactly what makes it misleading. This isn't about being dishonest — it's about understanding the techniques so you can recognize them when someone else uses them on you.

The Dataset

Let's use something simple. Here's quarterly sales data for a fictional Portland food cart:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

quarters = ["Q1 2023", "Q2 2023", "Q3 2023", "Q4 2023", "Q1 2024", "Q2 2024"]
revenue = [4200, 4350, 4500, 4400, 4550, 4600]

Revenue is going up — slowly but steadily. Now let's see how many different stories we can tell with the exact same numbers.

Technique 1: The Truncated Y-Axis

This is the classic. Instead of starting the y-axis at zero, start it at a number just below your lowest data point. A modest 10% increase suddenly looks like the chart is skyrocketing.

# Honest version
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.bar(quarters, revenue)
plt.ylim(0, 5000)
plt.title("Food Cart Revenue (Honest)")
plt.ylabel("Revenue ($)")
plt.show()

# Misleading version
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.bar(quarters, revenue, color="red")
plt.ylim(4100, 4700)
plt.title("REVENUE SURGES TO RECORD HIGHS")
plt.ylabel("Revenue ($)")
plt.show()

Same data. The honest version shows a gentle upward trend. The misleading version makes it look like revenue practically doubled. The only difference? Where the y-axis starts. News outlets do this all the time.

Technique 2: Cherry-Picked Date Range

What if revenue dipped in Q4 2023? If you only show Q3 2023 to Q1 2024, it looks like the business is struggling:

# Show only the dip
selected_quarters = ["Q3 2023", "Q4 2023", "Q1 2024"]
selected_revenue = [4500, 4400, 4550]

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 4))
plt.plot(selected_quarters, selected_revenue, marker='o', color='red', linewidth=2)
plt.ylim(4350, 4550)
plt.title("Revenue Takes a Hit in Late 2023")
plt.ylabel("Revenue ($)")
plt.show()

By choosing which dates to show — and combining it with a truncated y-axis — you can make a healthy business look like it's in trouble. Or vice versa. The person making the chart decides the narrative before the chart even exists.

Technique 3: Dual Y-Axes at Different Scales

Want to make two completely unrelated things look correlated? Put them on the same chart with different y-axis scales and fiddle with the ranges until the lines overlap.

months = ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun"]
ice_cream_sales = [200, 220, 310, 400, 480, 550]
shark_attacks = [1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))

ax1.plot(months, ice_cream_sales, color='blue', marker='o', label='Ice Cream Sales')
ax1.set_ylabel('Ice Cream Sales ($)', color='blue')

ax2 = ax1.twinx()
ax2.plot(months, shark_attacks, color='red', marker='s', label='Shark Attacks')
ax2.set_ylabel('Shark Attacks', color='red')

plt.title("EXPOSED: Ice Cream Linked to Shark Attacks?!")
plt.show()

Both go up in summer — because they're both driven by warm weather and more people being outdoors. But slap them on the same chart and suddenly it looks like ice cream is summoning sharks. This is the "correlation is not causation" problem in visual form, and it's shockingly effective at tricking people.

Technique 4: The 3D Pie Chart

Pie charts are already not great for comparing values. Make them 3D, and perspective distortion means the slices toward the front look bigger than they actually are.

# Flat (honest-ish) version
labels = ["Coffee", "Tacos", "Burritos", "Drinks"]
sizes = [30, 25, 25, 20]

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 6))
plt.pie(sizes, labels=labels, autopct='%1.0f%%')
plt.title("Menu Item Sales (Flat)")
plt.show()

To make a 3D pie chart in matplotlib, you'd use the mpl_toolkits library, but honestly? Just know that any time you see a 3D pie chart in the wild, someone is probably trying to make one slice look more impressive than it is. The front slice always looks bigger due to perspective. Presentation software like PowerPoint makes these trivially easy to create, and they show up in business presentations constantly.

Technique 5: The Scary Title

Sometimes the most misleading thing about a chart isn't the chart itself — it's the title and labels. "Revenue PLUMMETS" when the actual drop is 2%. "Crime SURGES" when the increase is 0.5%. The chart might be technically accurate, but the framing does the lying.

Try it! Take the food cart revenue data (or any data you have from Unit 8's lessons) and make the most misleading chart you can. Use any combination of the techniques above. Then write a short paragraph — even just 3-4 sentences — explaining exactly what you did and why it's misleading. What story does your chart tell, and how does that story differ from what the data actually shows?

Now Spot Them in the Wild

Go find one. Spend five minutes scrolling through a news site, social media, or a political ad. Look for a chart that uses any of these techniques. It won't take long — they're everywhere once you know what to look for. What technique did they use? Is it intentionally misleading, or just lazy design? Share it with a classmate if you find a particularly good (bad?) one.

The Takeaway

Every chart is an argument. The person who made it chose what to show, what to hide, where to start the axes, which dates to include, and how to title it. Those choices shape how you feel about the data before your conscious brain has time to read the actual numbers.

Now that you know the tricks, you can't unsee them. That's the point. You're not learning to lie with data — you're learning to read data honestly, even when the person who made the chart would rather you didn't.
