Notebooks, now with data

You've been working in Colab notebooks since Unit 3, so this part should feel familiar! What's new in this unit is how data scientists use them. A notebook keeps your code, your notes, and the results together in one document, and in this unit the results are mostly charts. So you can look at a chart sitting right next to the code that made it, change one word in the code, and watch the chart change. That's a big part of why notebooks are the standard tool for data analysis. (Colab is Google's hosted version of the popular Jupyter Notebook, if you ever run into that name.)

One habit I'll keep repeating, because it bites everyone at least once: when you open a notebook from a link, make your own copy first (File → Save a copy in Drive), or your changes will vanish when you close the tab.

Pandas

Pandas is the workhorse. It's a free, open-source library for working with data, and we'll use pandas to load CSV files into Python. (A CSV, short for "comma-separated values," is what you get when you export a spreadsheet as plain text.) Pandas turns a CSV into a dataframe, which is basically a table that Python knows how to work with.

It only takes a single line of pandas to read in a whole CSV file. Pandas figures out what type of data is in each column, and then gives you tools for picking out rows and columns, summarizing them, and plotting them. The notebooks handle that setup for you, so you can concentrate on the questions.

The notebook imports pandas near the top with import pandas as pd. The as pd part gives the library a two-letter nickname, so instead of typing pandas.read_csv you get to type pd.read_csv. (Data scientists are not known for their patience with typing.) You don't need to memorize any of the import statements, and there's nothing to install. Dog data

Now let's meet the dataset we'll use for most of the unit. Take a look at the Google Sheet below, which is full of information about dog breeds. Skim the different columns and let your curiosity wander. What questions could we answer with this data?

Try to make your questions specific. "What's interesting about dogs?" is really hard to answer with a spreadsheet. But "do heavier breeds tend to have shorter life spans?" or "which breed group has the most breeds in it?" points right at particular columns. Here's a good test: can you name the column (or two) you'd need to look at? If you can, it's a question the data can speak to. If you can't ("which breed is the friendliest?"), the data might not have anything to say about it, and that's worth noticing too. Remember from Lesson 1 that each row is a whole breed, not one dog, so the data can compare breeds with each other, but it can't tell you how long your particular dog is going to live.

Visualizations

Charts can help answer those questions, but why bother making them at all? Well, start with the raw table below. The cell where the "Max Life Span" column meets the "Australian Shepherd" row tells you exactly one fact: this dataset lists 16 years as the maximum life span for Australian Shepherds. (That's a summary of the breed, by the way, and not a promise about any one Aussie.)

Reading one fact at a time is slow going, and this spreadsheet holds hundreds of them. A chart lets your eyes take in a hundred observations at once, and spot patterns you'd never find scanning cells one by one. That's what the next lesson is about, and we'll start with exactly this column.

Data analysis process

Whatever the dataset, a data analysis tends to go through the same four steps:

  1. Collect or choose the data
  2. Clean the data
  3. Visualize and find patterns
  4. Obtain new information

Those look like a tidy straight line, but in practice you bounce back and forth between them. Collecting or choosing the data means deciding what counts. For us, someone already chose 105 breeds and a set of columns, and every question we ask is limited by those choices. Cleaning is fixing the messes, like a number typed out as a word or two different spellings of the same thing. (Lesson 6 covers this, and it's usually the longest step.) Visualizing is where the charts come in. And obtaining new information means saying, in plain words, what you learned, like "79% of the breeds in this dataset have a maximum life span from 12 to 15 years." Notice we can't turn that into "79% of dogs live 12 to 15 years," because nobody measured how long individual dogs lived.

Why all the bouncing around? Because a chart often shows you a problem you didn't know was there. You make a bar chart, notice a weird bar for a value that shouldn't exist, and realize you're back at step 2. Or the chart answers your question and raises a better one, and you're back at step 1. That doesn't mean you did something wrong! Going back to an earlier step because a chart taught you something is exactly how it's supposed to work.
