Data is already all around us

“Data” sounds like a word that belongs to labs and server rooms. For our purposes, though, data is recorded observations. Any time someone records a fact about the world—a number, a category, a timestamp, a yes-or-no answer—they are making data.

Which means ordinary life generates it constantly:

  • Your phone quietly counts your steps every day. 6,412 on Monday, 11,208 on Saturday (you walked to the food carts and back) - that's data.
  • The coffee shop down the street rings up every sale: what drink, what size, what time, how much. By the end of a week that's hundreds of little recorded facts - data.
  • Spotify Wrapped exists because Spotify wrote down every single song you played all year, then added it all up in December to gently expose you. Also data.

None of these observations is especially informative on its own. “One latte, 8:14 a.m., $5.50” tells us almost nothing. Analysis begins when we collect many related observations and look for patterns—and that's what this unit is about.

Datasets organize related observations

A dataset is a collection of related observations, organized so you can actually ask questions of it. The most common shape for a dataset - and the shape we'll use all unit - is a table:

  • Each row is a thing - one dog breed, one coffee sale, one day of steps.
  • Each column is a fact about those things - a name, a weight, a price, a count.

If that shape rings a bell, it should: it is exactly the list of dictionaries you built in Unit 5. Each row can be represented by one dictionary, and each column can be one of its keys. Everything in this unit rests on that same modeling idea; the supplied tools just work with much larger collections.

Here's a tiny dataset about dog breeds (you'll be seeing a lot more of these dogs - this unit's main dataset has 105 breeds in it):

Breed Breed Group Max Life Span (years) Max Weight (lbs)
Australian Shepherd Herding 16 65.0
Beagle Hound 16 35.0
Great Dane Working 10 190.0
Border Terrier Terrier 14 15.5

Every row is one breed; every column is one fact about that breed. Once data is in this shape, we can ask real questions: Which breeds live longest? Do heavier dogs live shorter lives? How many Herding breeds are there? The same table shape could hold coffee sales or step counts; the organization stays the same even when the topic changes completely.

One aside before we move on: not all data is table-shaped. Photos, voice memos, and the text of email messages are data too, but they are often called unstructured data rather than structured tables like ours. Analyzing them is a field of its own. We'll stick to tables here because they make the basic reasoning visible—and because a great deal of real-world analysis uses them.

Two big kinds of data

Here's the most important idea in this lesson - the one you'll use directly in Week 8 Assignment, so let's take it slowly. Every column in a dataset holds one of two kinds of data:

  • Categorical data (also called qualitative) puts things into groups. Breed names, breed groups, pizza toppings, majors. There's no math to do here - "Herding + Hound" isn't a thing, and there's no meaningful "average breed."
  • Quantitative data is genuinely numeric - amounts and measurements you can do math on. Life spans, weights, prices, step counts. Averages, sums, and "which is bigger?" all make sense.

And quantitative data splits one more time:

  • Discrete data is counted - it comes in separate steps with nothing in between. Number of dogs, number of siblings, number of lattes sold. You can sell 3 lattes or 4 lattes, but never 3.7 lattes.
  • Continuous data is measured - between any two values there's always another possible value. Weight, height, time. A dog can weigh 30 lbs, 30.5 lbs, 30.47 lbs, &c. - it just depends how precise your scale is.

Let's walk through our dog table with this lens:

  • Breed ("Beagle", "Great Dane") - categorical. It's a name, a label. No math possible.
  • Breed Group ("Hound", "Working") - also categorical. It's a group label, even though we can count how many breeds fall into each group (and we will - that count is quantitative, but the group itself isn't).
  • Max Life Span (16, 10, 14 years) - quantitative, and as recorded here it behaves like discrete data: the dataset only uses whole years, so the values come in steps. Time itself is continuous—a dog doesn't live exactly 15.000 years—but once it is rounded to whole years, we are working with discrete values. Data type can depend on how a measurement was recorded, not just on what it measures.
  • Max Weight (65.0, 15.5 lbs) - quantitative and continuous. Any weight in between is possible - the Border Terrier's 15.5 shows the scale doesn't have to land on whole pounds (even if, for most breeds, whoever recorded the data rounded to one).

One trap to watch for: a value made of digits isn't automatically quantitative. The 72 bus, zip code 97214, your student ID - those are numbers used as labels. The test is whether math makes sense: the average of two zip codes is not a place halfway between them, so zip codes are categorical.

Try it: Classify each of these as categorical or quantitative—and if it's quantitative, decide whether it's discrete or continuous:

  1. The number of siblings you have
  2. The bus line you take to campus (the 72, the 20, ...)
  3. The time it takes you to run (or let's be honest, walk) a mile
  4. A T-shirt size (S, M, L, XL)
  5. Your step count for yesterday
  6. Your favorite food cart's cuisine (Thai, tacos, pierogies, ...)
Show answers
  1. Quantitative, discrete. Siblings are counted, and nobody has 2.4 of them.
  2. Categorical! This is the trap. "72" is a label, not an amount - the average of the 72 and the 20 is not the 46 bus.
  3. Quantitative, continuous. Time is measured; between 12 minutes and 13 minutes there are infinitely many possible times.
  4. Categorical. The sizes have an order (S < M < L), but they're still labels - there's no "M + L" and no meaningful average size.
  5. Quantitative, discrete. Steps are counted, one whole step at a time.
  6. Categorical. Delicious, but not numeric.

The kind of data determines what we can sensibly do with it: which summaries mean anything and, as the next lessons show, which chart fits the question. Bar charts work well with categorical data; histograms and scatter plots require quantitative data. A mistaken classification can produce a chart that is technically rendered and conceptually meaningless.

Where Python comes in

Our dog dataset has 105 rows. That's small as datasets go—real ones routinely have thousands or millions—and yet even 105 is too many to inspect reliably by eye. How many breeds have a maximum life span of 15 years? Counting by hand is possible, slow, and easy to get wrong.

Back in Units 5 and 6 you learned the by-hand-in-code way to answer exactly this kind of question: loop over a list of dictionaries with an if inside, adding 1 to a counter each time a breed matches. That still works - and it's essentially what the tools below do for you under the hood. The difference is that pandas will answer the same question in a single line.

This is exactly the kind of tedious, repetitive work computers are for—Unit 4 says hello. In this unit we'll use Python with three libraries that data analysts reach for constantly:

  • pandas - loads a spreadsheet (a CSV file) into Python as a table and lets us slice, count, and summarize it.
  • matplotlib - draws charts: bar charts, histograms, scatter plots, all of it.
  • seaborn - a chart library built on top of matplotlib that makes certain charts easier and prettier. Several of this unit's notebooks use it.

One reassuring detail: the code in this unit is already written. The prepared notebooks ask us to read the code, adjust it—change a column, label, or setting—and, most importantly, interpret the resulting chart. After five units of learning to read Python, we can use that literacy to understand and adapt an existing tool instead of always starting from a blank page.

This corner of computing is called data science: the practice of using computation and statistics to draw justified insights from data. It feeds directly into the next unit because modern AI depends on large datasets. Everything we learn here about what data represents and how it can mislead carries forward.
