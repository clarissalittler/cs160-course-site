Unit 8 Summary

Wrap up

In this unit, you went from "data is a scary word" to actually doing data analysis. You saw that data is just recorded observations - your step count, a coffee shop's sales, 105 dog breeds in a table - and that once it's organized into rows and columns, Python can help you find the patterns hiding in it. You built (well, tweaked and interpreted - which is most of the real job) bar charts, histograms, scatter plots, and crosstab charts in Colab notebooks. And you picked up some self-defense: the catalog of tricks that make misleading charts so effective in the news and your feeds, and the questions to ask before trusting any chart.

Summary

  • Data is recorded observations; a dataset organizes related observations into rows (things) and columns (facts about them). Every column is either categorical (labels and groups) or quantitative (numbers you can do math on) - and quantitative data is either discrete (counted) or continuous (measured). That classification decides which chart makes sense (Lesson 1).
  • Charts built from perfectly true numbers can still mislead: truncated y-axes, cherry-picked time windows, counts with no denominator, dual y-axes, and 3-D pies. Before trusting a chart, ask where the axis starts, why that time window, and "out of how many?" (Lesson 2).
  • We can develop insights and knowledge about our world from manipulating and visualizing data, in particular by finding patterns - and different charts answer different questions, so choosing the right one matters (Lessons 5-10).
  • When investigating two columns of data we can observe patterns that show a relationship (correlation) - but as Lesson 8's ice-cream-and-drowning example showed, a correlation alone can't tell you why: A could cause B, B could cause A, a third thing could cause both, or it could be coincidence.
