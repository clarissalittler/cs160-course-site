Unit 8 Summary

Wrap up

In this unit we went from defining data to analyzing it. Data is recorded observations (step counts, coffee-shop sales, 105 dog breeds in a table), and once those observations are in rows and columns, Python can help us find the patterns. We adjusted and read bar charts, histograms, scatter plots, and crosstab charts in Colab notebooks. Just as important, we practiced the questions that catch a chart built from true numbers giving a false impression.

If there's one thread running through the whole unit, it's that every chart is the result of choices. Somebody chose which breeds to put in the dataset. Somebody decided whether "Jr" and "Junior" were the same thing. Somebody picked 5 bins instead of 10, or started the y-axis at 50 instead of 0, or chose a scatter plot instead of a crosstab. None of those choices is automatically wrong, but each one shapes what you end up seeing. Reading data well mostly means asking what those choices were, and making your own on purpose. That's going to matter even more in Unit 9, where the "choices" include which data an AI system gets trained on.

What to carry forward

  • Data is recorded observations, and a dataset organizes related observations into rows (the things) and columns (facts about each thing). Each column is either categorical (labels and groups) or quantitative (numbers you can do math on), and quantitative data is either discrete (counted) or continuous (measured). Knowing which kind of column you have tells you which chart makes sense (Lesson 1).
  • Always ask who and what is in a dataset. If air-quality sensors are clustered in wealthier neighborhoods, every number can be correct and the sample can still give you the wrong answer about the whole city, and a pattern in a handful of examples is shakier than one in thousands (Lesson 1).
  • A chart built from true numbers can still mislead you, with a truncated y-axis, a cherry-picked time window, counts with no denominator, two y-axes, or a 3-D pie. So before you trust a chart, ask where the axis starts, why that time window, and "out of how many?" (Lesson 2).
  • Manipulating and visualizing data can show you patterns, but different charts answer different questions. That's why choosing the chart (and the bins, and the axes) is part of the analysis and not just decoration (Lessons 4–6).
  • When a column is skewed, the median tells you more about a typical value than the mean does, because the mean gets pulled toward the long tail. Look at the tail too, though, since that's often where the story is, like the smoke days in a month of air-quality readings (Lesson 4).
  • Totals and rates answer different questions. The country with the most total CO2 emissions isn't the one with the most emissions per person, so whenever you see a big number, ask "out of how many, and over what time?" (Lesson 6).
  • When you look at two columns together you might see a relationship between them (a correlation), but as Lesson 5's ice-cream-and-drowning example showed, a correlation alone can't tell you why. A could cause B, B could cause A, a third thing could cause both, or it could just be a coincidence.
