Lesson 10 - Choosing a Chart

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Choose an appropriate chart type for a particular dataset and question.

Chart types

At this point we have a small toolbox of charts: bar charts, histograms, scatter plots, and crosstabs. The notebooks make any of them in a line or two of code, which means the hard part isn't making a chart anymore. It's picking one. A chart that doesn't fit the question won't crash or print an error; it'll just show you something that's hard to read, or worse, something that looks like an answer to a question you didn't ask.

Picking the right one comes down to three things: what question you're asking, what kind of data is in the columns, and whether you're looking at one column or two:

Are you using one column of data?

  • Bar Chart: Bar charts compare a number - a count, a frequency, an average - across discrete categories of data. (How many? What percentage?)
  • Histograms: Like a bar chart, but for numbers grouped into ranges - the height of each bar shows how many values fall into that range. Reach for one when a bar chart would have far too many categories. (What percentage/how many are between this and that?)

Are you trying to show the relationship between two columns of data?

  • Scatter Plot: Scatter plots shine when you have lots of data points and want to see the relationship between two variables (one on the x-axis, one on the y) - a person's weight and height, say. (Are heavier people also taller?) They can also be used to make a range chart when one column is categorical.
  • Crosstab: For counting combinations of values from two columns - at least one column must be categorical data (text), and both need a fairly small number of distinct values. (How many Herding breeds have a max life span of 12 years? Which combination of eye color and hair color is most common?)

Walking through a few questions

Let's try the diagram out on some questions about the dog data, and see how it lands on an answer.

"What's the most common breed group?" That's one column, Breed Group, and it only has a handful of values (Herding, Hound, Toy, &c.). One column, few values: a bar chart, one bar per group.

"Are most breeds small, medium, or large?" Still one column, Max Weight, but now it's a number that could be almost anything from 5 to 200 pounds. One column, many values: a histogram. And you already know the follow-up question from Lesson 6: how many bins, and does the answer change if you pick a different number?

"Do heavier breeds tend to have shorter lives?" Now it's two columns, Max Weight and Max Life Span, and both are numbers. Two columns, no text: a scatter plot, one dot per breed. If the dots drift downward from left to right, that's a pattern to talk about (and, from Lesson 8, a pattern that doesn't tell you why by itself).

"Which breed groups tend to live longest?" Two columns again, Breed Group and Max Life Span. Breed Group is text, and life span only takes about a dozen values, so we can count every combination: a crosstab, which is exactly the chart from Lesson 9.

Notice that the question came first every time. The same dataset gave us four different charts, because we asked four different questions. If you ever catch yourself making a chart and then trying to figure out what it's saying, back up a step and write the question down first.

One last thing. Sometimes more than one chart could work, and that's fine. A histogram of life spans and a bar chart of life spans would both be reasonable, since life span has a smallish number of values. When that happens, pick the one that makes the answer easiest to see, and be ready to say why you picked it.
