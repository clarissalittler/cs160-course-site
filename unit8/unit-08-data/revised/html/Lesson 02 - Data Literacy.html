What is data literacy?

Gartner defines data literacy as the ability to read, write, and communicate data in context. But what does that actually mean?

It means you need to know where the data is coming from (the source), how the data is being structured and analyzed, what the purpose of analyzing the data is — in other words, what problem are you trying to solve? — and what the results mean to the people who care about them (the stakeholders).

That's a lot of moving parts. Let's break down why it matters.

The disadvantages of not being data literate

  • You can't fully utilize the potential of all the data that's lying around.
  • You're losing out to competitors who are leveraging data for decision-making.
  • You end up making wrong assumptions, which leads to wrong decisions.
  • Garbage in, garbage out (GIGO) — a classic computing principle that's painfully relevant here.

The obstacles to data literacy

  • Company culture: Leaders need to model a data-first approach and show that data-driven decisions matter.
  • Insufficient training: Employees aren't trained on data analytics tools.
  • Empowerment: Employees aren't empowered to use data to improve their own work processes.
  • The data itself: Sometimes the data is incorrect, insufficient, or irrelevant — making it useless for analytics.

Types of data

There are two main types of data — structured and unstructured. This distinction matters a lot once you start trying to do things with data.

Structured data

Structured data has a predefined format. It's usually presented in a table with precisely defined fields — rows, columns, everything in its place.

Examples include:

  • Credit card numbers and addresses of customers at a bank
  • Records of purchases at a supermarket
  • Website traffic flow data

If you've ever looked at a spreadsheet, you've seen structured data. It's tidy. It's organized. Computers love it.

Unstructured data

Unstructured data is stored in its native format and doesn't get processed until someone actually needs to use it. There are usually no fixed fields, and the data isn't easily displayed in a table for meaningful comparison.

Examples include:

  • Photos of employees at a company
  • Email messages between a vendor and a customer
  • Songs recorded in MP3 format

Here's the wild part — it's estimated that around 80% of all data in the world is unstructured. That's a massive amount of information sitting around in formats that aren't easy to analyze without some serious tooling.

Data mining

Data mining is the process of extracting valuable information from raw data. The information you're looking for consists of patterns — insights that are useful for business needs, research, &c.

This is a critical component for creating AI models. It's also essential for business intelligence and data science. You can't build a smart system on messy, unexamined data.

Data storage

  • Storing digital files and documents for future use.
  • Storage systems may rely on electromagnetic, optical, or other media methods.
  • Data can be stored locally or in the cloud.
