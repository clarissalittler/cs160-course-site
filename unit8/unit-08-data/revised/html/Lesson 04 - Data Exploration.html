Lesson 4 — Data Exploration

After this lesson, you'll be able to:

  • Explain the difference between quantitative and qualitative data.
  • Understand the meaning of Data Exploration and Quantitative Reasoning.
  • Identify the importance of data analysis and data visualization, and why we need them.

Data types: quantitative vs. qualitative

Let's talk more about data! Data can be quantitative (numbers) or qualitative (categorical) — and quantitative data can be further divided into discrete or continuous.

Discrete data can only take specific values — you can have 3 pets or 4 pets, but not 3.7 pets. Continuous data can take any value within a range — height, weight, time, &c.

Qualitative (categorical) data has no inherent numeric meaning. "Running shoes" isn't greater than "sandals." "Art" isn't less than "Computer Science." These are just categories.

This distinction matters because the type of data you're working with determines what kinds of analysis and visualization make sense.

Except $500 million divided by 327 million people is about $1.53 per person. Not quite the same thing. A major news anchor even repeated this on air before someone did the actual math.

Data exploration

Data exploration is the initial step in data analysis. You're exploring a large dataset in an unstructured way to uncover initial patterns, characteristics, and points of interest.

This process isn't meant to reveal every bit of information a dataset holds. Instead, it helps you create a broad picture of important trends and major points to study in greater detail. Think of it like skimming a book before reading it closely — you're getting the lay of the land.

Data exploration can use a combination of manual methods and automated tools — data visualizations, charts, initial reports, &c.

This process makes deeper analysis easier because it can help target future searches and begin the process of excluding irrelevant data points and dead-end search paths. More importantly, it builds a familiarity with the existing information that makes finding better answers much simpler.

Why does data need to be visualized?

Here's a fun fact about human brains: we're terrible at staring at rows and columns of numbers and drawing conclusions, but we're amazing at spotting visual patterns. Give someone a table with 10,000 rows and they'll glaze over. Show them a chart of the same data and they'll immediately notice trends, outliers, and anomalies.

That's why data visualization forms such an important part of data exploration — it plays to our strengths as visual creatures. The visual cues can include shapes, lines, points, dimensions, and angles, all making patterns jump out at us that would be invisible in a raw spreadsheet.

What can data visualization reveal?

Using data visualization, analysts can:

  • Define the metadata and then perform data cleansing
  • Identify obvious errors in the data
  • Better understand patterns within the data
  • Detect outliers or anomalous events
  • Find interesting relationships among variables

In short, visualization isn't just a nice-to-have — it's how you actually see what your data is telling you. Without it, you're essentially trying to read a novel by looking at individual letters one at a time.
