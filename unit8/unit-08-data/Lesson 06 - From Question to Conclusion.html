Cleaning data

Let's take those problems one at a time, because each one breaks a chart in its own way.

Mixed types. A column with 6 in one row and "six" in another looks harmless to a person. When pandas reads this CSV, that word can cause the column to be inferred as text rather than numbers. A mean or a numeric histogram then won't work as intended until we've interpreted or marked the nonnumeric entries. Counting the raw strings would treat "6" and "six" as different categories. The student still meant six either way; it's just written down in a form the computer can't do math with until somebody fixes it.

Missing values. Empty cells come in as a special "no value here" marker (pandas calls it NaN, short for "not a number"). By default, pandas just skips those when it calculates an average, which sounds convenient, and it is, right up until you forget it's happening. If a quarter of the students left their age blank, the average age is really "the average age of the students who answered," and that might not be the same thing. (Remember the difference between "unknown" and 0 from Unit 5? Here's where it comes back. If someone filled the blanks in with 0, the average would come out wrong in the other direction.)

Invalid entries. "i like food" is a reasonable thing for a person to type into a box labeled "Major," and it's obviously not a major. There's no way to fix this automatically. Someone has to decide: drop the row, mark the major as unknown, or go back and ask. Each of those is a judgment call, so write down which one you made.

Inconsistent spellings. "Junior" and "Jr" mean the same thing to us, but to a computer they're two different strings. So a bar chart of class standing would show two separate bars, each one shorter than the real number of juniors. Nothing crashes, nothing warns you. The chart just undercounts, and it looks completely believable.

Any dataset can have problems like these: missing values, nonsense entries, or two tables that record the same thing two different ways. Finding and fixing those problems before you analyze anything is called cleaning the data, and working data scientists will tell you, usually with a sigh, that it's most of the job.

That last one, combining tables, deserves a word. Suppose the registrar's office records majors as "CS" and the tutoring center records them as "Computer Science". Each table is consistent on its own. Stick them together to ask "do students who use tutoring do better?" and suddenly you have two spellings for the same major, and nobody did anything wrong.

Most of these come down to the same thing: people entered the data, and people are inconsistent. The same person might type "Math" one day and "math" the next. That's not a failing on anyone's part, it's just what happens when free text meets a computer that compares strings character by character, the same thing we ran into with .lower() and .strip() back in Unit 5.

Cleaning usually means picking one standard form and converting everything to it: all lowercase, one spelling for each category, numbers stored as numbers. The important part isn't the code, it's that every one of those choices changes the data, so an honest analysis says what was cleaned and how. "We combined Jr and Junior, and dropped 3 rows with no major" is one sentence, and it lets anyone reading your chart know what they're looking at.

Some of this can be headed off at collection time. If a field really does have a fixed set of choices, a well-labeled dropdown stops "Junior" and "Jr" from both showing up. But a dropdown also shuts out any answer the designer didn't think of, which is the same tension we met in Unit 5: consistency on one side, and an honest way to record "unknown," "unsure," or "none of the above" on the other.

Chart types

At this point we have a small toolbox of charts: bar charts, histograms, scatter plots, and crosstabs. The notebooks make any of them in a line or two of code, which means the hard part isn't making a chart anymore. It's picking one. A chart that doesn't fit the question won't crash or print an error; it'll just show you something that's hard to read, or worse, something that looks like an answer to a question you didn't ask.

Picking the right one comes down to three things: what question you're asking, what kind of data is in the columns, and whether you're looking at one column or two. Lessons 4 and 5 covered what each chart is good for; here's the short version as a decision guide.

Walking through a few questions

Let's try the diagram out on some questions about the dog data, and see how it lands on an answer.

"What's the most common breed group?" That's one column, Breed Group, and it only has a handful of values (Herding, Hound, Toy, &c.). One column, few values: a bar chart, one bar per group.

"Are most breed records small, medium, or large by maximum weight?" Still one column, Max Weight, with values from 7 to 200 pounds in this dataset. For the specific small/medium/large question, first group weights using the stated thresholds (less than 25 lbs, 25–55 lbs, and more than 55 lbs), then make a bar chart of those three counts. If you want to explore the overall distribution instead, use a histogram. Its bin edges need not match those three size categories.

"Do breeds with larger recorded maximum weights tend to have lower recorded maximum life spans?" Now it's two columns, Max Weight and Max Life Span, and both are quantitative. Use a scatter plot, one point per breed record. Several records can overlap at the same point. If the points drift downward from left to right, that's a pattern to talk about (and, from Lesson 5, a pattern that doesn't tell you why by itself).

"Which breed groups have the highest recorded maximum life spans?" Two columns again, Breed Group and Max Life Span. The group labels and the small set of recorded years let us count every combination in a crosstab, which is exactly the crosstab from Lesson 5.

Did you see that the question came first every time? The same dataset gave us four different charts, because we asked four different questions. If you ever catch yourself making a chart and then trying to figure out what it's saying, back up a step and write the question down first.

One last thing. Sometimes more than one chart could work, and that's fine. A histogram of life spans and a bar chart of life spans would both be reasonable, since life span has a smallish number of values. When that happens, pick the one that makes the answer easiest to see, and be ready to say why you picked it.

Data Analysis and Quantitative Reasoning

Quantitative reasoning is the ability to use mathematics and information to solve real-world problems, which is a fancy way of describing what we've been doing all unit. This lesson pulls the pieces together with worked examples: ask a question, pick a chart, say what it shows.

A written example: counts or rates?

Let's work through the six steps from the Week 8 Assignment using the dog-adoption practice file. You can follow the whole example in text; the videos below are optional companions. This file is different from our 105-breed characteristics table. Here each row names a breed and records two counts: dogs available and dogs adopted. We can add those counts within a breed group.

1. Start with a question and a prediction

Which breed group has the highest adoption rate in this practice dataset? Before calculating, I might predict that the group with the most adopted dogs also has the highest rate. That's a testable guess, and it's easy to see why it could fail: a group could have more adoptions simply because more dogs were available.

2. Identify the information needed

We need Breed group, a categorical label, and two quantitative, discrete counts: Total Dogs Available and Number of dogs adopted. For each group, add the available dogs and the adopted dogs. Then calculate 100 * adopted / available. The result is a percentage. Use the sum of the counts rather than an average of each breed's percentage, because the breed rows have different numbers of available dogs.

Before calculating, check that both count columns contain numbers, that availability is positive, and that adoptions do not exceed availability. The supplied sample passes these checks. If availability were zero, the rate would be undefined; we would need to report that instead of dividing by zero or inventing a zero-percent rate.

totals = df.groupby("Breed group")[["Total Dogs Available", "Number of dogs adopted"]].sum()
totals["Adoption rate (%)"] = (
    100 * totals["Number of dogs adopted"] / totals["Total Dogs Available"]
)
print(totals.round(1).to_string())

This code goes after the notebook's data-loading cell. groupby gathers the breed rows that share a group label, sum() adds their counts, and the next assignment calculates one rate per group. Read the result below before making a chart.

3. Choose a representation

A bar chart fits: four categories, each with one numerical rate. Put breed group on one axis and adoption rate (%) on the other, start the numerical axis at zero, and label the bars. A scatter plot would answer a different question, such as whether rows with more available dogs also have more adoptions.

4. Interpret with numbers and units

Herding has the highest rate: 50 of 82 available dogs were adopted, about 61.0%. Terrier has the largest adoption count, 51, but 51 out of 94 is about 54.3%. Toy has the most available dogs, 118, and the lowest rate, 28/118 or about 23.7%. A sentence like "Herding did best" hides the measure; "Herding had the highest adoption rate in this file" says what we actually calculated.

5. Compare the result with the prediction

My prediction was wrong: the highest count and highest rate belong to different groups. The table answers our question about this file, but it doesn't explain the differences. Perhaps groups had different publicity or dogs with different ages and care needs. Perhaps their counts cover different periods or shelters. Those possibilities aren't recorded here.

Grouping by breed does not establish that breed group causes adoption. To investigate an explanation, we would need the collection dates, shelter, time available for adoption, and other relevant information. The practice file doesn't document its collection method or population, so these results are an exercise in reading a sample, not evidence about adoption everywhere. Notice the contrast with the dog-characteristics table: here the percentages really are calculated from counts of dogs, rather than counts of breed records.

6. Ask who could use the result

A shelter might use a carefully collected version of this analysis to decide where to investigate barriers or offer support. Staff, adopters, and animals would be affected by those decisions. A low rate should prompt questions about the adoption process, rather than a label such as "unwanted." We would also ask who supplied the records, how they classified mixed breeds, what counted as available or adopted, and which dogs were left out. This sample cannot answer those questions, so it can't justify an allocation decision on its own.

That's the whole analysis: a question, a prediction, the necessary columns, a fitting chart, a numerical answer, and limits on what the answer means. Python did the adding and dividing for us, but it couldn't have told us to divide by the dogs available instead of reporting raw counts, or that a file with no collection method can't speak for every shelter. Those were our calls.

Totals or per person? An environmental example

The counts-or-rates question from the adoption example shows up in one of the biggest environmental debates there is. Which country emits the most carbon dioxide? If you measure total emissions per year, the answer is China, by a wide margin; it has been the world's largest yearly emitter for well over a decade. But China also has more than four times as many people as the United States. Divide by population, and the average person in the United States is responsible for far more CO2 each year than the average person in China, recently somewhere around one and a half to two times as much.

Neither number is a trick; they answer different questions. "Where are the most emissions coming from?" and "how much does a typical person's life emit?" are both fair things to ask. (There's even a third answer: add up emissions since the 1800s, and the United States has emitted more than any other country.) People tend to pick whichever measure supports their side, so whenever you see an environmental ranking, ask the Lesson 2 question: counts or rates, out of how many, and over what time? Our World in Data lets you switch between all three views of the same data.
