Unit 5 Summary

In this unit we moved from single values to models of the world. Strings, lists, and tuples are all sequences. Tuples keep a fixed group together, dictionaries attach a name to every value, and a list of dictionaries is a small table of records. Loop by loop, the same jobs kept coming back: traverse, transform, filter, count, total, unpack, find an extreme. And at the end we asked a different kind of question about all of it: do the fields and the interface make sense to the people the records describe and the people using the tool?

Then the last lesson turned the question around. Every one of those programs has a single person using it, who can notice a mistake and try again. When a program applies one rule to a whole list of people, a mistake that's rare for any one person becomes certain across thousands, and nobody is watching each case. The Robodebt rule was a division, a comparison, and a loop, and it sent debt notices to people who didn't owe anything, because of an assumption about how income arrives. A scam filter can wrongly flag a message or let a scam slip through, and since those two mistakes land on different people, deciding where to set the filter's threshold means deciding who has to deal with which mistake.

If you look back over the unit, a few ideas kept coming back in different clothes. Immutability showed up with strings, then again with tuples, then again as the reason dictionary keys work at all. "Start with an empty result and build it up" showed up as building a string, then as transforming and filtering a list. And the difference between "nothing" and "zero" came up with None in average(), then again with unknown costs in an event record. Those aren't just Python details, either. Whatever language you program in next, you'll find some version of immutability, building up a result in a loop, and a value that means "nothing here."

What to carry forward

  • Indexes start at 0, negative indexes count backward from the end, and a slice includes its start but stops just before its stop, so "Portland"[0:4] is "Port".
  • Strings can't be changed in place. Methods like .lower() and .strip() hand you back a new string, so hold on to the exact text a person entered whenever its form matters, like the way they spell their own name.
  • Lists are ordered and can change, and an empty list or a missing item is something your program should expect rather than be surprised by. Be careful, too, because two names can refer to the same list, and a change made through one name shows up through the other.
  • Most loops over a collection turn out to be one of a handful of patterns: traversal, transformation, filtering, adding things up, counting, or finding the smallest or largest. Once you can name the pattern, you already know roughly what the loop will look like.
  • A tuple is an ordered group that can't change, which makes it good for small fixed bundles. Unpacking gives each piece its own name, and you'll see it all over the place: functions that return more than one value, enumerate(), zip(), a dictionary's .items(), and rows from a database. And since a tuple of immutable values can't change, it can even serve as a compound dictionary key.
  • A dictionary maps keys to values, and a list of dictionaries is a small table of records with named fields. Use a tuple when the positions are fixed and everyone knows what they mean, and a dictionary when names explain the record better or the fields might change.
  • Write down a schema, or data dictionary, for your records: what each field means, its type and units, whether it's required, and what a missing value looks like. Otherwise the next person to use the data has to guess.
  • Keeping calculations in their own functions, separate from input and display, means you can rerun the same tests, like an empty list or a boundary value, every time you change something.
  • A record is a selective model of a person or situation, not the thing itself, so think about what you're leaving out and what you shouldn't collect at all. Collecting less, errors people can recover from, keyboard access, descriptive labels, predictable navigation, and usability testing are part of whether the tool works, not extra credit.
  • Once one rule runs on hundreds of thousands of people, a rule that's right 99% of the time is wrong for thousands of them. The program only knows the facts someone wrote down, plus whatever assumptions are built into the rule, the way Robodebt's assumption about how income arrives sent debt notices to people who didn't owe anything.
  • A yes/no rule can go wrong in two ways, by wrongly flagging a case or by letting one slip through, and those mistakes usually land on different people, so count them separately and ask who bears each. Before a rule runs on everyone, ask whether people can see why they were flagged, who has to prove what, and whether a person can step in.

Readiness check

Can you create and unpack a tuple, explain a multiple-value return, and say when fixed positions are clearer than named fields? Can you explain why a catalog is a list of dictionary records, state its schema, filter without changing the source, summarize a numeric field, handle zero matches, and spot a field that shouldn't be collected in the first place? And the real test of all of it: can another person complete the intended task from the labels alone? Finally, can you look at a rule that's applied automatically to many people, name an assumption inside it, and say who gets each kind of mistake?
