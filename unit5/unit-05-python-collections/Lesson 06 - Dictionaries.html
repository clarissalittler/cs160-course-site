Lesson 6 - Dictionaries

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Create a dictionary of key/value pairs.
  • Look up, add, and change values by their key.
  • Loop over a dictionary.

Looking things up by name

With a list or a tuple, you find things by position - nums[0], student[1]. But often you'd rather look something up by a meaningful name: a person's age by their name, a word's definition by the word. That's what a dictionary is for. It stores key/value pairs - each key is a label you use to find its value. You write a dictionary with curly braces { }, with key: value pairs:

ages = {"Dahlia": 20, "Sam": 35}

print(ages["Dahlia"])   # 20  - look up a value by its key

It works just like a real dictionary: you don't flip to "page 200," you look up the word you want. Here the keys are names and the values are ages, but keys and values can be many types.

Adding and changing entries

To add a new pair (or change an existing one), just assign to a key. And looping over a dictionary gives you its keys, which you can use to grab each value. Step through this:

A cleaner way to loop when you want both pieces is .items(), which hands you each key and value as a pair you can unpack (that tuple-unpacking trick again!):

ages = {"Dahlia": 20, "Sam": 35, "Mia": 28}

for name, age in ages.items():
    print(f"{name} is {age}")

List or dictionary - which?

Reach for a list when you have an ordered sequence of things and position matters ("the 3rd item"). Reach for a dictionary when you want to look things up by a meaningful key ("the age for Dahlia"). A phone's contacts are a dictionary (look up a number by a name); a to-do list is a list (ordered items). Picking the right container makes a program much easier to write.

Try it! Build a dictionary that maps three friends' names to their favorite food. Look up and print one friend's food by name. Then loop over the whole dictionary with .items() and print everyone's, like "Sam loves tacos".

The fields you choose decide who fits. A dictionary is a set of labeled slots - {"name": ..., "gender": ...} - and quietly, those slots draw a box around what a person is allowed to be. If a form stores gender as a key that only accepts "M" or "F", anyone who is neither simply cannot be represented. If a name field caps out at 20 characters or rejects hyphens and apostrophes, real people's names get mangled or refused. The data structure isn't neutral plumbing - it encodes an assumption about who the software is for. Worth asking: who fits neatly into these fields, who gets left out or forced to misrepresent themselves, and who decided what the options would be?
