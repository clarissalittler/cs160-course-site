Lesson 1 - What Is an Algorithm, Really?

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Define an algorithm and describe what makes one well-formed.
  • Explain why an algorithm is an idea, separate from any person, machine, or programming language that carries it out.

Stepping back

For the last four units we've been writing Python. Now let's ask a question we skipped right past: what is coding, anyway? Underneath the Python, what was the thing we were building?

The answer is we were writing algorithms: step-by-step methods for solving a problem. Part of the key point here is that an algorithm exists on its own. The same algorithm can be carried out by a Python program, by a person with a pencil, or by a machine nobody has invented yet. This unit is about computation itself: what it is, what it can do, and very importantly what it can't.

Algorithms are everywhere

We follow algorithms all day without calling them that. What do these three things have in common?

Chocolate Cream Pie

  1. Heat milk, marshmallows and chocolate in a 3-quart saucepan over low heat, stirring constantly, until chocolate and marshmallows are melted and blended. Refrigerate about 20 minutes, stirring occasionally until the mixture mounds slightly when dropped from a spoon.

  2. Beat whipping cream in a chilled small bowl with an electric mixer on high speed until soft peaks form. Fold the chocolate mixture into whipped cream. Pour into a pie shell. Refrigerate uncovered about 8 hours or until set. Garnish with milk chocolate curls and whipped cream.

Directions to John's House

  1. From the Quik Mart, you should follow Saddle Road for four miles until you reach a stoplight.
  2. Then make a left-hand turn at the stoplight. Now you will be on Hollow Street.
  3. Continue driving on Hollow Street for one mile. You should drive past four blocks until you reach the post office.
  4. Once you are at the post office, turn right onto Jackson Road. Then stay on Jackson for about 10 miles.
  5. Eventually, you will pass the Happy Meadow farm on your right. Just after Happy Meadow, you should turn left onto Brickland Drive. My house is the first house on your left.

How to change your motor oil

  1. Place the oil pan underneath the oil plug of your car.
  2. Unscrew the oil plug.
  3. Drain oil.
  4. Replace the oil plug.
  5. Remove the oil cap from the engine.
  6. Pour in 4 quarts of oil.
  7. Replace the oil cap.

Every one of them is an algorithm: a precise, ordered list of steps that gets you from a starting point to a goal. Not one of them is computer code, and every one of them is a real algorithm. A recipe is an algorithm a cook can run. Directions are an algorithm a driver can run. Notice the pattern: the algorithm is the set of steps, separate from whoever or whatever follows them.

What makes a good algorithm?

If you've ever followed a recipe with a missing step, or directions that said "turn at the big tree," you know that not every list of instructions actually works. For a set of steps to count as a proper algorithm, it needs five things:

  • It's well-ordered. The steps come in a clear sequence, so you always know what to do next.
  • Every step is unambiguous. Each instruction means exactly one thing - no guessing. The basic actions a computer (or person) already knows how to do are called primitives; an algorithm has to be built out of those, not out of vague wishes like "make it taste good."
  • Every step is actually doable. "Divide by zero" or "list every prime number" can't be carried out, so they can't be steps in a real algorithm.
  • It produces a result. The whole point is to end up somewhere - an answer, a sorted list, a baked pie.
  • It halts. It finishes in a finite number of steps. (Hold onto this one! "Does it halt?" turns out to be a thorny question that we'll come back to.)

Let's hold our three examples up against that list, because none of them are quite there like you need from a real algorithm.

The pie recipe says to refrigerate "about 20 minutes, stirring occasionally until the mixture mounds slightly when dropped from a spoon." How often is occasionally? How much is slightly? An experienced cook fills those gaps in without thinking, which is exactly why recipes get away with them. A person who's never made a pie might not. The directions to John's house say "about 10 miles" and "eventually, you will pass the Happy Meadow farm," which works fine if you know what a farm looks like from the road, and not at all if someone painted over the sign. And the oil change skips steps that a mechanic would consider obvious, like "let the engine cool first" and "check the level when you're done."

That's the difference between instructions for people and instructions for computers. People bring common sense, context, and the ability to ask "wait, what did you mean?" A computer brings none of that. It does exactly what the steps say, and if a step is vague, it can't guess. That's why programming languages are so fussy: they're built so that every step means exactly one thing. When Python complained about a missing colon back in Unit 3, it was refusing to guess.

Primitives are worth a second look too. In Python, the primitives are things like print(), arithmetic, comparing two values, and looking up an item in a list. Everything you wrote in the last four units, from the vowel counter to the Tiny Dungeon, was built by stacking those small, precise steps into bigger ones. "Sort this list" isn't a primitive, but "compare these two numbers" and "swap these two items" are, and Lesson 4 will build sorting out of exactly those.

And halting? Think of the old joke about shampoo bottles: "Lather. Rinse. Repeat." Follow that literally and you'll be in the shower forever, because it never says when to stop. Any while loop you've written whose condition never became false did the same thing. For a list of steps that small, it's easy to spot. For a big program it can be very hard to tell whether it will ever finish, and Lesson 5 shows that in general it's impossible.

Try it: Write an algorithm for something ordinary—making toast, brushing your teeth, logging into your email—but write it for a very literal robot with no common sense. Then hand it to a friend and have them follow it exactly as written, doing nothing you didn't say. You'll find out very fast where you were relying on common sense. That gap is what “unambiguous” really means.
