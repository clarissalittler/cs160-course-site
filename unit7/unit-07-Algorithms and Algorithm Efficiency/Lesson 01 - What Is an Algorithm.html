Lesson 1 - What Is an Algorithm, Really?

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Define an algorithm and describe what makes one well-formed.
  • Explain why an algorithm is an idea, separate from any person, machine, or programming language that carries it out.

Stepping back

For the last four units you've been writing Python. But here's a question we've skipped past: what is the thing you were actually writing? Not "Python" - underneath the Python, what was the real object you were building?

The answer is the algorithm: a step-by-step method for solving a problem. And the big idea of this whole unit - the thing worth carrying with you long after you've forgotten any particular bit of syntax - is that an algorithm is an idea that exists on its own. It doesn't belong to Python, or to any computer. The same algorithm can be carried out by a Python program, by a phone, by a person with a pencil, or by a machine that hasn't been invented yet. This unit is about computation itself - what it is, what it can do, and what it can't.

Algorithms are everywhere

You follow algorithms all day without calling them that. What do these three things have in common?

Chocolate Cream Pie

  1. Heat milk, marshmallows and chocolate in a 3-quart saucepan over low heat, stirring constantly, until chocolate and marshmallows are melted and blended. Refrigerate about 20 minutes, stirring occasionally until the mixture mounds slightly when dropped from a spoon.

  2. Beat whipping cream in a chilled small bowl with an electric mixer on high speed until soft peaks form. Fold the chocolate mixture into whipped cream. Pour into a pie shell. Refrigerate uncovered about 8 hours or until set. Garnish with milk chocolate curls and whipped cream.

Directions to John's House

  1. From the Quik Mart, you should follow Saddle Road for four miles until you reach a stoplight.
  2. Then make a left-hand turn at the stoplight. Now you will be on Hollow Street.
  3. Continue driving on Hollow Street for one mile. You should drive past four blocks until you reach the post office.
  4. Once you are at the post office, turn right onto Jackson Road. Then stay on Jackson for about 10 miles.
  5. Eventually, you will pass the Happy Meadow farm on your right. Just after Happy Meadow, you should turn left onto Brickland Drive. My house is the first house on your left.

How to change your motor oil

  1. Place the oil pan underneath the oil plug of your car.
  2. Unscrew the oil plug.
  3. Drain oil.
  4. Replace the oil plug.
  5. Remove the oil cap from the engine.
  6. Pour in 4 quarts of oil.
  7. Replace the oil cap.

Every one of them is an algorithm - a precise, ordered list of steps that takes you from a starting point to a goal. Not one of them is "computer code," yet each is a genuine algorithm. A recipe is an algorithm a cook can run. Directions are an algorithm a driver can run. Notice the pattern: the algorithm is the set of steps, and it's a separate thing from whoever (or whatever) follows them.

What makes a good algorithm?

If you've ever followed a recipe with a missing step, or directions that said "turn at the big tree," you know that not every list of instructions actually works. For a set of steps to count as a proper algorithm, it needs five things:

  • It's well-ordered. The steps come in a clear sequence, so you always know what to do next.
  • Every step is unambiguous. Each instruction means exactly one thing - no guessing. The basic actions a computer (or person) already knows how to do are called primitives; an algorithm has to be built out of those, not out of vague wishes like "make it taste good."
  • Every step is actually doable. "Divide by zero" or "list every prime number" can't be carried out, so they can't be steps in a real algorithm.
  • It produces a result. The whole point is to end up somewhere - an answer, a sorted list, a baked pie.
  • It halts. It finishes in a finite number of steps. (Keep this one in the back of your mind - near the end of the unit, "does it halt?" turns out to be a surprisingly deep question.)

Try it! Write an algorithm for something totally ordinary - making toast, brushing your teeth, logging into your email - but write it for a very literal robot that has no common sense. Then hand it to a friend and have them follow it exactly as written, doing nothing you didn't say. You'll discover just how hard "unambiguous" really is - and that's the whole challenge of computing in a nutshell.
