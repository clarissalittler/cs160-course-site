Lesson 08 - Big-O: Classifying Algorithms

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use worst-case formulas to compute comparisons and copies as list size grows.
  • Explain what Big-O notation means in plain language.
  • Classify algorithms as O(1), O(log n), O(n), or O(n²).

Watching the Numbers Grow

In the last lesson we figured out formulas for the worst-case performance of our sorting algorithms. Now let's put those formulas to work and see what happens as the list gets bigger.

Here are the formulas again. (We've converted swaps to copies by multiplying by 3, so we can compare apples to apples.)

Algorithm Comparisons (worst case) Copies (worst case)
Insertion Sort (n−1) + (n−2) + ... + 1 3 × [(n−1) + (n−2) + ... + 1]
Selection Sort (n−1) + (n−2) + ... + 1 3 × (n−1)

Quick math shortcut: that summation (n−1) + (n−2) + ... + 1 can be rewritten as (n × (n−1)) / 2. That's much easier to plug numbers into, especially when n gets large.

Comparisons as the List Grows

Both sorts have the same formula for comparisons, so they produce identical numbers:

# of Items Insertion Sort Selection Sort
10 45 45
100 4,950 4,950
1,000 499,500 499,500
10,000 49,995,000 49,995,000

Look at that jump. Going from 10 items to 10,000 items—a factor of 1,000—the number of comparisons explodes from 45 to almost 50 million. That's not growing in proportion to the list size. It's growing much faster.

Copies as the List Grows

# of Items Insertion Sort Selection Sort
10 135 27
100 14,850 297
1,000 1,498,500 2,997
10,000 149,985,000 29,997

Now this is where things get dramatic. Insertion Sort's copies are skyrocketing—from 135 all the way to nearly 150 million. Selection Sort's copies, meanwhile, are growing much more gently—from 27 to about 30,000. Why the enormous difference?

Let's look at the formulas. If we simplify Insertion Sort's copy formula using our shortcut:

# of copies = 3 × [(n−1) + (n−2) + ... + 1]

# of copies = 3 × [(n × (n−1)) / 2]

# of copies = (3/2) × [n² − n]

See that n² in there? That's the culprit. When n gets big, the n² term completely dominates. Selection Sort's copy formula is just 3 × (n−1), which only has an n term—no n². That's why it grows so much more slowly.

From Formulas to Categories

Okay, so we've been staring at tables of numbers and formulas with n² terms and n terms. Where is this all going?

Here's the key insight: when we're comparing algorithms, we don't really care about the exact number of operations. We care about how fast that number grows as the input gets bigger.

Think of it like this. Imagine two friends are driving to the same city. One friend's trip takes 2 hours; the other's takes 2 hours and 15 minutes. Not a big difference, right? But now imagine the city were twice as far away. If the first friend's trip takes 4 hours (doubled, as you'd expect) but the second friend's trip takes 9 hours (it quadrupled)—that's a very different situation. The exact times didn't matter as much as the pattern of growth.

That's exactly what we're looking at with algorithms. An algorithm whose work grows proportionally to the input size (n) behaves very differently from one whose work grows proportionally to n². And that difference becomes enormous when the input gets large.

Big-O Notation

This is where Big-O comes in. Big-O notation is a way of describing how fast an algorithm's work grows as the input gets bigger. It's not about exact numbers—it's about the shape of the growth. We look at the formula, find the term that dominates when n is large, and throw away everything else (constants, smaller terms, &c.).

Here are the most common Big-O classes you'll encounter:

Big-O Class Name What It Means Example
O(1) Constant Work stays the same no matter how big the input is Looking up a value in a list by its index
O(log n) Logarithmic Work grows, but very slowly—you can double the input and only add a tiny bit more work Binary Search
O(n) Linear Work grows in direct proportion to the input size Linear Search
O(n²) Quadratic Work grows as the square of the input—double the input, quadruple the work Selection Sort, Insertion Sort

In this graph, the x-axis is n (the number of items), and the y-axis is the amount of work, f(n). You can see at a glance why O(n²) algorithms become impractical for large inputs, while O(log n) algorithms barely break a sweat.

Classifying Our Sorts

So where do our sorting algorithms land?

Both Selection Sort and Insertion Sort have comparison formulas with an n² term as the dominant term. That means both are O(n²)—quadratic algorithms.

That's the bad news. The good news? Plenty of smarter sorting algorithms exist that do much better than O(n²). You might have heard of some of them—Merge Sort, Quick Sort, &c. Most of them use a strategy called "divide and conquer" to cut the work down dramatically.

A Worked Example

Let's practice using our formulas. Suppose we want to know the worst-case total operations for Selection Sort on n = 5 items.

Comparisons = (n × (n−1)) / 2 = (5 × 4) / 2 = 10

Copies = 3 × (n−1) = 3 × 4 = 12

Total operations = 10 + 12 = 22

Not so bad for 5 items. But remember, the comparisons have that n² term. At n = 5,000, comparisons alone would be about 12.5 million. The growth is relentless.

Discussion: Can we do better?

The sorting algorithms we've learned so far—Insertion Sort and Selection Sort—are both quadratic. They work fine for small lists, but they choke on large ones. There are sorting algorithms out there that perform much better. Most of them rely on a strategy called "divide and conquer."

Key Takeaways

  • Big-O notation describes how an algorithm's work grows as the input gets bigger—not the exact number of operations.
  • We classify algorithms by the dominant term in their formula: O(1) is constant, O(log n) is logarithmic, O(n) is linear, O(n²) is quadratic.
  • Both Selection Sort and Insertion Sort are O(n²)—they're fine for small lists but get painful fast.
  • Better sorting algorithms exist (we just haven't studied them yet).
