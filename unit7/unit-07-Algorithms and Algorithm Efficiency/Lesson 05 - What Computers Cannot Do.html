Lesson 5 - What Computers Can't Do

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain, in plain terms, that some problems can't be solved by any algorithm, in any language, on any computer.

This lesson and the next are short, and they are not about memorizing definitions. They offer a glimpse of two deeper ideas in computer science: computation has strict limits, and remarkably simple systems can nevertheless support universal computation.

A different kind of question

So far we've asked, “How fast can a computer do this?” Here is a stranger question: is there anything a computer fundamentally can't do? Not “can't do yet, until computers get faster,” but can't do ever, no matter how powerful they become. The answer is yes, and the result predates electronic computers.

The halting problem

Here's one such impossible task. We'd love a program - call it Halts - that can look at any program and tell us whether that program will eventually finish, or get stuck running forever. This would be incredibly useful! It would catch every infinite loop before it ever ran. It seems like it ought to be possible.

It isn't. And here's the gist of why (this is the idea, not a careful proof). Suppose Halts existed and was always right. Then we could build a little troublemaker program that uses it:

Troublemaker:
    ask Halts: "will Troublemaker (me!) eventually finish?"
    if Halts says "yes, you'll finish":
        loop forever       <-- so... it does NOT finish
    if Halts says "no, you'll run forever":
        stop right now     <-- so... it DOES finish

Read that carefully. Whatever Halts predicts about Troublemaker, Troublemaker is built to do the opposite. If Halts says it will finish, it loops forever; if Halts says it will loop forever, it finishes. So Halts cannot be right about Troublemaker - which means a perfect, always-correct Halts cannot exist. This is called the halting problem, and it was worked out by Alan Turing in 1936 - years before anyone had built a real computer.

Why this is a big deal

Notice what kind of limit this is. It's not that our computers are too slow, our languages too weak, or programmers not clever enough. It's a limit on computation itself. No faster chip, future programming language, or quantum computer removes it. There are well-defined computational problems for which no general algorithm exists.

And this isn't just a brain-teaser. The halting problem is closely related to the fact that you can never write a program that perfectly detects every bug, or every virus, in every other program. Those tools can be good - very good - but "perfect, in all cases" runs straight into this wall. The limits of computation have real, practical fingerprints all over the software you use.

Something to chew on: the "a thing that contradicts itself" flavor of this argument is a cousin of an ancient paradox - "This sentence is false." Is it true or false? Sit with it for a second; you'll feel the same gears grind.

"Can we?" is not the same question as "should we?" This lesson is about what algorithms cannot do - but some of the most important questions are about what they can do and maybe shouldn't. An algorithm can rank every résumé in a stack, score which neighborhoods to police more heavily, or decide which posts a billion people see - fast, tirelessly, and with an air of objectivity that hides the human choices baked into it. Sorting a list of numbers is neutral; a system that sorts people is making value judgments at scale. As computing reaches further into hiring, lending, medicine, and criminal justice, the hard part stops being "is it possible?" and becomes: who benefits when we automate this decision, who is harmed by the mistakes it will inevitably make, and who gets to decide whether it should be automated at all?
