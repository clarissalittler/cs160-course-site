Lesson 6 - Computation From Almost Nothing: Conway's Game of Life

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the rules of Conway's Game of Life.
  • Explain how a few local rules can support patterns capable of universal computation.

Three rules on a grid

Last lesson we saw that computation has limits. This one shows the complementary idea: surprisingly rich computation can emerge from almost nothing. The example is a famous system called Conway's Game of Life, introduced by the mathematician John Conway in 1970.

It's not really a game - there are no players. Picture a giant grid of squares, like graph paper. Each square (a "cell") is either alive or dead. Then time ticks forward in steps, and at each step every cell looks at its eight neighbors and follows three tiny rules:

  • A live cell with 2 or 3 live neighbors stays alive. (Just right.)
  • A live cell with fewer than 2 or more than 3 live neighbors dies. (Loneliness, or overcrowding.)
  • A dead cell with exactly 3 live neighbors becomes alive. (Birth.)

That's the entire thing. Three rules, applied to every cell at the same time, over and over. There is no conventional programming language here and no goal—just cells switching on and off according to their neighbors.

Try the system

The behavior becomes much easier to understand when the rules are run repeatedly. In either simulator below, select some cells as alive and advance the system one step at a time or press play:

  • playgameoflife.com - draw your own, or load famous patterns from the menu.
  • copy.sh/life - a fast one with a big library of patterns to load and watch.

A few things worth trying:

  • Draw three live cells in a row and run it. It's called a "blinker" - it flips back and forth forever.
  • Load a glider - a little five-cell shape that crawls across the grid on its own, step after step.
  • Load a glider gun - a pattern that sits there manufacturing gliders and firing them off forever.

Sit with how strange that is. Nobody told the glider to move. There's no "move" rule - just "live or die based on your neighbors." Movement, and growth, and machinery emerge from the three little rules all on their own.

The punchline: it's a computer

Here is the remarkable part. The Game of Life is not just visually interesting; it is a universal computer—in technical language, it is Turing-complete. That means anything a computer can compute can, in principle, also be computed in Life by arranging cells in the right starting pattern. People have built calculators, digital clocks, prime-number generators, a working game of Tetris, and even the Game of Life running inside the Game of Life.

The optional “Life in Life” video shows that last construction in action. As the view zooms out, remember that every cell is still following only the three local rules above.

This closes the loop on the unit. A computation can be studied in its own right (Lessons 1–2). Its growth can be measured (Lessons 3–4). It has hard limits (Lesson 5). And universal computation can emerge from three rules on a grid—no conventional language or physical processor required—once those rules are allowed to run.

Try it: Spend ten minutes in one of the simulators above. Load a named pattern such as “Gosper glider gun,” “spaceship,” or “puffer,” advance it step by step, and describe what changes and what repeats. The “Life in Life” video is an optional second example.

Complete on your own!

Once you've had your ten minutes of playing and wondering, jot down two things for yourself:

  • A pattern you found interesting. Which one was it (name it if it has a name, or describe what you drew), and what did it do when you pressed play?
  • Your honest reaction to the punchline. These three tiny rules can, in principle, compute anything a computer can. Does that change what you think "computation" even means? "Yes, and here's why," "no, and here's why," and "I'm still not sure I believe it" are all perfectly good answers - what we want is your thinking, not a verdict.

That's the end of Unit 7. We began with the ability to write algorithms and ended with a broader account of computation itself: language-independent, measurable, limited, and universal. The questions only get more interesting from here.
