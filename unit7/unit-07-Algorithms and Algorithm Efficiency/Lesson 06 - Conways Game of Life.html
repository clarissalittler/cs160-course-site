Lesson 6 - Computation From Almost Nothing: Conway's Game of Life

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the rules of Conway's Game of Life.
  • Appreciate the astonishing fact that a few trivial rules can build a full-blown computer.

Three rules on a grid

Last lesson we saw that computation has limits. This one shows the opposite, equally jaw-dropping side: computation can rise up out of almost nothing. The example is a famous little system called Conway's Game of Life, invented by the mathematician John Conway in 1970.

It's not really a game - there are no players. Picture a giant grid of squares, like graph paper. Each square (a "cell") is either alive or dead. Then time ticks forward in steps, and at each step every cell looks at its eight neighbors and follows three tiny rules:

  • A live cell with 2 or 3 live neighbors stays alive. (Just right.)
  • A live cell with fewer than 2 or more than 3 live neighbors dies. (Loneliness, or overcrowding.)
  • A dead cell with exactly 3 live neighbors becomes alive. (Birth.)

That's the entire thing. Three rules, applied to every cell at the same time, over and over. There is no programming language here, no instructions, no goal - just cells switching on and off according to their neighbors.

Now go play with it

You really have to see this to believe it. Open one of these simulators, click some cells to make them alive, and press play:

  • playgameoflife.com - draw your own, or load famous patterns from the menu.
  • copy.sh/life - a fast one with a big library of patterns to load and watch.

A few things worth trying:

  • Draw three live cells in a row and run it. It's called a "blinker" - it flips back and forth forever.
  • Load a glider - a little five-cell shape that crawls across the grid on its own, step after step.
  • Load a glider gun - a pattern that sits there manufacturing gliders and firing them off forever.

Sit with how strange that is. Nobody told the glider to move. There's no "move" rule - just "live or die based on your neighbors." Movement, and growth, and machinery emerge from the three little rules all on their own.

The punchline: it's a computer

Here's the part that should genuinely amaze you. The Game of Life isn't just pretty. It is a universal computer - in the technical jargon, it's "Turing-complete." That means: anything a computer can compute, the Game of Life can compute too, just by arranging cells in the right starting pattern. People have built working calculators inside it, a digital clock, a pattern that prints out the prime numbers, and - because the rules are themselves a kind of computer - even the Game of Life running inside the Game of Life. Folks have built a working game of Tetris out of these cells.

Here it is, and it's worth a couple of minutes of your full attention: "Life in Life" - the Game of Life, built inside the Game of Life. Keep watching as the camera zooms out, and remember: every one of those cells is following only the three little rules above.

Let that close the loop on the whole unit. A computation is a thing in its own right (Lessons 1-2). It has a speed we can measure (Lessons 3-4). It has hard limits (Lesson 5). And it is so fundamental that it comes pouring out of three rules on a sheet of graph paper - no language, no chips, no engineers - the moment you let those rules run. That is what computer science is really about. Welcome to the deep end.

Play and wonder: spend ten minutes in one of the simulators above. Load the fanciest pattern you can find (look for "Gosper glider gun," "spaceship," or "puffer") and just watch it go. Then watch the "Life in Life" video one more time, now that you know what you're looking at.
