Lesson 6 - Computation From Almost Nothing: Conway's Game of Life

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the rules of Conway's Game of Life.
  • Explain how a few local rules can support patterns capable of universal computation.

Three rules on a grid

Last lesson we saw that computation has limits. This one shows the flip side: surprisingly rich computation can come out of almost nothing. The example is a famous system called Conway's Game of Life, invented by the mathematician John Conway in 1970. (Conway reportedly came to resent how famous it made him, since he'd done far deeper mathematics. It really is that good, though.)

It's not really a game - there are no players. Picture a giant grid of squares, like graph paper. Each square (a "cell") is either alive or dead. Then time ticks forward in steps, and at each step every cell looks at its eight neighbors and follows three tiny rules:

  • A live cell with 2 or 3 live neighbors stays alive. (Just right.)
  • A live cell with fewer than 2 or more than 3 live neighbors dies. (Loneliness, or overcrowding.)
  • A dead cell with exactly 3 live neighbors becomes alive. (Birth.)

That's the entire thing. Three rules, applied to every cell at once, over and over. There's no programming language here and no goal, just cells switching on and off based on their neighbors.

Try the system

To see what the rules do, try either a visual simulator or the coordinate trace below. In either simulator, click some cells alive and step forward one tick at a time, or press play:

  • playgameoflife.com - draw your own, or load famous patterns from the menu.
  • copy.sh/life - a fast one with a big library of patterns to load and watch.

Here are a few things to try:

  • Draw three live cells in a row and run it. It's called a "blinker" - it flips back and forth forever.
  • Load a glider - a little five-cell shape that crawls across the grid on its own, step after step.
  • Load a glider gun - a pattern that sits there manufacturing gliders and firing them off forever.

Think about how strange that is. Nobody told the glider to move. There's no "move" rule, only "live or die based on your neighbors." Movement, and growth, and machinery come out of three little rules all by themselves.

A text route: follow a blinker

You can explore the same rules using coordinates instead of a simulator. Choose either route for the activity below. A coordinate (x, y) names one cell: x increases to the right and y increases upward. Its eight neighbors are the surrounding coordinates, including diagonals. Each row below lists every live cell; all other cells are dead.

Check the first change yourself. At generation 0 the center cell has two live neighbors, so it survives. Each end cell has only one, so it dies. The dead cell just above the center, (0, 1), has all three live cells as neighbors, so it is born; the same is true just below, at (0, -1). Every other dead cell has fewer than three live neighbors. All changes use the old generation and happen together.

Now explain generation 1 to generation 2 using the same neighbor counts, and predict generations 3 and 4 before checking the repeating pattern. For another experiment, begin with just (0, 0) alive. It has no live neighbors; what remains one generation later? Compare the two patterns: one repeats, while the other disappears. These simple rules allow for huge variety in behavior. How much variety?

Computing inside the Game of Life

The Game of Life isn't just a neat toy, it's a universal computer; or in the technical phrase, it's Turing-complete! That means anything any computer can compute can, in principle, be computed in Life with a clever choice of the right starting pattern of cells. People have built calculators, digital clocks, prime-number generators, a working game of Tetris, and even the Game of Life running inside the Game of Life.

The “Life in Life” video shows that last construction. As the view zooms out, keep reminding yourself that every single cell is still following only the three rules above. It's optional, but it's a few minutes long and you'll want to see it.

So here's the journey we've taken in this unit. A computation can be studied on its own (Lessons 1–2). Its cost can be measured (Lessons 3–4). It has hard limits (Lesson 5). And universal computation can arise from three rules on a grid, with no language and no processor, as long as the rules are allowed to run.

Try it: Spend ten minutes with either route. In a simulator, load a named pattern such as “Gosper glider gun,” “spaceship,” or “puffer” and advance it step by step. In the text route, verify the blinker's transitions, predict two more generations, and compare it with the isolated cell. Describe what changes and what repeats. The “Life in Life” video is an optional second example.

Complete on your own!

Once you've had your ten minutes of playing and wondering, jot down two things for yourself:

  • A pattern you found interesting. Which one was it (name it if it has a name, or describe what you drew), and what did it do as the generations advanced?
  • Your honest reaction to the punchline. These three tiny rules can, in principle, compute anything a computer can. Does that change what you think "computation" even means? "Yes, and here's why," "no, and here's why," and "I'm still not sure I believe it" are all fine answers, as long as you say what's behind yours.

Want to see how far down "almost nothing" goes? The Week 7 activity, Rule 110 and Friends, shrinks the Game of Life to a single row of cells, where each cell only looks at its two neighbors. There are just 256 possible rules, each named by an 8-bit number, and one of them, Rule 110, is Turing-complete too.
