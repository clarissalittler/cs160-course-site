Unit 1 Summary: One Material, Many Meanings

This unit began with a puzzle: how can one machine be a camera, a map, a library, a music player, a calculator, and a communication device? Not because those experiences are secretly identical—they aren't. It's because people learned to represent each of them as patterns of digital information, and to build layered systems that interpret those patterns.

1. Computers are layered information systems

Every computer performs four broad jobs: input, processing, storage, and output. A component's role depends on what's happening at the moment—a network connection is output when it sends a message and input when it receives one.

Hardware supplies the physical mechanisms: the CPU carries out instructions, RAM holds the active working information, persistent storage keeps files around, and input/output devices connect the system to people and the physical world. Software supplies organized instructions and meaning—applications rely on operating-system services, which rely on device software and hardware. Abstraction lets us work at one layer without juggling every detail below it, while still letting us climb down when we need to diagnose a problem or evaluate a design.

2. Bits create patterns; agreements create meaning

A bit is one choice between two possible states, usually labeled 0 and 1. With n bits we can form 2n patterns. A byte is a common group of eight bits, giving 256 patterns.

Binary place value reads patterns as numbers using powers of two. In 101101₂, the 32, 8, 4, and 1 positions are active, so the value is decimal 45. An unsigned field with n bits that starts at zero maxes out at 2n − 1.

The same bit pattern could just as easily be part of a letter, a color, a sound, a program instruction, or a compressed file. Bits don't carry their interpretation with them; a representation system and its context supply the meaning.

3. Standards let computers exchange language

A character encoding maps written characters to numbers. ASCII is an influential seven-bit encoding covering a limited, English-centered set—it maps A to decimal 65 and binary 01000001. Unicode provides a much larger shared repertoire: a code point identifies an abstract character, and an encoding such as UTF-8 specifies the bytes used to store it.

One visible, user-perceived character may contain several code points, and a font selects the visual glyph. Those distinctions matter whenever software counts, searches, displays, or deletes text. Character standards are technical agreements—but deciding what they represent, and how well systems implement them, shapes language access and participation.

4. Sampling and compression make digital media practical

Digital images sample space into pixels. A simple black-and-white pixel may use one bit; an RGB pixel commonly uses three eight-bit channel values ranging from 0 to 255. Digital audio samples a changing signal across time: sample rate says how often measurements are taken, bit depth says how many levels each measurement can use, and channels store simultaneous signals.

More samples and more bits per sample usually preserve finer distinctions—and cost more space. Metadata, such as an image's width, height, and color system, tells software how to interpret the data, and can carry privacy-relevant details along for the ride.

Lossless compression finds a shorter representation from which the original can be rebuilt exactly. Lossy compression discards selected information to get a smaller file. Which is right depends on what must survive: exact program code and master files generally want lossless storage, while a carefully compressed photograph or spoken-word recording may improve access over a limited connection. Smaller isn't automatically better, and bigger isn't automatically more useful.

5. The ability to copy is not the same as permission

Copyright protects original human expression once it is fixed in a lasting form. It does not protect ideas, facts, systems, or methods as such. Owners receive a limited bundle of rights, though ownership can be affected by employment and contracts, and the rights are subject to exceptions.

Common paths for reuse include the public domain, an explicit license or individual permission, and a legal exception such as fair use. Fair use requires a contextual four-factor analysis—education alone is not an automatic exemption. Creative Commons licenses grant standardized permission in advance through the elements BY, SA, NC, and ND. Good attribution names the work, creator, source, license, and any changes you made.

Copyright is not the same thing as plagiarism, privacy, trademark, or a platform's terms. The dance-emote case shows that even when a small movement is not protected by copyright, questions about credit, compensation, culture, and power remain.

Generative AI adds separate questions about acquiring training data, using copies during training, generating recognizable expression, and determining human authorship of outputs. AI training is not automatically fair use and not automatically infringement—purpose, the kinds and sources of works, the amount copied, output controls, and market effects all matter. A tool's ability to generate or copy something does not answer whether a particular use is legal, permitted by a course or employer, or responsible.

The thread connecting the unit

At every stage, computing involves both a physical or mathematical mechanism and a human agreement—and those agreements become part of daily life:

Ask all three questions

Who benefits? A system often creates real value: convenience, access, expression, knowledge. Who might be harmed or left out? Benefits and costs can land on different people. Who decides? The person clicking a button is operating inside choices already made by designers, institutions, markets, and law.

A strong analysis avoids two easy extremes—“technology is neutral, so impacts aren't our problem” and “a harmful outcome means the technology has no value.” It explains the mechanism, names the tradeoff, and asks how power and responsibility are distributed.

  • Trace one familiar action through input, processing, storage, output, hardware, and software.
  • Explain why six bits provide 64 patterns and why an unsigned six-bit number has a maximum value of 63.
  • Explain how the same bits can mean 65, the letter A, or part of another kind of data.
  • Describe how resolution or sample rate affects both detail and file size.
  • Choose lossless or lossy compression for a scenario and defend the choice.
  • Explain why finding an image online does not by itself give permission to republish it.
  • Apply the recurring lens to one example: identify who benefits, who might be harmed or excluded, and who decides.
