Unit 1 Summary: One Material, Many Meanings

This unit began with a puzzle: how can one machine be a camera, a jukebox, and a game console? Those things aren't secretly identical. What they share is that people figured out how to represent each of them as patterns of digital information, and then built layered systems to interpret those patterns.

1. Computers as layered systems

Every computer does four broad jobs: input, processing, storage, and output. Which job a part is doing depends on the moment, so a network connection is output when it sends a message and input when it receives one.

Hardware is the physical side: the CPU carries out instructions, RAM holds whatever the computer is working on right now, persistent storage keeps files around, and input/output devices connect the system to people and the physical world. Software is the organized instructions that give all of that meaning, and it comes in layers too, with applications relying on operating-system services, which rely on device software and hardware. That's what abstraction gets you: you can work at one layer without juggling every detail below it, and you can still climb down a layer when you need to figure out what went wrong or judge whether a design makes sense.

2. Bits, patterns, and meaning

A bit is one choice between two possible states, usually written 0 and 1. With n bits you can make 2n different patterns, so a byte, the common group of eight bits, gives you 256 of them.

To read a pattern as a number, binary uses place values that are powers of two. In 101101₂, the 32, 8, 4, and 1 positions are on, so the value is decimal 45. That's also why an unsigned field with n bits that starts at zero tops out at 2n − 1: one of the 2n patterns gets used up on zero.

So what does 01000001 mean? It depends on how you read it. As a binary number it's 65, as ASCII text it's A, and it could just as easily be one color channel of a pixel. The bits don't carry their interpretation around with them, so it's the representation system and the context that say how to read them.

3. Text and character standards

A character encoding is an agreement that maps written characters to numbers. ASCII is an influential seven-bit encoding covering a limited, English-centered set of characters, and it maps A to decimal 65 and binary 01000001. Unicode is a much larger shared repertoire, and it splits the job in two: a code point identifies an abstract character, and an encoding such as UTF-8 says which bytes are used to store it.

Text gets more complicated from there. One character, as a person sees it, may be made of several code points, and the font picks the visual glyph you see on screen. Those distinctions matter whenever software counts, searches, displays, or deletes text. And while character standards are technical agreements, deciding what they represent, and how well systems implement them, shapes whose languages and names work well on a computer.

4. Images and sound as samples

A digital image samples space into pixels. A simple black-and-white pixel may use one bit, while an RGB pixel commonly uses three eight-bit channel values ranging from 0 to 255. Digital audio samples a changing signal across time instead: the sample rate says how often a measurement is taken, the bit depth says how many levels each measurement can use, and the channels are separate signals stored at the same time, like the left and right sides of a stereo recording.

More samples and more bits per sample usually preserve finer distinctions, and they cost more space, so someone always has to decide how much detail is enough. Metadata, such as an image's width, height, and color system, tells software how to interpret the data, and it can carry privacy-relevant details along for the ride.

5. Lossless and lossy compression

Lossless compression finds a shorter way to write the data down, one the original can be rebuilt from exactly. Lossy compression throws away selected information to get a smaller file. So which one should you use? It depends on what has to survive. Program code and master files generally need to come back exactly, so they want lossless storage, while a carefully compressed photograph or spoken-word recording can lose a little detail and still be much easier to get to someone over a limited connection.

6. Copying, copyright, and permission

Copyright protects original human expression once it's fixed in a lasting form. It doesn't protect ideas, facts, systems, or methods as such. Owners get a limited bundle of rights, though who owns a work can be affected by employment and contracts, and the rights come with exceptions.

So how can you reuse someone else's work? The common paths are the public domain, an explicit license or individual permission, and a legal exception such as fair use. Fair use takes a four-factor analysis of the specific situation, and being for education doesn't make something fair use automatically. Creative Commons licenses grant standardized permission in advance through the elements BY, SA, NC, and ND. And when you do reuse something, good attribution names the work, the creator, the source, the license, and any changes you made.

Copyright also isn't the same thing as plagiarism, privacy, trademark, or a platform's terms. The dance-emote case shows why that matters: even when a small movement isn't protected by copyright, the questions about credit, compensation, culture, and power are all still there.

Generative AI raises its own separate questions: how the training data was acquired, what copies get used during training, whether outputs reproduce recognizable expression, and whether there's a human author for an output at all. AI training isn't automatically fair use, and it isn't automatically infringement either. The purpose, the kinds and sources of works, the amount copied, the controls on outputs, and the effects on the market all matter. And just because a tool can generate or copy something doesn't tell you whether a particular use is legal, allowed by your course or employer, or responsible.

The three questions

Who benefits? A system often creates real value: convenience, access, expression, knowledge. Who might be harmed or left out? Benefits and costs can land on different people. Who decides? The person clicking a button is operating inside choices already made by designers, institutions, markets, and law.

Why ask all three? Because it's easy to fall into one of two traps. One says “technology is neutral, so impacts aren't our problem.” The other says “a harmful outcome means the technology has no value.” Neither one helps you understand what happened. Explaining the mechanism, naming the tradeoff, and asking how power and responsibility are distributed gets you much further.
