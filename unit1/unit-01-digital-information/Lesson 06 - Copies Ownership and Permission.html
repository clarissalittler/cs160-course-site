Lesson 6: Copies, Ownership, and Permission

Big question

Digital information can be copied almost perfectly, quickly, and at very low cost. What are we allowed to copy, adapt, publish, and build upon?

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to tell copyright ownership apart from permission to use a work; identify public-domain, licensed, and fair-use pathways; interpret the elements of a Creative Commons license; explain why legal permission and social responsibility can point in different directions; and explain why generative-AI training requires a fact-specific fair-use analysis rather than a universal answer.

Scope: This lesson is a general introduction centered on United States copyright. It is not legal advice. Laws differ by country, facts matter, and institutional policies or contracts may add requirements.

The superpower and problem of digital copying

Copy an analog recording over and over and quality may decline—tape hiss piles up, photocopies blur. Copy a digital file and the new bit pattern can be identical to the old one, down to the last bit. Add networks, and those copies can reach a worldwide audience almost instantly.

That ability supports backups, open education, libraries, collaboration, accessibility, and creative remix. It can also separate a work from its creator, erase context, violate privacy, or interfere with how a creator is paid. Computing gives us the capability to copy; law, licenses, ethics, and community norms are how we reason about when and how to use it.

Copyright begins with authorship and fixation

In the United States, copyright protects an original work of human authorship once it is fixed in a sufficiently lasting form. Writing a poem, taking a photograph, recording a song, drawing an illustration, saving original program code—any of these can create a copyrighted work. Copyright normally arises automatically; a copyright symbol or registration is not required for protection to begin, although registration provides important legal benefits.

Copyright protects a creator's particular expression, not an underlying idea, fact, discovery, procedure, method, system, or process. You may write your own story about star-crossed lovers; you may not copy someone else's distinctive wording. You may use a documented algorithm; copying a particular software implementation can raise a different question.

The U.S. Copyright Office introduction explains originality, fixation, protected works, ownership, exclusive rights, exceptions, and the public domain.

What ownership controls

A copyright owner receives a bundle of exclusive rights, subject to limitations. These generally include rights to:

  • reproduce the work;
  • prepare adaptations or derivative works;
  • distribute copies;
  • publicly perform certain works; and
  • publicly display certain works.

The human creator is often the first owner, but not always the current owner. Work created by an employee within the scope of employment may be a work made for hire, and rights can also be transferred by contract or inherited. Owning a physical object or file does not necessarily include owning its copyright: buying a painting lets you possess that canvas, but it does not automatically give you the right to sell prints.

A license does not necessarily transfer ownership—it gives permission to exercise specified rights under specified conditions. When you install software, stream a film, use a stock photograph, or post to a platform, a license or terms of service may define what the provider and user can do.

Four common paths for using someone else's work

  1. Use material not protected by copyright. Facts, ideas, and methods are not copyrighted, though a particular presentation of them may be. You should still cite sources when academic or professional practice requires it.
  2. Use a public-domain work. Copyright may have expired, may never have applied, or the owner may have dedicated rights to the public. Verify the status rather than relying on the work's age or where you found it.
  3. Follow a license or seek permission. A license may already authorize the use; otherwise, the owner can grant permission. Read the actual terms, including attribution, adaptation, commercial-use, and redistribution limits.
  4. Rely on an exception or limitation. U.S. law includes fair use and other specific exceptions. These depend on circumstances; “educational” is not a blanket exemption.

“I found it online” is not a fifth path. Publicly viewable does not mean public domain, and the absence of a copyright notice does not mean permission. Search results point to files; they do not determine legal status.

Fair use is a contextual analysis

Fair use permits some uses of copyrighted works without permission. Criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research are examples of purposes that may qualify—but the label alone does not decide the result. U.S. law calls for four factors to be weighed together:

  1. Purpose and character of the use. Is the use commercial or nonprofit? Does it add a genuinely different purpose or character, or merely substitute for the original?
  2. Nature of the copyrighted work. Is the source highly creative or more factual? Is it published or unpublished?
  3. Amount and substantiality. How much was used, and did the use take the work's qualitative “heart”? Sometimes an entire work can be fair; sometimes a small but central portion weighs against fairness.
  4. Effect on the potential market. Does the use replace demand for the original or a market the owner would ordinarily serve?

There is no automatic safe percentage, number of seconds, or word count—courts evaluate specific facts. The Copyright Office Fair Use Index explains the factors and collects court decisions, but it also warns that the index is not a substitute for legal advice.

Creative Commons: permission stated in advance

Creative Commons (CC) provides standardized public copyright licenses. A creator who owns the relevant rights can use a CC license to tell everyone, in advance, which reuse is allowed—no separate negotiation with each user required.

Four abbreviations combine into six main licenses:

  • BY — Attribution: give appropriate credit.
  • SA — ShareAlike: license adaptations under the same or a compatible license.
  • NC — NonCommercial: use only for purposes not primarily intended for commercial advantage or monetary compensation.
  • ND — NoDerivatives: distribute only unadapted material.
The six main Creative Commons license combinations

CC0 is different from the six licenses. It is a tool for a rights holder to waive copyright and related rights as fully as the law allows, placing a work as close as possible to the public domain worldwide.

The Creative Commons license guide provides the current deeds and fuller terms. When applying a license to your own work, make sure you actually control the rights you're licensing: a CC license does not grant permission for copyrighted material by other people that you included without authority.

Give useful attribution

A practical attribution identifies the title, author, source, and license, with links when possible, and says whether you changed the work. For example:

Good attribution isn't just a legal ritual. It lets an audience find the source, understand what was changed, and follow a chain of creative contribution. And take it from me: record the source and license information when you download an asset, because reconstructing it at the end of a project is miserable.

Copyright, plagiarism, privacy, and platform rules are different questions

Several distinct issues get collapsed into the word “copyright,” and they are not interchangeable:

  • Copyright asks whether protected expression may be copied, adapted, distributed, performed, or displayed.
  • Plagiarism is presenting someone else's contribution as your own. It is an ethical and academic-integrity issue. Copying a public-domain paragraph without credit may not infringe copyright, but it can still be plagiarism.
  • Privacy and publicity concern personal information, images, identity, or likeness. Owning copyright in a photograph does not settle every question about the person pictured.
  • Contracts and platform rules may restrict behavior that copyright law would otherwise permit. A site's terms of service, an employment agreement, or a course policy is a separate layer of obligations.
  • Trademark and patent protect different interests, such as source-identifying brands and certain inventions. They are not alternative names for copyright.

A responsible reuse decision may require asking all of these questions—not stopping the moment you find one license.

Case study: when a dance becomes a digital product

Imagine a musician or dancer creates a short dance, shares it in a video, and watches it spread through a community. Then a game company records or reconstructs the movements as an animated “emote,” gives it a new name, and sells it to players—without naming or paying the person who made it popular.

Technically, this is another representation problem. Motion across time gets sampled into positions, rotations, or keyframes. Once it's data, the animation can be copied exactly and attached to any game character. But a dance carries human context the keyframes don't: who developed it, which music and community gave it meaning, and who had enough organizational power to turn it into a product.

The legal answer can be narrower than the ethical one. U.S. copyright can protect an original choreographic work that is fixed in a lasting form, but ordinary movements, individual steps, commonplace gestures, and some social dances may not contain enough protectable authorship. The Copyright Office guide to choreography explains that distinction. Whether a particular sequence qualifies depends on its specific facts.

Suppose the brief move is not protected by copyright. It does not follow that using it without credit is socially harmless. A company may gain sales and cultural relevance while the creator loses recognition, context, or a chance to negotiate. At the same time—and reasonable people wrestle with this—dance often develops communally, and giving one person absolute ownership over a shared practice can also erase contributors and restrict participation. Copyright answers some questions about legal control; it does not automatically answer questions about attribution, compensation, cultural appropriation, or respect.

Use the recurring lens

  • Who benefits? The company earns revenue, players gain a form of expression, and wider circulation may introduce people to a dance—if its origin remains visible.
  • Who might be harmed? A creator or community may lose credit, income, context, or control over how the dance is presented.
  • Who decides? Platform designers choose what to sell and credit; creators may negotiate or protest; copyright law sets a minimum boundary; players decide what practices they reward.

Generative AI raises several different copyright questions

Generative AI systems complicate this landscape because their outputs arise from interactions among training materials, model design, human prompts, selection, revision, and arrangement. “AI and copyright” is not one question—at least three stages need to be pulled apart:

  1. Acquisition: How did the developer obtain books, images, recordings, code, or other training material? Was it licensed, public domain, openly available under relevant terms, purchased, scraped, or downloaded from a pirate source?
  2. Training: Copies are processed so a model can adjust numerical parameters and learn statistical relationships. Is that use sufficiently different from the works' original purpose, and what safeguards limit memorization or reproduction?
  3. Output and deployment: What can users generate? Does the system reproduce recognizable expression, imitate a creator on demand, or compete in the same market as the works used to build it?

A conclusion about one stage does not automatically decide the others. A court could view a particular training process as fair while treating the creation of a permanent library from pirated books differently. A model may also be trained lawfully yet produce an infringing output in response to a particular request.

Is AI training fair use?

The honest answer in the United States is: sometimes it may be, sometimes it may not be, and “training” alone does not decide. Apply the same four factors, but ask questions specific to the system:

Applying the fair-use factors to an AI-training claim
Fair-use factorQuestions about AI training
Purpose and characterIs the model used for noncommercial analysis or for commercial generation? Does it enable a new purpose, or produce substitutes serving much the same purpose as the source works?
Nature of the worksAre the sources factual or functional materials, or highly expressive novels, songs, illustrations, and photographs? Were they published?
Amount and substantialityWere entire works copied? Was that amount reasonably connected to the claimed new purpose? Does the model retain or reveal protected expression?
Market effectCan outputs substitute for the originals or for commissioned work? Is there an established or developing licensing market for training? Could widespread similar use change a creator's ability to earn a living?

Source and system design matter too. Lawful access is not identical to fair use, but deliberately building a permanent library from pirate sources presents a different case from analyzing licensed or public-domain material. Output controls that reduce verbatim reproduction can also change the facts.

In its 2025 report on generative-AI training, the U.S. Copyright Office described a spectrum. Noncommercial research or analysis that does not enable reproduction of the works is more likely to be fair. Copying expressive works from pirate sources to generate unrestricted commercial content that competes with those works is less likely to be fair. Many actual systems fall between those endpoints.

Federal trial courts have likewise emphasized specific facts rather than announcing that all AI training is lawful or unlawful. In Bartz v. Anthropic, a 2025 order treated the training use as fair but did not excuse a separately maintained library of pirated books. In Kadrey v. Meta, the court ruled for Meta on the evidence presented while explicitly warning that the decision did not establish that Meta's use of copyrighted material was lawful in every case. These are trial-court decisions involving particular records, not a universal rule for every model or future case.

Why reasonable people disagree

  • Creators and rightsholders may argue that their work was taken without meaningful consent, attribution, or payment and then used to create systems that compete for their audiences and jobs.
  • Developers, researchers, and users may argue that learning statistical relationships is a new use that supports research, accessibility, education, and creative tools rather than distributing the original works.
  • Small developers and the public may worry that expensive work-by-work licensing would leave only the largest technology companies able to build models. Others respond that innovation should not be subsidized by uncompensated creators and that collective or revenue-sharing licenses are possible.
  • Communities represented in the data may benefit when a model supports their language or history, but they may also be stereotyped, exposed, or represented without authority. Copyright ownership does not capture every collective or cultural interest.

These disagreements mix legal, economic, and moral claims. A court's fair-use ruling establishes a legal boundary for a case; it does not by itself decide what an ethical product, fair labor arrangement, or healthy creative culture should look like.

Use the recurring lens again

  • Who benefits? Model companies, researchers, users, and organizations that can automate or expand services may gain capability and income.
  • Who might be harmed? Creators may lose licensing income, commissions, attribution, privacy, or bargaining power; workers may face changes imposed without participation.
  • Who decides? Developers choose sources and safeguards; users choose outputs; creators negotiate or challenge uses; courts apply existing law; legislatures and standards bodies can create new rules.

Who owns an AI-assisted output?

For copyrightability of an output in the United States, human authorship remains central. The Copyright Office's current position is that material generated entirely by a machine is not protected by copyright merely because a person supplied prompts. Human-authored expression in a larger work can still be protected—for example, a person's original writing, sufficiently creative modifications, or selection and arrangement—even when unprotectable generated material is also present. The analysis depends on what a person actually contributed, not on whether a particular tool was used.

Voice and likeness introduce still more issues. A cloned voice used in a family-emergency scam or a synthetic video of a public figure may cause serious harm even when copyright is not the main legal tool. Privacy, publicity rights, fraud, labor agreements, and emerging digital-replica laws may matter.

The U.S. Copyright Office AI initiative publishes its reports on digital replicas, copyrightability, and generative-AI training. Because law and policy are still developing, check current authoritative guidance rather than treating one platform's confident answer as settled law.

Even when an output can legally be used, a course, employer, publication, or contest may require disclosure or prohibit particular uses. An output may reproduce bias, expose private data, imitate a living artist, or contain a recognizable fragment from training material. “The tool produced it” does not eliminate the user's responsibility to inspect, document, and make choices.

Try it: choose a defensible path

  1. You find a photograph on a public blog with no license information. May you assume it is free to republish because anyone can view it?
  2. You adapt a CC BY-SA illustration for a public project. What two central obligations follow from the license?
  3. You quote part of a novel in an essay that analyzes the author's language. What doctrine might apply, and what must you do before claiming it definitely applies?
  4. An AI image service says you may use its output commercially. Name two questions that its terms alone do not settle.
  5. A company turns a community-created dance into a paid game emote without credit. Suppose the move is too short to receive copyright protection. What ethical questions remain?
  6. Compare two training projects: a nonprofit studies licensed historical newspapers to identify changing language, while a commercial generator downloads pirated novels and markets “new books in the style of” living authors. Which fair-use facts differ?
Check your reasoning
  1. No. Public access is not the same as public domain or licensed reuse. Locate the owner and terms, seek permission, choose an openly licensed substitute, or determine whether a specific exception applies.
  2. Credit the creator appropriately and license your adaptation under the same or a compatible license.
  3. Fair use may apply. Analyze all four factors in the actual context; “criticism” or “education” does not guarantee the result.
  4. Possible answers include whether the output contains protectable human authorship; whether it resembles protected source expression; whether people depicted have privacy or publicity interests; whether course or employer policy permits it; and whether using it without disclosure is ethical.
  5. Credit, compensation, cultural context, unequal bargaining power, communal contribution, and who profits still matter even if copyright supplies no claim.
  6. The projects differ in purpose, commerciality, source and permission, kinds of works, output controls, and likely market substitution. Those facts point in different directions, but a real court would still need a complete case-specific record.

A reusable workflow

  1. Identify the work, creator, original source, and any people or private information represented.
  2. Check status: public domain, explicit license, permission, or a carefully analyzed exception.
  3. Read conditions, including attribution, adaptation, commercial-use, ShareAlike, disclosure, and platform requirements.
  4. Document the source, license version, link, date, and your modifications.
  5. Consider impact beyond minimum legal permission: privacy, accessibility, context, compensation, and the expectations of the community involved.

Lesson takeaway

Digital copying is technically easy; permission is contextual. Copyright protects original human expression fixed in a lasting form and gives owners a limited bundle of rights. Public domain, licenses, permission, and legal exceptions offer different reuse paths, and Creative Commons makes some permissions legible in advance. Fair use requires a fact-specific analysis, and generative AI adds authorship, training, policy, and ethical questions rather than making those questions disappear.
