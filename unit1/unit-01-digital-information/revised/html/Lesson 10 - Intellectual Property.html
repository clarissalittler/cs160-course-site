Lesson 10 — Intellectual Property

After this lesson, you'll be able to:

  • Define "intellectual property"
  • Understand the basic rules of copyright
  • Explain when you can (and can't) reuse or remix digital information

A Thought Experiment

Imagine you spent hours using our pixelation tools to create an image — something you're genuinely proud of — and you post it online for your friends to see. A week later, you discover that someone took your image, slapped it on a T-shirt, and is selling it for $10 a pop.

How would that feel?

Probably... not great. You made that thing. You put in the work. And now someone else is profiting from it without asking you, without crediting you, without sharing any of the money. That uncomfortable feeling? That's the emotional core of why intellectual property law exists.

What Is Intellectual Property?

When you create something — an image, a piece of writing, a song, a line of code — you own it. It's your intellectual property. This is true whether you register it with anyone or not. The moment you create an original work and fix it in some tangible form (write it down, save the file, &c.), you automatically have copyright over it.

Using someone else's creative work and trying to pass it off as your own? That's plagiarism. Doing it without the creator's permission can also be illegal — and we're not talking about a slap on the wrist. Copyright infringement can involve serious legal consequences, including lawsuits and significant financial penalties.

But here's where it gets complicated: the internet makes copying trivially easy. Right-click, save image. Copy-paste. Screenshot. The technology doesn't care about copyright law. So we need to understand the rules ourselves.

Creative Commons

Creative Commons --- Wanna Work Together?

Here's the hopeful counterpoint. Not every creator wants to lock down their work with full copyright restrictions. Some people want others to use, remix, and share their creations — they just want to set some ground rules. That's what Creative Commons licenses are for. They give creators a way to say things like "you can use this for free, but you have to credit me" or "you can share this but you can't sell it" or "go wild, do whatever you want with it."

Creative Commons is a beautiful example of working with the legal system rather than against it. Instead of defaulting to "all rights reserved," creators can choose from a menu of permissions. It's copyright, but friendlier.
