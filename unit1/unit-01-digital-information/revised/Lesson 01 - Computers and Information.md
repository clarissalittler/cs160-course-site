## What is a computer?

Here's a thing that surprises people: computer science isn't really the study of *computers*. I mean, yes, obviously computers are involved --- it's right there in the name --- but the field is really about **information** and **information processes** for solving problems. Computer scientists are, at their core, problem solvers. The machine on your desk is just the tool.

So in this lesson, we're going to think about what "information" and "information processes" actually mean. These sound like dry, textbook-y terms, but stick with me --- it gets interesting.

As you watch the video below, think about:

- What are the 4 processes that all computers perform?
- What role does information play during these processes?

## Hardware basics

The physical devices that make up a computer are called **hardware**. You've probably got a general sense of this already --- the stuff you can touch, drop, and accidentally spill coffee on.

Figure 1.1 shows the different categories of computer hardware:

![Hardware categories of a computer: CPU is processor; Output devices: speakers, monitor, printer; Memory: RAM (random access memory); Storage: hard drives, solid state drives, USB drive; Input Devices: keyboard, mouse, webcam](images/hardwarebasics.png)
*Figure 1.1. Computer hardware categories*

The big categories are: **input** (keyboard, mouse, webcam --- things that get information *into* the computer), **output** (speakers, monitor, printer --- things that get information *out*), **processing** (the CPU, which does the actual thinking), **memory** (RAM --- fast, temporary storage), and **storage** (hard drives, SSDs, USB drives --- slower but persistent). Every computer you've ever used --- from your phone to a supercomputer --- has all of these components in some form.

## Hardware and software

If you're interested in working with computer hardware --- physically designing circuits and chips --- then computer engineering would be the path for you! But even as computer scientists (which is more about the *software* side), we still need to understand the basics of how the hardware works. You can't write good software if you don't understand what the machine is actually doing with it.
