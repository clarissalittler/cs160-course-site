Lesson 2: Bits, Bytes, and Binary

Big question

How many different messages can we build from components that each have only two states?

Our goals for this lesson: you'll be able to explain what bits and bytes are, read a binary number, convert small whole numbers between binary and decimal, figure out how many patterns a given number of bits can represent, and explain how a fixed representation can harden into a rule that excludes cases nobody anticipated.

Invent a representation before learning one

Suppose all you've got is a stack of cards with a circle on one side and a square on the other. Each card, then, has exactly two possible states. Your partner can see a row of cards but can't hear you. How would you use the cards to send them a number?

There's no single unavoidable answer—really. You might decide a circle means “one” and just count circles. You might give each card position a different value. You might reserve a few patterns for punctuation or instructions instead of numbers. The cards don't mean anything by themselves; people create a representation system by agreeing on a mapping between patterns and meanings. That agreement is where all the magic lives.

If you'd like to actually play with this, try the optional Circle–Square challenge in Google Slides. You can also do it with paper, coins, spoken words (“circle, square, square”), or a text sequence such as CSS. The real work is designing and explaining the system, not dragging shapes around.

One two-state choice is one bit

A bit—short for binary digit—is one choice between two possibilities. We usually write the states as 0 and 1, but the physical reality might be low or high voltage, two magnetic orientations, light reflected or not, or any other pair of states you can reliably tell apart.

Those symbols—0 and 1—are labels, not objects; there's no tiny printed numeral hiding inside the computer. So why two states rather than, say, ten? Because real signals are noisy and measurements are imperfect. Telling “near low” from “near high” is easy to do dependably; telling apart hundreds of exact voltage levels is not. Hardware designers bet on billions of small, dependable distinctions—and it turns out you can build everything else out of those.

Patterns multiply

With one bit you get two patterns: 0 and 1. Add a second bit and the possibilities double, because each of its states can sit alongside every pattern you already had:

In general, n bits give you 2n patterns. This rule is about how many patterns exist, not what they mean. Eight patterns could name numbers, colors, directions, musical notes, or eight different commands—whoever designs the encoding decides.

Patterns versus maximum value: Three bits give you eight patterns. If we spend those patterns on whole numbers starting at zero, they cover 0 through 7—so the maximum is 23 − 1 = 7. Zero is one of the eight values, and forgetting that is a classic off-by-one mistake.

A byte is a convenient group

Computers commonly organize bits into groups of eight called bytes. One byte has 28, or 256, possible patterns. File sizes and storage capacities are normally measured in bytes and in larger units built from them: kilobytes, megabytes, gigabytes, terabytes, &c.

Bit and byte are annoyingly easy to confuse, and the world does not help you: network speeds are usually advertised in bits per second, while file sizes are shown in bytes. Since one byte is eight bits, an 80-megabit transfer works out to about 10 megabytes of raw data—before overhead and other real-world limits take their cut. Watch the capitalization, too: a lowercase b usually abbreviates bit, while an uppercase B abbreviates byte.

Binary as a number system

Our everyday decimal system uses ten digits, 0 through 9, with place values that are powers of ten. In 347, the 3 means three hundreds, the 4 means four tens, and the 7 means seven ones:

347 = (3 × 100) + (4 × 10) + (7 × 1)

The binary number system works exactly the same way, just with two digits and place values that are powers of two. Reading right to left, the positions are worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and so on.

Place values in the binary number 101101
Place value32168421
Binary digit101101

A 1 means “include this place value,” and a 0 means “skip it.” So:

101101₂ = 32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45₁₀

The little subscripts mark which base we're in, for moments when context doesn't make it obvious. The pattern 101101 is the binary representation of the decimal number 45.

Convert decimal to binary

Going the other direction, you pick out powers of two that add up to your number. Start with the largest power that fits, subtract it, and keep going with what's left.

For 45, the largest power that fits is 32, leaving a remainder of 13. Then 8 fits, leaving 5; 4 fits, leaving 1; 2 doesn't fit; and 1 finishes the job. Marking the 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, and 1 positions gives 1 0 1 1 0 1.

  1. 45 − 32 = 13, so the 32 position is 1.
  2. 16 is too large, so the 16 position is 0.
  3. 13 − 8 = 5, so the 8 position is 1.
  4. 5 − 4 = 1, so the 4 position is 1.
  5. 2 is too large, so the 2 position is 0.
  6. 1 − 1 = 0, so the 1 position is 1.

Leading zeros and fixed widths

In ordinary arithmetic, leading zeros change nothing: 00101₂ and 101₂ both mean 5. Inside a computing system, though, the number of positions can matter quite a lot. If a field is exactly eight bits wide, we write 5 as 00000101. Fixed widths are how the computer knows where one piece of information ends and the next begins—without them, a long stream of bits is just soup.

With eight bits, unsigned whole numbers run from 0 to 255. Bigger values need more bits, or a different interpretation. Negative numbers and fractional values need agreed-upon encodings of their own, too. A bit pattern never announces how it should be interpreted; context supplies the meaning, every time.

Everyday life and social impact: when a representation becomes a rule

A fixed-width field isn't just a storage detail. Once institutions build other systems around it, the representation can shape what those systems even recognize.

The most famous example is the “Y2K” problem. Many older programs stored a year as just two decimal digits to save scarce, expensive space: 87 meant 1987. When the year became 00, software could read 2000 as 1900 and happily file it before 1999. Two digits gave more than enough patterns for 100 labels—the trouble was that the interpretation had a century baked into it. Repairing that one assumption across financial, government, and infrastructure systems took an enormous worldwide effort.

For a smaller example, imagine a transit system gives its fare-category field two bits: 00 for standard, 01 for youth, 10 for senior, and 11 for disability fare. The city later adds a low-income fare. Four patterns are no longer enough. Now the designers must expand the field, repurpose a code, bolt on another field, or force some riders into a category that doesn't describe them. None of those options is free.

And more bits solve the capacity problem, but not the social question of which categories should exist in the first place. A database can have thousands of codes and still reflect unfair or incomplete assumptions.

  • Who benefits? Compact, stable codes can make systems faster and easier to coordinate.
  • Who might be harmed? People or situations left out of the original categories may be rejected, misclassified, or told to fit the system rather than having the system fit reality.
  • Who decides? Programmers implement the encoding, but agencies and institutions usually decide the categories—and what it costs to change them. The people being represented deserve a voice too.

Lesson takeaway

Bits are two-state choices, and groups of bits multiply into exponentially many patterns. Binary place value is one agreement about how to read those patterns as numbers—the same patterns can mean something else entirely under a different agreement. In Lesson 3 we'll watch whole communities hammer out exactly such agreements so that computers could represent written language.
