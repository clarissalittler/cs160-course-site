Lesson 2: Bits, Bytes, and Binary

Big question

How many different messages can we build from components that each have only two states?

Our goals for this lesson: you'll be able to explain what bits and bytes are, read a binary number, convert small whole numbers between binary and decimal, figure out how many patterns a given number of bits can represent, and explain how a fixed representation can harden into a rule that excludes cases nobody anticipated.

Invent a representation before learning one

Suppose all you've got is a stack of cards with a circle on one side and a square on the other. Each card, then, has exactly two possible states. Your partner can see a row of cards but can't hear you. How would you use the cards to send them a number?

There are different possible encodings! You might decide a circle means “one” and just count circles. You might give each card position a different value. You might reserve a few patterns for punctuation or instructions instead of numbers. In line with one of the themes of this class, cards don't mean anything by themselves; we create a representation system by agreeing on what patterns mean.

If you'd like to actually play with this, try the optional Circle–Square challenge in Google Slides. You can also do it with paper, coins, spoken words (“circle, square, square”), or a text sequence such as CSS. The real work is designing and explaining the system, not dragging shapes around.

One two-state choice is one bit

A bit—short for binary digit—represents a choice between two possibilities. We usually write the states as 0 and 1. If you've taken a physics class, it's worth pointing out that the physical realization of these states might be low or high voltage, two magnetic orientations, light reflected or not, or any other pair of states you can reliably tell apart.

The symbols 0 and 1 are suggestive labels, but it's not like there are tiny printed numerals hiding inside the computer. So why two states rather than, say, ten? Real signals are noisy and measurements are imperfect, so telling “near low” from “near high” is easier to do reliably with the electronic technologies used through most of computing history. Early in the history of 20th-century computing, however, some researchers experimented with three-state (ternary) computers; a notable example was the Soviet Setun. We'll actually play lightly with the concept in this week's homework.

Patterns multiply

We've established that with one bit you get two patterns: 0 and 1. Add a second bit and the number doubles, because now you have to choose between two things twice:

In general, n bits give you 2n patterns. For example, three bits name eight patterns. Eight patterns could be the numbers 0 through 7, but eight patterns could also name colors, directions, musical notes, or eight different gamepad inputs.

Patterns versus maximum value: Three bits give you eight patterns. If we spend those patterns on whole numbers starting at zero, they cover 0 through 7—so the maximum is 23 − 1 = 7. Zero is one of the eight values, and forgetting that is a classic off-by-one mistake.

A byte is a convenient group

Computers commonly organize bits into groups of eight called bytes. One byte has 28, or 256, possible patterns. File sizes and storage capacities are normally measured in bytes and in larger units built from them: kilobytes, megabytes, gigabytes, terabytes, &c.

Bit and byte are annoyingly easy to confuse, and the way the tech world talks about them doesn't help: network speeds are usually advertised in bits per second, while file sizes are shown in bytes. Since one byte is eight bits, an 80-megabit transfer works out to about 10 megabytes of raw data. Worse, capitalization matters: a lowercase b usually means bit, while an uppercase B means byte.

Binary as a number system

The everyday way we write numbers is a decimal system; this means that it uses ten digits, 0 through 9, with place values that are powers of ten. In 347, the 3 means three hundreds, the 4 means four tens, and the 7 means seven ones:

347 = (3 × 100) + (4 × 10) + (7 × 1)

Yes, we're returning to the way you probably learned to read numbers as a child: tens place, hundreds place, thousands place.

The binary number system works exactly the same way, just with two digits and place values that are powers of two. Reading right to left, the positions are worth 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and so on.

Place values in the binary number 101101
Place value32168421
Binary digit101101

A 1 means “include this place value,” and a 0 means “skip it.” So:

101101₂ = 32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45₁₀

The little subscripts mark which base we're in, for moments when context doesn't make it obvious. The pattern 101101 is the binary representation of the decimal number 45.

Convert decimal to binary

Going the other direction, you pick out powers of two that add up to your number. Start with the largest power that fits, subtract it, and keep going with what's left.

For 45, the largest power that fits is 32, leaving a remainder of 13. Then 8 fits, leaving 5; 4 fits, leaving 1; 2 doesn't fit; and 1 finishes the job. Marking the 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, and 1 positions gives 1 0 1 1 0 1.

  1. 45 − 32 = 13, so the 32 position is 1.
  2. 16 is too large, so the 16 position is 0.
  3. 13 − 8 = 5, so the 8 position is 1.
  4. 5 − 4 = 1, so the 4 position is 1.
  5. 2 is too large, so the 2 position is 0.
  6. 1 − 1 = 0, so the 1 position is 1.

Leading zeros and fixed widths

In ordinary arithmetic, leading zeros change nothing: 00101₂ and 101₂ both mean 5. Inside a computing system, though, the number of positions can matter quite a lot. If a field is exactly eight bits wide, we write 5 as 00000101. Fixed widths are how the computer knows where one piece of information ends and the next begins—without them, a long stream of bits is just soup.

With eight bits, unsigned whole numbers run from 0 to 255. Bigger values need more bits, or a different interpretation. Negative numbers and fractional values need agreed-upon encodings of their own, too, but we're not going to worry about those in this class because they're much more complicated topics, though also very fun ones.

Everyday life and social impact: the accidental consequences of representation

Sometimes a choice of how to encode and store data can have some rather bizarre consequences.

The most famous example is the “Y2K” problem. Many older programs stored a year as just two decimal digits to save scarce, expensive space: 87 meant 1987. This was fine for dates in the 20th century, but the obvious problem is that the year 2000 could be stored as 00 and interpreted as the year 1900. This made sense from the perspective of programmers who hadn't imagined their code living inside important financial, government, and infrastructure systems decades later.

This one bad, cascading assumption meant that in the last few years of the 1990s, programmers around the world were hired to fix all these legacy systems, lest failures in critical government and financial systems cost lives. If that sounds overly dramatic, imagine what delayed insurance payments for medication could do to people who couldn't afford their medication and for whom stopping suddenly could be dangerous, or what might happen if all SNAP benefits across the country were delayed for a month or more.

While software engineering might not have the same scientific tradition as, say, civil engineering, it has no less impact on our lives than bridge-building does.

Lesson takeaway

Bits are two-state choices, and groups of bits multiply into exponentially many patterns. Binary place value is one agreement about how to read those patterns as numbers. The same patterns can mean something else entirely under a different agreement. In Lesson 3 we'll watch whole communities hammer out exactly such agreements so that computers could represent written language.
