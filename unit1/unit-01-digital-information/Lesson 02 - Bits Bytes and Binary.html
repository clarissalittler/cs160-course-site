Lesson 2: Bits, Bytes, and Binary

Big question

How many different messages can we build from components that each have only two states?

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain what bits and bytes are, interpret a binary number, convert small whole numbers between binary and decimal, calculate how many patterns a given number of bits can represent, and explain how a fixed representation can become a rule that excludes new or unanticipated cases.

Invent a representation before learning one

Suppose your only supplies are cards with a circle on one side and a square on the other. A card therefore has exactly two possible states. Your partner can see a sequence of cards but cannot hear you. How could you use the cards to communicate a number?

There is no single unavoidable answer. You might decide that a circle means “one” and count the circles. You might assign a different value to each card position. You might reserve some patterns for punctuation or instructions instead of numbers. The physical cards do not contain meaning by themselves: people create a representation system by agreeing on a mapping between patterns and meanings.

Try the optional Circle–Square challenge in Google Slides. You can also do it with paper, coins, spoken words such as “circle, square, square,” or a text sequence such as CSS. The important work is designing and explaining the system, not manipulating a visual object.

One two-state choice is one bit

A bit, short for binary digit, is one choice between two possibilities. We usually write its states as 0 and 1, but the physical states may be low or high voltage, two magnetic orientations, reflected or unreflected light, or some other reliably distinguishable pair.

The symbols 0 and 1 are labels. A bit is not a tiny printed numeral hiding inside the computer. Hardware designers use two-state systems because real signals contain noise and imperfect measurements. It is easier to distinguish “near low” from “near high” reliably than to distinguish hundreds of exact voltage levels. Billions of small, dependable distinctions can be combined into complex behavior.

Patterns multiply

With one bit, there are two patterns: 0 and 1. Adding a second bit doubles the possibilities because either of its states can accompany every earlier pattern:

In general, n bits can form 2n patterns. This rule concerns the number of patterns, not necessarily the numbers those patterns mean. Eight patterns could name numbers, colors, directions, musical notes, or eight commands. The designer of an encoding decides.

Patterns versus maximum value: Three bits have eight patterns. If we use those patterns for whole numbers beginning with zero, they represent 0 through 7. The maximum is therefore 23 − 1 = 7. Zero is one of the eight values.

A byte is a convenient group

Computers commonly organize bits into groups of eight called bytes. One byte has 28, or 256, possible patterns. File sizes and storage capacities are normally measured in bytes and larger units such as kilobytes, megabytes, gigabytes, and terabytes.

The words bit and byte are easy to confuse. Network speeds are often advertised in bits per second, while file sizes are usually shown in bytes. Because one byte contains eight bits, an 80-megabit transfer represents about 10 megabytes of raw data, before considering overhead and other real-world limits. A lowercase b often abbreviates bit, while an uppercase B abbreviates byte.

Binary as a number system

Our everyday decimal system uses ten digits, 0 through 9, and place values that are powers of ten. In 347, the 3 means three hundreds, the 4 means four tens, and the 7 means seven ones:

347 = (3 × 100) + (4 × 10) + (7 × 1)

The binary number system works the same way but uses two digits and place values that are powers of two. Reading from right to left, the positions are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and so on.

Place values in the binary number 101101
Place value32168421
Binary digit101101

A 1 means “include this place value,” while a 0 means “do not include it.” Therefore:

101101₂ = 32 + 8 + 4 + 1 = 45₁₀

The small subscripts identify the base when context might be unclear. The pattern 101101 is the binary representation of the decimal number 45.

Convert decimal to binary

To represent a whole number in binary, select powers of two that add to the number. Begin with the largest power that is not too large, subtract it, and continue with the remainder.

For 45, the largest usable power is 32. The remainder is 13. Use 8, leaving 5; use 4, leaving 1; skip 2; and use 1. Marking the 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, and 1 positions gives 1 0 1 1 0 1.

  1. 45 − 32 = 13, so the 32 position is 1.
  2. 16 is too large, so the 16 position is 0.
  3. 13 − 8 = 5, so the 8 position is 1.
  4. 5 − 4 = 1, so the 4 position is 1.
  5. 2 is too large, so the 2 position is 0.
  6. 1 − 1 = 0, so the 1 position is 1.

Leading zeros and fixed widths

In ordinary arithmetic, leading zeros do not change a value: 00101₂ and 101₂ both mean 5. In a computing system, however, a fixed number of positions may matter. If a field is exactly eight bits wide, we write 5 as 00000101. Fixed widths let the computer know where one piece of information ends and the next begins.

With eight bits, unsigned whole numbers range from 0 to 255. Larger values require more bits or a different interpretation. Negative numbers and fractional values also require agreed-upon encodings. The bit pattern alone never announces how it should be interpreted; context supplies meaning.

Everyday life and social impact: when a representation becomes a rule

A fixed-width field is not merely a storage detail. Once institutions build other systems around it, the representation can shape what those systems recognize.

The best-known historical example is the “Y2K” problem. Many older programs stored a year using only two decimal digits to save scarce space: 87 meant 1987. When the year became 00, software could interpret 2000 as 1900 or place it before 1999. Two digits had enough patterns for 100 labels, but the interpretation assumed a century. Repairing that assumption across financial, government, and infrastructure systems required enormous effort.

For a smaller example, imagine a transit system gives two bits to a fare-category field: 00 for standard, 01 for youth, 10 for senior, and 11 for disability fare. The city later adds a low-income fare. Four patterns are no longer enough. Designers must expand the field, repurpose a code, create another field, or force some riders into a category that does not describe them.

More bits solve the capacity problem, but not the social question of which categories should exist. A database can have thousands of codes and still reflect unfair or incomplete assumptions.

  • Who benefits? Compact, stable codes can make systems faster and easier to coordinate.
  • Who might be harmed? People or situations omitted from the original categories may be rejected, misclassified, or asked to fit the system rather than having the system fit reality.
  • Who decides? Programmers implement the encoding, but agencies and institutions usually decide the categories and the cost of changing them. The people being represented should have a voice too.

Lesson takeaway

Bits are two-state choices, and groups of bits create exponentially many patterns. Binary place value is one agreement for interpreting those patterns as numbers. The same patterns can represent something entirely different under another agreement. In Lesson 3, we will see how communities developed such agreements to represent written language.
