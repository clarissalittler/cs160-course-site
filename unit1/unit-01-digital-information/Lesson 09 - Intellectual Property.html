Lesson 9 - Intellectual Property

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Define "intellectual property".
  • Understand the rules of copyright.
  • Explain when we can reuse or remix digital information.
  • Explain why generative AI raises new, still-unsettled copyright questions.

Prompt

Imagine you were using some of our pixelation tools to create an image and you posted it online for your friends to see - but, a week later you find out someone took that image and put it on a T-shirt that they’re selling for $10 each. How would you feel in this situation?

When you create materials on the computer, you own them - they are your Intellectual Property. Using materials created by someone else and trying to pass them off as your own is plagiarism when you don't have the creator's permission. There may even be legal consequences for using these materials!

The short version (in case you can't watch)

Here's the core idea if the videos aren't an option right now. Copyright is the right to control who copies your work. The moment you make something - a drawing, a song, a photo, a paragraph of writing - you automatically own the copyright to it. You don't have to file paperwork or slap a © symbol on it; it's yours by default. That means anyone who wants to copy, sell, or remix your work is supposed to get your permission first.

Why does this get so tangled up online? Because on a computer, copying is effortless and perfect. Photocopying a book smudges it a little more each time, but copying a digital file makes a flawless duplicate in a fraction of a second, for free, a million times over. That's wonderful when you want to share something - and a genuine headache when the thing being copied is somebody's livelihood. The very property that makes digital information so powerful (Unit 1's whole theme) is exactly what makes intellectual property so hard to protect.

That's where Creative Commons licenses come in. Instead of "all rights reserved," a creator can attach a license that grants permission in advance - say, "you're free to reuse this as long as you credit me" (that condition is called attribution), or "as long as you don't sell it." It's a way of saying yes ahead of time, on your own terms, so people don't have to email you for every little use. And it circles right back to the question running through this whole unit: who benefits, who's harmed, who decides? Whoever holds the copyright is the one who gets to decide who's allowed to profit - which is a big part of why it matters so much who that person is.

A vivid recent example: the musician Taylor Swift didn't own the master recordings of her early albums - a company did - so she re-recorded every one of them from scratch ("Taylor's Version") to finally control and profit from her own music. Same songs, same voice, but now the ownership matched the authorship. It's a sharp reminder that in copyright, who made a thing and who owns it aren't always the same person - and that gap is exactly where a lot of the money and power live.

The newest fight: AI and copyright

Everything above was more-or-less settled for decades. Then generative AI showed up and scrambled it. Here's the twist: an AI image generator or chatbot doesn't just copy a work the way a photocopier does - it's trained on millions of works (paintings, photos, songs, books, code, maybe your own posts) and learns their patterns well enough to spit out brand-new stuff in the same vein. So the old question "did you copy my work?" mutates into a stranger one: did you use my work to build a machine that now competes with me?

Nobody has fully answered that, and it's being fought out in courtrooms right now. A few of the live battles:

  • The training data. The New York Times is suing OpenAI, arguing its articles were used without permission to train ChatGPT. Getty Images is suing an AI image company over its photos. Novelists, visual artists, and even the major record labels (against AI music apps like Suno and Udio) have filed their own versions of the same case. The core dispute: is training an AI on copyrighted work "fair use," or is it copyright infringement on an enormous scale?
  • Who owns the output? A genuinely surprising one: the U.S. Copyright Office has ruled that a work made purely by AI - with no meaningful human authorship - can't be copyrighted at all. Copyright was designed as a deal to reward human creativity, and typing a prompt doesn't count. So an image you conjure from a single sentence may belong to no one, and anyone can reuse it freely.
  • Your voice, your face. In 2023 an anonymous creator used AI to fake a convincing new song "by" Drake and The Weeknd - neither of whom had anything to do with it. It pulled millions of plays before it was yanked offline. Around the same time, actors went on strike partly over studios wanting to scan their likenesses and reuse them forever. Cloning a specific person's voice or face is a whole new flavor of "copying" that the old rules never imagined.

And here's the honest part: this isn't a tidy villain story. These tools are genuinely useful, plenty of people love making things with them, and "learning from everything that came before" is what human artists do too. But the creators whose life's work got vacuumed up as training data - usually without being asked, credited, or paid - have a real grievance. It's our three questions again, with higher stakes than ever: who benefits (the companies building the models, and everyone generating with them), who's harmed (the illustrators, writers, and musicians now competing against a machine trained on their own work), and who decides (right now, courts and lawmakers scrambling to catch up to the technology)?
