Unit 1: Digital Information

A phone can display a novel, edit a photograph, play a song, and run a game because all of those experiences can be represented as digital information. This unit follows that idea from the physical parts of a computer to the social decisions involved in copying and sharing digital work.

We will build the story in five steps. First, we will treat a computer as a layered system for transforming information. Next, we will build complex patterns from bits and interpret some of those patterns as binary numbers. We will then examine two families of representation—text and sampled media—before asking what compression preserves, what it discards, and who gets to decide. Finally, we will connect the unusual ease of digital copying to copyright, licenses, fair use, public domain, and generative AI.

How to use these lessons: Each page contains a complete text explanation and text-based practice. Videos, diagrams, spreadsheets, and interactive widgets provide optional additional ways to explore. You will not need to see or operate an embedded visual to learn material that is absent from the prose.

A recurring lens: technology, purpose, and people

Representation is never only a technical act. Someone decides what a system can sense, which categories it recognizes, what information it preserves, and what it is allowed to copy. Every lesson therefore returns to three questions:

  • Who benefits?
  • Who might be harmed or left out?
  • Who decides, and who should have a voice?

These questions do not replace technical analysis. They make it complete. To understand a computing system, we need to know both how it works and how its design enters everyday life.

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

  1. Trace input, processing, storage, and output through the hardware and software layers of a computing system.
  2. Explain bits and bytes, determine the number of patterns available with a given number of bits, and represent small whole numbers in binary.
  3. Explain how standards, metadata, and sampling allow computers to represent text, images, and sound.
  4. Compare lossless and lossy compression and justify a choice based on purpose, audience, and consequences.
  5. Distinguish ownership from permission and identify appropriate paths for reusing digital work, including public domain, licenses, and fair use.
  6. Analyze an everyday representation or reuse decision by identifying who benefits, who might be harmed or excluded, and who has decision-making power.

Lesson map

The five lessons in Unit 1
LessonCentral ideaQuestions to carry forward
1. Computers as Layered SystemsComputers use cooperating hardware and software layers to input, process, store, and output information.What layer am I working in? What details are hidden, and whose needs does the design assume?
2. Bits, Bytes, and BinaryTwo-state choices combine into patterns; an encoding agreement gives those patterns meaning.How many patterns are possible? What happens when reality no longer fits the available codes?
3. Text, Unicode, and StandardsShared character standards let independent systems exchange written language.Who agrees on the mapping? Which languages and users does the standard handle well?
4. Images, Sound, and CompressionSampling turns physical signals into data; compression balances fidelity against size and access.Which distinctions must survive? Better for what, for whom, and under what constraints?
5. Copies, Ownership, and PermissionDigital copying is technically easy, while legal and ethical permission remains contextual.What is legally permitted? Who receives credit or compensation? Who benefits, who is harmed, and who decides?
