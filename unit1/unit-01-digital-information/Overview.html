Unit 1: Digital Information

A phone can display a novel, edit a photograph, play a song, and run a game. The reason it can do all these things is that every one of them can be encoded as digital information. This unit explores what computers really are at heart and the implications of being able to represent (almost!) everything we care about as information.

First, we talk about the layered ways that computer hardware takes in, processes, and outputs information in various forms. Then, we see how complex patterns and arbitrary numbers can be understood as sequences of bits before we jump into how everyday data such as text, sound, and images get represented as patterns of bits. Finally, we dip our toes into the complex and grey area of copyright and licensing for digital information, including how they intersect with the infinite replicability of digital goods and with debates about machine learning and training data.

How to use these lessons: Each page contains a complete text explanation and text-based practice. The videos, diagrams, spreadsheets, and interactive widgets are optional extra ways in—you will never need to see or operate an embedded visual to learn material that's covered in the prose.

Our recurring theme: technology, purpose, and people

In this unit, we'll start with a theme of this class: the larger social implications of design decisions in our technology. Someone decides—often many someones—what a system can sense, which categories it recognizes, what information it preserves, and what it's allowed to copy. So many lessons in this unit come back to three questions:

  • Who benefits?
  • Who might be harmed or left out?
  • Who decides, and who should have a voice?

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

  1. Trace input, processing, storage, and output through the hardware and software layers of a computing system.
  2. Explain bits and bytes, determine the number of patterns available with a given number of bits, and represent small whole numbers in binary.
  3. Explain how standards, metadata, and sampling allow computers to represent text, images, and sound.
  4. Compare lossless and lossy compression and justify a choice based on purpose, audience, and consequences.
  5. Distinguish ownership from permission and identify appropriate paths for reusing digital work, including public domain, licenses, and fair use.
  6. Analyze an everyday representation or reuse decision by identifying who benefits, who might be harmed or excluded, and who has decision-making power.

Lesson map

The six lessons in Unit 1
LessonCentral ideaQuestions to carry forward
1. Computers as Layered SystemsYour phone, a game console, and a microwave controller all take in, process, store, and output information. Layers of hardware and software make those jobs manageable.Which layer is handling this job? What details does it hide, and whose needs did its designers have in mind?
2. Bits, Bytes, and BinaryA bit gives us two possible states. Put bits together and we get patterns, but we still need an agreement about what those patterns mean.How many patterns can these bits make? What happens when the thing we want to represent doesn't fit the available patterns?
3. Text, Unicode, and StandardsHow does the text you type make sense on someone else's computer? Shared standards give characters agreed-upon representations that different systems can use.Who gets a say in the standard? Which languages and users does it work well for?
4. Images and SoundA digital image records measurements across space; digital sound records measurements over time. The number and precision of those measurements affect detail and file size.How much detail does this purpose need? What information must the computer have to interpret the measurements?
5. CompressionCompression reduces the space data takes up. Lossless methods let us recover the original exactly; lossy methods discard some information.Which details can we afford to lose, if any? Who gets to choose the quality and file size?
6. Copies, Ownership, and PermissionMaking a digital copy is easy. Figuring out whether we're allowed to use someone else's work, and what we owe them when we do, takes a little more thought.Does a license, permission, or an exception allow this use? Who gets credit or compensation, and who gets a say?

Learning activities

  1. Work through Lessons 1–6, including their text-based “Try it” and social-impact checks (learning objectives 1–6).
  2. Complete Quiz 1.
  3. Complete and submit the Week 1 Assignment.
  4. Complete and share one Week 1 Activity Discussion: Broken Poetry or Mona Lisa Underdrive. Lesson 3 introduces the first option, and Lesson 4 introduces the second.

Looking ahead: This course ends with a final project, an eight-page mini-zine about any computer science topic you like, due at the end of Week 10. You don't need to do anything for it yet. But as you work through this unit and the ones after it, start keeping a list of things you'd enjoy explaining to someone else. How a computer stores a photo, from this very unit, would make a great zine!
