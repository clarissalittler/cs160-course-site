Unit 1: Digital Information

A phone can display a novel, edit a photograph, play a song, and run a game. The reason it can do all these things is that every one of them can be encoded as digital information. This unit explores what computers really are at heart and the implications of being able to represent (almost!) everything we care about as information.

First, we talk about the layered ways that computer hardware takes in, processes, and outputs information in various forms. Then, we see how complex patterns and arbitrary numbers can be understood as sequences of bits before we jump into how everyday data such as text, sound, and images get represented as patterns of bits. Finally, we dip our toes into the complex and grey area of copyright and licensing for digital information, including how they intersect with the infinite replicability of digital goods and with debates about machine learning and training data.

How to use these lessons: Each page contains a complete text explanation and text-based practice. The videos, diagrams, spreadsheets, and interactive widgets are optional extra ways in—you will never need to see or operate an embedded visual to learn material that's covered in the prose.

Our recurring theme: technology, purpose, and people

In this unit, we'll start with a theme of this class: the larger social implications of design decisions in our technology. Someone decides—often many someones—what a system can sense, which categories it recognizes, what information it preserves, and what it's allowed to copy. So many lessons in this unit come back to three questions:

  • Who benefits?
  • Who might be harmed or left out?
  • Who decides, and who should have a voice?

Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

  1. Trace input, processing, storage, and output through the hardware and software layers of a computing system.
  2. Explain bits and bytes, determine the number of patterns available with a given number of bits, and represent small whole numbers in binary.
  3. Explain how standards, metadata, and sampling allow computers to represent text, images, and sound.
  4. Compare lossless and lossy compression and justify a choice based on purpose, audience, and consequences.
  5. Distinguish ownership from permission and identify appropriate paths for reusing digital work, including public domain, licenses, and fair use.
  6. Analyze an everyday representation or reuse decision by identifying who benefits, who might be harmed or excluded, and who has decision-making power.

Lesson map

The six lessons in Unit 1
LessonCentral ideaQuestions to carry forward
1. Computers as Layered SystemsComputers use cooperating hardware and software layers to input, process, store, and output information.What layer am I working in? What details are hidden, and whose needs does the design assume?
2. Bits, Bytes, and BinaryTwo-state choices combine into patterns; an encoding agreement gives those patterns meaning.How many patterns are possible? What happens when reality no longer fits the available codes?
3. Text, Unicode, and StandardsShared character standards let independent systems exchange written language.Who agrees on the mapping? Which languages and users does the standard handle well?
4. Images and SoundSampling turns light and sound into finite measurements whose detail and size depend on representation choices.Where and how precisely should we measure? What context does software need to interpret the results?
5. CompressionLossless and lossy methods reduce file size under different promises about what survives.Which distinctions must survive? Better for what, for whom, and under what constraints?
6. Copies, Ownership, and PermissionDigital copying is technically easy, while legal and ethical permission remains contextual.What is legally permitted? Who receives credit or compensation? Who benefits, who is harmed, and who decides?
