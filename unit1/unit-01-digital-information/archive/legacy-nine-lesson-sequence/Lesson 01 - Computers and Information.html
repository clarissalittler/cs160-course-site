What is a computer?

You might assume computer science is the study of computers themselves - the physical machines we have on our desks and carry around in our pockets - but it's really the study of information, and of how we process information to solve problems. Computer scientists are problem solvers first, machine-owners second. So in this lesson, we're going to think about what "information" and "information processes" actually mean.

What makes a computer a computer?

Every computer — from a supercomputer to the chip in your microwave — does four basic things with information: it takes information in (input), holds onto it (storage), transforms it (processing), and sends the results back out (output). Keep those four in your back pocket — you'll see them again and again all term.

So, what is information?

We just claimed computer science is really about information — so let's not skate past the word. Here's the surprisingly deep idea: information is what lets you tell one possibility apart from another. Every time you answer a yes-or-no question — heads or tails, left or right, on or off — you've pinned down one possibility out of two, and that single choice is the smallest possible scrap of information. Computer scientists call it a bit. Everything else — this sentence, your favorite song, a photo of your dog — is just a lot of those yes/no choices stacked up together.

The second big idea is that information doesn't care what it's made of. The story of Little Red Riding Hood is the same story whether it's spoken aloud, printed in a book, tapped out in Morse code, or beamed to your phone. Swap out the physical stuff carrying it — sound waves, ink, flashes of light — and the information rides along unchanged. Computer scientists call this being substrate-independent, and it's the whole reason one machine can juggle so many kinds of things: once you can turn something into information, the computer doesn't need to know or care where it came from.

Which leads to the third, and maybe most world-changing, idea: if everything can be reduced to the same stuff — bits — then one machine can do it all. Your phone swallowed the camera, the map, the calculator, the jukebox, the photo album, the newspaper, and the shoebox of old letters, because underneath, to the phone, they are all the same thing: numbers. That's not a metaphor — it's literally why a single pocket device could absorb a dozen once-separate industries.

And one last idea, because it echoes through the rest of this unit: information can be copied perfectly. Photocopy a photo and it gets a little worse each time; copy a digital file a million times and the millionth copy is flawless — identical to the first, and costing essentially nothing to make. That single fact is a superpower and a headache: it's why you can back up everything you own for free, and also why music, movies, and software are so easy to pirate. (We'll come back to that tension at the end of the unit.)

So here's the thread that ties this whole unit together: if all information really is just bits — yes/no, on/off, 0/1 — then how on earth do we squeeze numbers, letters, photos, and music down into nothing but 0's and 1's? That's exactly what the next several lessons work out, one kind of information at a time.
