Lesson 4: Images, Sound, and Compression

Big question

Pictures and sounds seem very different from text. How can a computer turn both into numbers, and what must it trade away to keep their files manageable?

By the time you finish this lesson you should be able to explain pixels, RGB color, and sampled sound; say what metadata is for; estimate how representation choices change file size; pick between lossless and lossy compression for a given purpose; and work out who gains and who loses when a platform chooses quality settings on its users' behalf.

From continuous experience to discrete measurements

Light and sound in the physical world vary continuously—there's no smallest step. A digital computer can't store every possible point in a scene or every instant of a sound, so a device samples the world instead: it takes a finite set of measurements and records each one with a finite number of bits.

Sampling forces two decisions on you, every time:

  • Where or when do you measure? An image sensor carves a scene into locations; an audio system measures a signal at successive moments.
  • How precisely do you record each measurement? More bits allow more possible values—more shades, finer sound-pressure levels.

More samples and more bits per sample can preserve finer distinctions, but they also pile up more data. Digital media design is a permanent negotiation among detail, file size, processing time, transmission speed, and what your audience actually needs.

Black-and-white images: one bit per pixel

A raster image is a rectangular grid of small picture elements called pixels. For the simplest black-and-white image, one bit per pixel does the job. Let's agree that 0 means white and 1 means black.

Consider this 5-by-5 image, written one row at a time:

10001
01010
00100
01010
10001

Under our agreement, the four corner pixels are black, the center is black, and diagonals of black pixels connect them into an X. But the same 25 bits would form a different picture if we changed the width or read columns before rows. A file clearly needs more than pixel values.

Metadata tells us how to interpret data

Metadata is information about other data. An image file may carry its width, height, color system, creation time, camera settings, and geographic location. The first two tell software how to slice a long stream of pixels into rows; the color-system information says how to interpret each pixel's numbers.

Metadata is useful—and it can also reveal more than a person expects. A shared photograph may include the time and location where it was taken. Social platforms often strip some metadata, but I wouldn't bet on every service doing it. Representation choices affect privacy as well as appearance.

Resolution describes how many samples make up an image, usually given as width by height in pixels. Higher resolution can preserve smaller spatial details—but it doesn't automatically make an image better. Enlarging a low-resolution file just gives you bigger versions of the measurements you already have. And whether extra pixels are even noticeable depends on display size and how far away you're standing.

The optional Pixelation Widget lets you enter width, height, and binary pixel values. If a visual grid isn't useful to you, build a textual image like the 5-by-5 example above: state the dimensions, read each row aloud or in text, and describe the resulting pattern.

Color images: several measurements per pixel

Many screens create colors by mixing red, green, and blue light. In the common RGB representation, each pixel stores three numbers: how much red, how much green, how much blue. With eight bits per channel, each value runs from 0 to 255.

Three eight-bit channels use 24 bits per pixel and give 224, or 16,777,216, possible patterns. Not every display reproduces every distinction exactly, and human perception doesn't treat all numeric changes equally, but the scheme gives software one consistent vocabulary for color.

Web colors are often written in hexadecimal, a compact base-16 notation. The six digits in #RRGGBB are three two-digit values, each running from 00 (decimal 0) to FF (decimal 255). So black is #000000, white is #FFFFFF, and bright red is #FF0000. You won't need to do hexadecimal arithmetic in this unit—just recognize that it's another way of writing the same channel numbers.

Sound: samples across time

Sound reaches you as changing air pressure. A microphone converts those changes into an electrical signal, and an analog-to-digital converter measures that signal again and again. Each measurement is a sample. Play the values back in sequence through a speaker and they approximate the original pressure pattern.

  • The sample rate is how many measurements are taken per second. A higher rate can capture faster changes in the signal—and therefore higher frequencies—at the cost of more samples.
  • The bit depth is how many bits each measurement gets. More bits provide more possible levels and can represent smaller differences, but they make each sample larger.
  • The number of channels counts separate simultaneous signals. Stereo audio normally stores a left and a right channel, doubling the sample data compared with one-channel audio at the same settings.

Sampling never captures an infinite physical signal in its entirety—that isn't the goal. The practical question is whether the representation preserves the distinctions your purpose needs. Speech for a phone call, a studio music master, a hearing-assistance device, and a scientific recording may all want different settings.

A concrete sound-file calculation

Record one minute of uncompressed stereo audio at 44,100 samples per second, with 16 bits per sample in each channel, and the raw sample data comes to:

44,100 samples/second × 16 bits/sample × 2 channels × 60 seconds = 84,672,000 bits

Divide by eight and you get 10,584,000 bytes—about 10.6 megabytes for a single minute, before any file metadata. A ten-minute recording would hold about 106 megabytes of raw sample data. What's nice about this calculation is that every design choice is visible in it: double the duration, sample rate, bit depth, or channel count and you double the data.

For comparison, an audio file delivered at 128,000 bits per second contains roughly 960,000 bytes per minute, plus a small amount of metadata. Getting that much smaller normally requires lossy audio compression: the decoder can produce convincing sound, but it can't reconstruct every original sample value. A lossless audio format may also reduce the raw size, but how much it saves depends on the patterns in the recording.

Why uncompressed media becomes large

The same arithmetic works for images. A 1920-by-1080 RGB image with 24 bits per pixel contains:

1920 × 1080 × 24 = 49,766,400 bits

That's 6,220,800 bytes—about 6.2 megabytes—before headers and other metadata. Now imagine thirty such images every second: uncompressed video gets enormous, fast. Audio grows the same way with sample rate, bit depth, channels, and duration.

But files need to cross networks, fit on devices, and open before your patience runs out. Compression represents information using fewer bits, and it comes in two flavors—lossless or lossy—depending on whether decompression can reconstruct the original data exactly.

Lossless compression: find a shorter description

Lossless compression preserves every bit of the original: decompress and you get back exactly what went in. It works by finding patterns or repetition and describing them more efficiently.

A tiny example: the sequence AAAAAAAAAABBBBBCC is ten A's, five B's, and two C's, so the run-length description 10A5B2C says the same thing in less space. Real formats get much fancier—they build dictionaries of repeated sequences, hand shorter codes to common patterns, or predict values and store only the differences—but the spirit is the same: say it shorter, lose nothing.

What “lossless” means in practice

Imagine a file named draft.txt containing the sentence Meet at 10:30. You add it to a ZIP archive and email the smaller archive. The recipient can't read the compressed codes as ordinary prose; their software must decompress the archive first. After extraction, draft.txt is byte-for-byte identical to what you started with: the capital M, the spaces, the digits, the colon, the period—every byte in exactly its old position.

So lossless compression does not summarize the paper, remove “unimportant” vowels, swap in synonyms, or lower the quality and hope nobody checks. It creates a reversible description. If decompression changes even one character in the file, either the process wasn't lossless or the archive was damaged.

A practical exactness test: Software can compare the original and decompressed files one byte at a time, or compare their checksums—short values calculated from all of a file's bytes. Matching files produce the same checksum. Backup systems, software downloads, and scientific workflows lean on such tests to catch accidental changes.

What must be identical depends on the format:

  • A ZIP archive can preserve the complete bytes of the files placed inside it. After extraction, the contents of a program file or paper are exactly as they were before.
  • A PNG image losslessly preserves its decoded pixel values. A PNG encoder may organize or compress those values differently, and resaving may change optional metadata, but compression need not blur a line, alter a color value, or invent an artifact.
  • A FLAC audio file losslessly preserves the decoded audio samples: it can reconstruct the same sample numbers as an uncompressed PCM recording from a shorter description of their patterns.

One caveat: lossless doesn't promise a dramatically smaller file. A one-character text file can actually grow when placed in an archive, since the archive needs headers and a decoding key. And JPEG photographs, MP3 audio, ZIP archives, and DOCX documents are already compressed, so compressing them again often saves little and can even add overhead. The guarantee is exact reconstruction, not a particular percentage.

The optional Text Compression Widget demonstrates a dictionary method: you replace repeated phrases with shorter symbols and store a key that allows the original to be rebuilt. The key counts toward the file size, so replacing a phrase that appears only once can make the result larger rather than smaller.

Reach for lossless whenever every detail must survive: program code, financial records, a term paper, database contents, or an image that will undergo further exact editing. Common lossless formats include ZIP archives and PNG images.

Lossy compression: preserve what matters for a purpose

Lossy compression gets files smaller by deliberately throwing some information away. Decompression produces an approximation rather than the original bit-for-bit data. Good lossy methods use knowledge about perception and about a medium's purpose to discard the distinctions least likely to matter.

Photographic compression may record broad color patterns precisely while skimping on tiny changes people rarely notice. Audio compression may spend fewer bits on sounds masked by louder neighbors. Video compression can store how one frame differs from the previous one instead of repeating a nearly identical image.

Three everyday comparisons:

  • A screenshot containing small text: PNG is usually the right call, because its lossless image compression keeps the captured pixels exact and the letters sharp. Save the same screenshot as a heavily compressed JPEG and you can get fuzzy halos around every letter.
  • A phone photograph: A moderate-quality JPEG can be far smaller than the raw pixel data while remaining convincing at webpage size. The decoded pixels aren't the camera's original pixels—but for that purpose, the lost distinctions may not matter.
  • A music recording: FLAC can reconstruct every stored source sample; a lossy format such as MP3 or AAC stores an approximation designed for listening. You may never notice a good lossy copy in ordinary conditions, but audio analysis or later editing cannot recover the discarded sample information.

Lossy doesn't mean “bad,” and lossless doesn't always mean “best.” A smaller file loads over a slower connection, eats less mobile data, uses less energy, and reaches more people. Push compression too hard, though, and you smear the text in an image, add ringing to the audio, destroy evidence someone needed, or make speech harder to understand. Quality has to be judged by people, in context—there's no dial that measures it for you.

Resaving an already compressed file compounds the loss, because each generation discards a little more. If you expect to edit and export a work more than once, keep a high-quality or lossless original and make your smaller delivery copies from that.

A practical media workflow often has three stages:

  1. Capture or create a source with enough detail for the work you intend to do.
  2. Keep a master in an uncompressed or lossless format, so future edits begin from the best available information.
  3. Export delivery copies for particular audiences: a smaller JPEG for a webpage, compressed audio for a phone connection, a higher-quality copy for an archive.

Delivery copies aren't inferior work—they're versions optimized for a use. The mistake is discarding the master and later expecting a delivery copy to contain information its compression deliberately removed. (It won't. It can't.)

When someone asks “which is better?”, the strongest answer is usually another question: better for what, for whom, and under what constraints?

Everyday life and social impact: platforms choose quality for us

Upload a photograph, send a video message, or stream a song, and a platform will often compress it automatically. It may create several versions and pick one based on your device and network. Most users never see the sample rates, bit depths, resolutions, or quality settings involved—the knobs exist; you just don't get to touch them.

That automatic choice can be valuable. Smaller files cost less to deliver, start playing sooner, use less storage and energy, and can make participation possible for someone on a data-limited phone plan or an unreliable connection. But discarded detail matters differently to different people. Compression that sounds fine to one listener can make unfamiliar accents or quiet speech harder to understand. A photograph compressed for casual scrolling may be unsuitable for documenting fine artwork, reading distant text, or preserving evidence.

There's also a power difference between choosing to make your own delivery copy and having a platform alter every upload. A responsible service can disclose what it changes, retain an original when appropriate, offer a high-quality option, and test results with the people most likely to be affected.

  • Who benefits? People on slow or expensive connections, the platform paying for storage and delivery, and audiences who receive media quickly.
  • Who might be harmed? Creators may lose important detail or control; audiences may receive less intelligible or less trustworthy information; people with particular sensory or access needs may experience quality differently.
  • Who decides? Engineers and product managers set defaults, platform owners set business priorities, creators sometimes choose export settings, and users may—or may not—receive a quality control.

Try it: make the tradeoff explicit

  1. A black-and-white icon is 20 pixels wide and 10 pixels high and uses one bit per pixel. How many bits are needed for its raw pixels?
  2. What additional information would software need to display those bits correctly?
  3. You are publishing an audio interview for students who may use limited mobile data. Would you choose lossless or lossy compression? Name one quality you would test before publishing.
  4. You compress a program file into a ZIP archive and then extract it. What must be true for the compression to count as lossless?
Check your reasoning
  1. 20 × 10 × 1 = 200 bits.
  2. At minimum, the width and height, the order in which pixels are stored, and the mapping between bit values and colors. A real format needs further metadata.
  3. A carefully chosen lossy speech format is reasonable because smaller downloads improve access. Test intelligibility—including with quiet voices, different accents, and assistive listening setups—rather than judging file size alone.
  4. The extracted program's complete byte sequence must be identical to the original. It is not enough for the program to look approximately right or usually run.

Lesson takeaway

Digital images sample space into pixels; digital audio samples a signal across time. More samples and more bits per sample preserve more distinctions and create more data, and metadata supplies the context needed to interpret all of it. Lossless compression reconstructs the original exactly, while lossy compression trades selected information for smaller files. Neither choice is automatically correct: the purpose, audience, and consequences determine what must survive.
