Lesson 1 - What Is AI?

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain, in plain words, what people mean when they say "AI."
  • Identify AI systems already woven through everyday life.
  • Distinguish narrow AI from the contested idea of artificial general intelligence (AGI).
  • Describe the big shift that made modern AI work — letting computers learn from examples instead of being told every rule.

So what is AI, really?

We can begin with an honest but slightly inconvenient answer: AI is a broad goal, not a single gadget. "Artificial intelligence" names the project of getting computers to perform tasks we might call intelligent when people do them—recognizing a face in a photo, interpreting a sentence, recommending a song, finding a route, or controlling a vehicle.

Those tasks have little in common, and that is the point. AI is not one specific technology. Many different tools are grouped under the name because each handles a task that once seemed to require human intelligence.

When someone says, "We added AI," a useful response is: what kind, and for what task? That question will help us distinguish a technical description from a marketing label.

AI outside science fiction

Science fiction often represents AI as a conscious robot. Most real AI systems are less dramatic: they are software systems performing particular tasks, often invisibly and in the background.

Many of us use several such systems in an ordinary day without noticing:

  • Your email quietly dumped junk mail into a spam folder.
  • A maps app figured out a route and guessed your arrival time.
  • Your phone's photo app grouped pictures by who's in them.
  • Autocomplete finished your words (sometimes hilariously wrong).
  • Netflix, Spotify, or YouTube lined up the next thing to watch or hear.
  • Your bank flagged a weird charge as possible fraud.
  • Your face unlocked your phone in a quarter of a second.

These examples are ordinary precisely because the technology has become part of the background. A spam filter is more representative of present-day AI than a fictional robot is.

Narrow AI and general AI

This distinction is worth keeping in view throughout the unit.

Narrow AI is designed or trained for a bounded range of tasks. A spam filter sorts messages but cannot drive a car; a route finder plans routes but cannot screen email. Even broadly capable systems have particular architectures, training, and limits rather than a human's general experience of the world.

Chatbots complicate the label because one model may write a poem, debug code, and explain photosynthesis. That breadth is impressive, but fluent performance across many prompts does not by itself establish human-like understanding or awareness. Later lessons will examine the learned prediction mechanisms beneath that performance.

Artificial general intelligence, or AGI, is the proposed idea of a system that could learn and reason flexibly across domains. There is no agreed technical definition or test for AGI, and experts disagree about how current systems relate to it.

The grounded position is therefore to ask what a system can do, how it was built and evaluated, and where it fails. Those questions are more informative than either hype or panic.

Try it: Think back over the last 24 hours and list five occasions when an AI system may have been involved—for example, a recommendation, phone unlock, spam filter, autocompleted word, or fraud alert. The exercise makes visible how often these systems operate in the background.

Where this is all headed

If AI is now woven into so many products, why did progress on these tasks take so long?

For decades, the plan was to hand-program intelligence directly. Smart people would sit down and try to write out every rule a computer would need. Want a program to recognize a cat? Just describe a cat in rules — has fur, has whiskers, has pointy ears... and it falls apart almost immediately. What about a hairless cat? A cat curled into a ball? A cat in shadow? A cat that's mostly behind a food cart? You can't write enough rules to cover the messy, fuzzy real world, and for a long time, that's exactly where AI got stuck. It could win at chess (rigid rules, clean board) but couldn't reliably tell a dog from a muffin in a photo.

A different approach is to stop trying to write every rule. Instead of describing a cat explicitly, we can provide many images labeled "cat" and "not cat" and train a system to find statistical patterns in those examples.

That approach—machine learning—powers many contemporary AI systems, and it is our next subject.
