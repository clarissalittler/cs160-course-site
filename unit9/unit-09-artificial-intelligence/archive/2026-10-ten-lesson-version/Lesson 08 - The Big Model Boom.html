Lesson 8 - The Big Model Boom

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain, in plain terms, how attention distinguishes a transformer from earlier sequence models
  • Describe the training objective behind many large language models and why next-token prediction can support varied tasks
  • Explain how model size, data, and computing resources contributed to recent improvements
  • Place GPT-3 and ChatGPT on a rough timeline, and describe the role of human-preference tuning

So far, we have examined neural networks, gradient-based training, and the rise of deep learning in vision and speech. Those developments set the stage for the rapid growth of large generative models in the late 2010s and early 2020s.

A new design shows up: the transformer

In 2017, researchers published a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need." It introduced a network architecture called the transformer, which became central to later language models.

The main idea does not require advanced mathematics. Earlier recurrent language models processed a sequence step by step while carrying information forward. A transformer uses attention to calculate relationships among positions in a sequence, and its design allows much of the training computation to run in parallel.

Consider the sentence "The dog chased the ball because it was excited." Interpreting "it" requires relating that position to earlier words. Attention gives a model a direct way to represent such relationships. Combined with efficient parallel training, that design made it practical to learn from very large text collections.

The recipe that changed everything

A simplified version of the training recipe is:

Take a large neural network, train it on a broad collection of text and code, and use gradient descent and backpropagation to improve its prediction of the next token. A token may be a word, part of a word, or punctuation.

For example, given "The cat sat on the ___," a model assigns probabilities to possible continuations. Repeating next-token prediction across a large corpus encourages the model to represent grammar, factual associations, styles, and recurring patterns in explanations and code. Those learned patterns can then support drafting, summarization, question answering, and programming tasks.

These systems are large language models, or LLMs. The broader term foundation model refers to a model that can be adapted for many downstream applications.

The surprise was scale

Researchers found that increasing model size, training data, and computing resources often improved performance across many tasks. Some capabilities became much more visible at larger scales, although researchers continue to debate how abrupt or predictable those changes are.

The boom therefore did not come from one isolated trick. It combined transformer architectures, scaling, data, hardware, training methods, and substantial investment.

The timeline that put it in your hands

A few historical signposts show the pace of development:

  • 2020—GPT-3. This large model demonstrated fluent continuation and adaptation from a small number of examples across writing and coding tasks.
  • November 2022—ChatGPT. A conversational interface made this class of model accessible to a broad public and brought generative AI into everyday discussion.
  • The wave that followed. Later systems from several organizations expanded multimodal input, tool use, and multi-step task performance. Product names change quickly, but prediction, gradient-based training, and additional forms of tuning remain part of the technical foundation.

One more ingredient: human feedback

A model trained only for next-token prediction is not automatically a useful conversational assistant. Its output may be rambling, evasive, unsafe, or otherwise poorly suited to a user's request. Additional training can shape that behavior.

This is one place where reinforcement learning can return. After initial training, developers may fine-tune a model using examples and human preference judgments. Evaluators compare responses, and the resulting feedback helps tune the model toward desired behavior. Reinforcement learning is one method for doing this; other preference-tuning methods are also used.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, is one influential version of this process. It helps explain why an assistant behaves differently from a raw text-completion model. The polished tone should not be confused with a guarantee of accuracy, and fluent output alone does not settle questions about understanding or consciousness.

Try it: In a chatbot, enter the beginning of a familiar saying, such as "A penny saved is...", and observe the continuation. Then ask for an ending that avoids the expected phrase. Compare how strongly the response follows the familiar pattern in each case.
