Lesson 3 - Fitting a Line and Rolling Downhill

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what it means to fit a line to data, and describe a line with two knobs: a slope and an intercept.
  • Explain how a single "wrongness" number lets a computer compare one guess to another.
  • Describe gradient descent as feeling your way downhill, one step at a time.
  • Explain what the step size (learning rate) does, and why too big and too small both cause trouble.
  • Read a two-knob wrongness map, and explain why gradient descent doesn't always head straight for the lowest point.

Last lesson, I said that learning means "adjusting numbers to be a little less wrong." That's a nice slogan, but let's actually watch it happen. We'll start with the simplest learning problem there is: drawing a line through some dots.

Dots with a trend

Say you jotted down how long you studied for a few quizzes and what you got:

  • 1 hour → 60
  • 2 hours → 70
  • 3 hours → 80
  • 4 hours → 90

If you plot those, with hours across the bottom and score up the side, the dots march up and to the right in a straight line. A line through them summarizes the trend and lets you make predictions. For 2.5 hours, it would guess about 75. (Predicting way outside your data is riskier, though. The line also "predicts" 130 for 8 hours, which is not how quizzes work!)

Finding a line like this is called linear regression, and it's one of the oldest and most useful tools in data science.

A straight line has exactly two knobs. The slope is how steeply it tilts; here, each extra hour goes with about 10 more points. The intercept is where it crosses the vertical axis, which here is 50. Turn the slope knob and the line tilts; turn the intercept knob and the line slides up or down. These numbers are invented to keep the arithmetic easy, by the way, so please don't take them as a promise about real quizzes.

Measuring "wrong"

You can look at a line and tell it's a good fit. A computer can't "look," so it needs a number. For each dot, we measure the vertical gap between the dot and the line. Then we square each gap (so that misses above and below the line both count as bad) and add them all up. That total is the loss, which I'll keep calling the wrongness. A line hugging the dots has a small wrongness; a line way off has a big one.

Now "find the best line" becomes something a computer can do: find the knob settings that make the wrongness as small as possible. But how does it know which way to turn the knobs?

One knob, three tables

Let's shrink the problem down to one knob. Suppose we're predicting the tip someone leaves from the size of their bill, with this tiny model:

predicted tip = knob × bill

Here's an invented dataset from three tables:

BillActual tip
$10$2
$20$4
$30$6

You can probably see the answer already: every tip is 20% of the bill, so the best knob setting is 0.2. The fun part is watching the computer get there without being told.

Say it starts with a bad guess, knob = 0.5. Then it predicts tips of $5, $10, and $15, which are all too big. The gaps are 3, 6, and 9, and the wrongness is 3² + 6² + 9² = 126. Try a few smaller knob settings and the wrongness drops: 56 at 0.4, 14 at 0.3, and 0 at 0.2. So turning the knob down is the way to go.

Rolling downhill

Here's the picture I want you to carry around. Imagine you're standing on a hillside in thick fog. You can't see the bottom of the valley. But you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a step downhill. Then you feel the slope again and take another step. Keep doing that, and you'll work your way down toward the bottom, even though you never saw the whole landscape.

That's gradient descent. The "landscape" is the wrongness for every possible knob setting, and where you're standing is the current setting. The gradient is the math version of "which way does the ground slope, and how steeply?" The computer calculates it, takes a step in the downhill direction, and repeats. That's the whole trick, and it's the same trick that trains enormous neural networks.

How big a step?

There's one more choice to make: how far do you move on each step? That's the step size, usually called the learning rate, and it matters enormously.

  • If the steps are too big, you can leap right over the bottom of the valley and land on the other side, then leap back, bouncing around or even flying off.
  • If the steps are too small, you'll get there eventually, but it'll take forever, and with a giant model "forever" costs real money.

So how do you pick a good one? There's no formula that always works, so in practice people try a few step sizes and watch what the wrongness does, which makes choosing a learning rate one of the practical arts of training a model.

Two knobs: a valley seen from above

The tip model is nice and simple, but real models have more than one knob. So let's go back to fitting a line, which has two. This time the data is about ice cream: how many cones an ice cream stand sold on ten summer days, and how hot it was each day. (The numbers are invented, but realistic. Remember the ice cream from Unit 8?) Our line has two knobs: how many cones the stand sells on a 60°F day, and how many extra cones it sells for every 10 degrees warmer.

With two knobs, the wrongness landscape isn't a curve anymore. It's a whole surface, with hills and valleys, and the right-hand picture shows it from above, the way a hiking map does. Light means less wrong and dark means more wrong, and the bands work like contour lines. Every spot on the map is one possible line, and the ball shows which one you've got right now.

(If the playground doesn't appear, open it in a new tab.)

Press Run and watch closely, because the ball doesn't head straight for the lowest point! It drops quickly down into the valley, and then it slides along the valley floor toward the bottom. Why? This valley is long and tilted. Turning both knobs together in just the right way barely changes the wrongness, while turning them against each other changes it sharply. Gradient descent always steps in the steepest direction from wherever it happens to be standing, and the steepest direction isn't always pointed at the goal. (Real landscapes, with millions of knobs, are full of long, tilted valleys like this one.)

Experiment:

  1. Click somewhere far from the green X to drop the ball there, then press Run. Try a few different starting spots. Does the ball always end up in the same place?
  2. Move the Step size slider one notch below the maximum and run it again. Now the ball zigzags back and forth across the valley, overshooting each side, before it settles down.
  3. Now push the step size all the way up. What happens?
  4. Watch the left-hand picture while the ball moves. Every step on the map is the line tilting and sliding a little closer to the dots.

From one knob to billions

Our tip model has one knob, so "downhill" just means left or right. The ice cream line has two, so "downhill" can point anywhere on a map. A big neural network can have billions, which means a landscape with billions of directions that nobody can picture. But the loop is the same: measure the wrongness, figure out which way is downhill for every knob, take a small step, repeat.

That leaves one big question. With a billion knobs, how does the computer figure out which way to turn each one, without testing them one at a time? That's what the next lesson is about.
