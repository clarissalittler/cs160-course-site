Lesson 9 - AI in Everyday Life

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify at least five ways AI is used in daily life
  • Explain how recommendation systems, voice assistants, and self-driving cars use concepts from earlier lessons

You're Already Using AI

Here's something that might surprise you: by the time you sit down to read this lesson, you've probably already interacted with AI a dozen times today—and you might not have noticed any of them.

Think about your morning. A spam filter sorted your email. An algorithm decided which social media posts to show you. You unlocked your phone with your face. Your keyboard predicted the next word before you could finish thinking it. None of these felt like "using AI"—they just felt like using your phone. But every one is powered by machine learning. Let's trace through the biggest ones.

Recommendation Systems

Ever wondered how Netflix seems to know you'll like a show before you've heard of it—or how Spotify builds a playlist eerily suited to your taste? The answer is recommendation systems.

Remember unsupervised learning from Lesson 4? That's what's happening here. These systems analyze the behavior of millions of users—what they watch, skip, replay, rate—and find patterns without anyone labeling the data. The algorithm notices that people who binge-watched that one moody Scandinavian crime drama also tend to enjoy slow-paced Japanese mystery films. Nobody told it those genres were related. It figured it out from the data.

YouTube, Amazon, TikTok—they all do some version of this. Netflix has estimated that over 80% of shows watched on their platform were found through recommendations, not searching. These systems don't just suggest content; they shape what millions of people see, read, buy, and listen to every day.

Search Engines

When you type a question into Google, you're not just running a keyword search through a giant index. Modern search engines use machine learning at nearly every stage.

First, the engine has to understand what you're asking. If you type "how to fix a running toilet," it needs to figure out you mean plumbing, not a toilet that has somehow learned to jog. That's natural language processing—the same techniques behind the LLMs from Lesson 6. Then the engine ranks results using models trained on billions of past searches to predict which pages actually answer your question. Google's BERT and MUM systems are neural networks—the same architecture from Lesson 5—applied to understanding search queries.

Voice Assistants

"Hey Siri, what's the weather?" seems simple enough. But there's a surprisingly deep stack of AI happening between your voice and that weather forecast.

First, speech recognition: the assistant converts the sound waves of your voice into text. This is supervised learning—the model was trained on enormous datasets of human speech paired with transcriptions (those labels again!). It handles different accents, background noise, mumbling, &c.

Second, natural language understanding: once it has the text, it figures out what you mean. "What's the weather?" is straightforward. But "Will I need an umbrella tomorrow?" requires the assistant to understand you're really asking about rain, for your location, for tomorrow. That's not keyword matching—that's understanding intent.

Third, text-to-speech: the response gets turned back into natural-sounding spoken language. Modern TTS systems use deep learning to generate speech that sounds remarkably human—a far cry from the robotic voices of early GPS systems.

Three separate AI systems, chained together, running in about a second.

Autocomplete and Predictive Text

Your phone's keyboard is, in a very real sense, a tiny language model. Every time it predicts the next word you're about to type, it's doing a scaled-down version of exactly what we talked about in Lesson 6—using patterns in language to guess what comes next.

Modern keyboards also learn from your typing habits. They pick up on words you use often, phrases you repeat, even the people and places you mention frequently. That's why your autocomplete suggestions are different from your friend's. Gmail's "Smart Compose" takes this further, suggesting entire sentences as you draft an email. It's machine learning running quietly in the background of something you do every day.

Self-Driving Cars

Self-driving cars pull together almost everything we've discussed in this unit. Let's be honest: fully autonomous cars that handle any situation without a human aren't quite here yet. But partially autonomous systems—lane-keeping, adaptive cruise control, automatic emergency braking—are already in millions of cars on the road.

These systems combine multiple AI techniques. Computer vision (supervised learning) lets the car "see"—identifying lane markings, pedestrians, traffic signs, &c. Sensor fusion combines cameras, radar, and lidar to build a 3D model of the surroundings. And reinforcement learning—from Lesson 4—helps the system learn strategies: when to brake, when to merge, how to handle a four-way stop where everyone arrived at the same time. (Humans aren't great at that one either.)

The challenge is that driving is a problem where mistakes have life-or-death consequences. A kid chasing a ball into the street, a mattress in the road, broken traffic lights—these edge cases are exactly where current AI struggles most.

Medical Imaging

Here's one that might genuinely save your life someday. AI systems are now being used to analyze medical images—X-rays, MRIs, CT scans, &c.—and in some cases they can detect diseases as accurately as experienced doctors.

This is supervised learning in action. Researchers train models on huge datasets of medical images labeled by specialists—"this mammogram shows a tumor," "this retinal scan shows diabetic eye disease," "this skin lesion is benign." The model learns the visual patterns and can flag potential problems in new images for a radiologist to review.

It's not replacing doctors—the AI serves as a second pair of (very tireless, very consistent) eyes. The AI won't get tired, won't get hungry, and won't have an off day. Used together, humans plus AI often outperform either one alone.

Something to try:

Try keeping a log for one day of every time you interact with an AI system. Include the obvious ones (voice assistants, search engines) but also the hidden ones—spam filters, photo tagging, fraud alerts from your bank. You might be surprised how long the list gets.
