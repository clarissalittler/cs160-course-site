## AI Is Everywhere (and That's the Point)

Before we get into specific career paths, I want to make something clear: you don't have to become an "AI person" for this unit to matter to your career.

AI is touching basically every field now. Healthcare. Education. Agriculture. Music. Law. Transportation. Finance. Journalism. If you work with data, make decisions, create things, or interact with customers -- and that covers pretty much every job -- you'll encounter AI systems in your career. Understanding how they work, what they can and can't do, and what can go wrong gives you an enormous advantage, regardless of your field.

That said, if you *are* interested in working directly with AI, there are a lot of fascinating career paths. And one of the things I find most exciting about this field is how diverse the work is. It's not just programmers in hoodies. It's researchers, engineers, artists, ethicists, policymakers, data analysts, and more. There's room for a lot of different kinds of people with a lot of different kinds of skills.

Let's look at some of the options.

## AI Researcher

### What they do

AI researchers are the people pushing the boundaries of what's possible. They come up with new techniques, test new ideas, publish papers, and generally try to figure out how to make AI systems do things they couldn't do before. Think of them as the scientists of the AI world.

Most work in university labs, but major tech companies also have research divisions (Google DeepMind, Meta AI, Microsoft Research, &c.). The line between academic and industry research in AI is blurrier than in most fields.

### Skills and backgrounds that help

- Strong math and statistics foundation
- Programming skills (Python is the lingua franca of AI research)
- Creative thinking -- you're coming up with things nobody's tried before
- Ability to read and build on other people's research
- Communication skills -- if you can't explain your ideas, they don't go anywhere

Most AI researchers have graduate degrees, but there's a growing recognition that the field benefits from diverse educational backgrounds. Some of the most interesting work comes from people who combine AI with expertise in another field.

### People doing this work

- **Ayanna Howard** works in robotics, building robots that can interact with children in therapeutic settings. She's combined AI with healthcare in ways that directly help people. She's been doing this work for decades and is now Dean of Engineering at Ohio State University.

- **Timothy Brathwaite** worked at Lyft on transportation modeling -- using AI to understand how people move through cities, including by bicycle. His work is a great example of AI applied to urban planning and everyday life.

- **Angela Fan** has worked at Meta (formerly Facebook) on AI research in natural language processing -- the branch of AI focused on understanding and generating human language. Her work on text generation is directly relevant to the LLMs we talked about in Lesson 10.

## AI/ML Engineer

### What they do

If researchers are the scientists, engineers are the builders. AI/ML engineers take ideas -- sometimes from research papers, sometimes from their own experimentation -- and turn them into working systems. They write the code, train the models, optimize performance, and deploy systems into production where real users interact with them.

This is a huge and varied category. An ML engineer at a hospital might be building a system to detect tumors in medical images. An ML engineer at a music streaming service might be building the recommendation algorithm. An ML engineer at a bank might be building a fraud detection system. The AI techniques are similar, but the domains are wildly different.

### Skills and backgrounds that help

- Programming -- this is non-negotiable, but you don't need to have been coding since age 10. Plenty of great ML engineers started in their 20s or later
- Math, particularly statistics and linear algebra (we touched on why in the linear regression lesson)
- Problem-solving -- breaking big problems into smaller ones
- A willingness to keep learning, because this field changes fast. Seriously. Techniques that are state-of-the-art right now might be outdated in two years. You need to enjoy learning new things

### People doing this work

- **Omoju Miller** has worked at GitHub on machine learning systems that help developers write better code. She's also a passionate advocate for diversity in tech and has spoken widely about making AI accessible to people from all backgrounds.

- **Yizel Vizcarra** works in Natural Language Processing (NLP) -- teaching computers to understand and work with human language. If you think about it, every time you talk to Siri or Alexa or Google Assistant, you're using NLP systems that engineers like Yizel helped build.

## Data Scientist

### What they do

Data scientists are the people who collect, clean, analyze, and make sense of data. This might or might not involve AI directly, but it almost always involves the skills that AI is built on -- statistics, programming, and the ability to tell a story with numbers.

I want to emphasize the "clean" part because it's important and often underappreciated. Real-world data is messy. It has gaps, errors, inconsistencies, weird formatting, duplicate entries, &c. A huge part of being a data scientist is wrangling data into a form that's actually usable. It's not glamorous, but it's essential -- and if you're the kind of person who likes bringing order to chaos, you might find it deeply satisfying.

### Skills and backgrounds that help

- Statistics -- understanding what data is telling you (and what it's not)
- Programming -- especially Python and SQL for working with data
- Data visualization -- presenting information in ways that people can actually understand. A beautiful chart that communicates a key insight is genuinely valuable
- Domain knowledge -- understanding the field you're working in. A data scientist at a hospital needs to understand something about healthcare. A data scientist at a retail company needs to understand something about consumer behavior. Your expertise in another field can be a superpower here

### People doing this work

- **Kristian Lum** worked at the Human Rights Data Analysis Group, using data science to investigate human rights abuses. Her work on predictive policing bias -- showing how these systems can perpetuate racial disparities -- is directly related to what we discussed in Lesson 11. Data science applied to justice.

- **Carolina Barcenas** is a Senior Vice President of Research at Visa, where she uses data science to understand consumer spending patterns and detect fraud. Financial data science is one of the biggest employers in this field.

- **Devin Guillory** has worked at Etsy on computer vision and NLP systems, and has a background in electrical engineering. His career is a great example of how different technical backgrounds can converge on AI work.

## AI/Data Policymaker

### What they do

Policymakers work at the intersection of technology and government. They help define guidelines and laws around AI -- what companies are allowed to do with AI systems, what protections citizens have, how to balance innovation with safety. They also help countries develop strategies for AI talent and infrastructure.

This is a *brand new* field. Like, really new. There aren't well-worn career paths here yet. Which is either terrifying or exciting, depending on your personality. (I think it's exciting.) If you care about justice, fairness, and the public good -- and you're willing to learn enough about technology to understand what you're regulating -- this is a field that desperately needs more people.

### Skills and backgrounds that help

- Understanding of law and policy (how laws get made, how regulation works)
- Enough technical knowledge to understand what AI systems do and how they can go wrong
- Communication and persuasion -- you'll be explaining complex technical concepts to non-technical decision-makers
- Cross-cultural awareness -- AI policy is a global issue, and what works in one country might not work in another

### People doing this work

- **Terah Lyons** led the Partnership on AI, a multi-stakeholder organization that brings together companies, researchers, and civil society groups to develop AI best practices. Her work bridges the gap between the tech industry and the public interest.

- **Linet Kwamboka** works on data protection policy in Kenya, helping shape how a rapidly growing tech ecosystem handles personal data. AI policy isn't just a Silicon Valley concern -- it matters everywhere.

- **Philip Thigo** serves as a technical advisor for data, innovation, and open government in Kenya. His work shows how AI policy connects to broader questions about governance, transparency, and development.

## AI Ethicist

### What they do

AI ethicists are closely related to policymakers, but where policymakers tend to work with governments, ethicists often work directly within companies or at research organizations. Their job is to think critically about the risks and consequences of AI systems -- before those systems are deployed, ideally.

They create ethical guidelines, evaluate new products and features for potential harms, connect with policymakers and the public, and generally serve as the conscience of organizations building AI. This is, in my opinion, one of the most important roles in the entire AI ecosystem right now. After everything we discussed in Lesson 11, I hope you can see why.

### Skills and backgrounds that help

- Deep understanding of AI -- you need to know how these systems work to identify what can go wrong
- Background in ethics, philosophy, social science, or related fields
- Research and writing skills
- The ability to push back against powerful people and institutions when necessary -- this takes courage

### People doing this work

- **Timnit Gebru** is probably the most well-known AI ethicist in the world. She co-founded Black in AI, an organization that works to increase the presence and visibility of Black people in AI. She co-authored the "stochastic parrots" paper we discussed in Lesson 10. She was fired from Google after raising concerns about bias in their AI systems, which became a major controversy in the field. She now runs the DAIR (Distributed AI Research) Institute. Her story illustrates both the importance of this work and the risks of doing it.

- **Mira Lane** worked on design and ethics at Microsoft, thinking about how AI systems should behave and how to embed ethical considerations into the design process from the start.

- **Rumman Chowdhury** has worked on responsible AI at Accenture and later at Twitter, developing frameworks for auditing AI systems and holding companies accountable. She's been a vocal advocate for making AI accountability practical, not just theoretical.

## AI Artist

### What they do

AI artists use AI tools to create art -- visual art, music, video, interactive experiences, and more. Some build their own AI systems. Others use existing tools in creative ways. And then there's a whole category of people who write the personalities and dialogue for AI assistants -- a job that requires creative writing skills in a very non-traditional context.

This is a field that's changing incredibly fast, especially since the explosion of generative AI tools in recent years. The question of what it means to create art "with" or "using" AI is itself a fascinating creative and philosophical question.

### Skills and backgrounds that help

- Creativity and artistic vision -- the AI is a tool, but you still need ideas
- Enough technical understanding to know what the tools can do and how to push their boundaries
- Willingness to experiment and play -- some of the most interesting AI art comes from people who tried something weird just to see what would happen
- For personality/dialogue writing: strong creative writing skills, empathy, and an understanding of how people interact with technology

### People doing this work

- **Anna Ridler** creates visual art and video installations using AI, often exploring the relationship between nature, technology, and data. Her work is shown in galleries and museums worldwide. She's a great example of someone with an art background who learned enough about AI to use it as a creative medium.

- **Holly Herndon** is a musician who has created an AI model of her own voice and uses it in her compositions. She's thought deeply about the ethics of AI-generated music and what it means for artistic identity. Her work raises questions that feel urgent now that AI-generated music is everywhere.

- **Jonathan Foster** and **Deborah Harrison** are writers who helped create the personality for Microsoft's Cortana. Deborah is a poet. Jonathan is a comedy writer. Their work is a reminder that AI products need human voices and human sensibilities, even -- especially -- when the "person" you're writing for is a machine.

## Unit 9 Summary: What We've Learned About AI

We've covered a lot of ground in this unit. Let's pull it all together.

### Key Takeaways

1. **AI is software that finds patterns in data and makes predictions** -- not sentient robots, not science fiction. The AI you encounter every day (autocomplete, recommendations, spam filters) is pattern-matching at various levels of sophistication.

2. **Machine learning models learn from data.** There are three main approaches: **supervised learning** (you give the model labeled examples and it learns the mapping), **unsupervised learning** (you give the model unlabeled data and it finds structure on its own), and **reinforcement learning** (the model learns by trial and error, getting rewards for good actions).

3. **Linear regression is a foundational technique:** fit a line to data to make predictions. It's simple, it's interpretable, and understanding it gives you intuition for how more complex models work.

4. **Deep learning uses many layers of neurons** to automatically discover complex patterns in data. It's what powers image recognition, language translation, and most of the flashy AI applications you've seen.

5. **Large language models work by predicting the next token** -- one word (or piece of a word) at a time. This is deceptively simple and remarkably powerful, but it means LLMs are prone to **hallucination**: confidently generating incorrect information.

6. **AI systems can inherit and amplify bias from their training data.** This isn't theoretical -- it has caused real harm in facial recognition, hiring, criminal justice, and other domains. Feedback loops can make bias worse over time.

7. **As informed citizens, understanding AI helps you participate in important conversations** about how it should be used. AI policy is being shaped right now, and your voice matters.

### The Bigger Picture

If there's one thing I hope you take away from this unit, it's this: AI is a tool. A powerful, fascinating, sometimes dangerous tool -- but a tool. It's not magic and it's not destiny. The choices about how AI is built, deployed, and regulated are *human* choices. They're being made by people right now, and those people need to hear from a broad range of voices -- not just engineers and executives.

You now know enough about AI to have informed opinions about it. That matters. Use it.
