Lesson 6 - How Large Language Models Work

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what tokens are and why LLMs use them instead of words.
  • Describe how LLMs are trained on one core task: predicting the next word.
  • Explain what "hallucination" means and why it happens.
  • Describe what a context window is and why it matters.

So How Does This Actually Work?

You've probably used ChatGPT, or Claude, or Gemini. You type something in, it types something back, and it sounds like a person. Sometimes a surprisingly knowledgeable person. How does that actually work?

The answer is both simpler and weirder than you might think.

There's no secret database of answers it's looking things up in. There's no little person behind the curtain. What's happening is — genuinely — a very sophisticated form of autocomplete. But before you say "that's it?!" — let's dig into why that turns out to be so powerful.

Tokens: How an LLM Reads

Here's something that might surprise you: LLMs don't actually read words. They read tokens — little chunks of text that might be whole words, parts of words, or even single characters.

For example, the word "unbelievable" might get broken up into three tokens: "un", "believ", and "able". A common short word like "the" is usually one token. A long or unusual word might get split into several. Punctuation marks, spaces, &c. — those are tokens too.

Why bother with this? Think about it: there are a lot of words in the English language. And then there are names, technical terms, slang, words from other languages, typos... If the model had to have a separate entry for every possible word it might ever encounter, the list would be impossibly huge. Tokens are a clever compromise. You break text down into a few tens of thousands of common chunks, and you can build any word — any word in any language, actually — from those chunks.

This also matters practically. When you hear people talk about a model's "token limit," they're talking about how much text the model can handle at once. We'll come back to that in a moment.

Training: Predict the Next Word

Here's the big secret. The core idea behind every large language model — GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Llama, all of them — is one deceptively simple task:

Given all the text so far, predict the next word.

That's it. The entire foundation of ChatGPT, Claude, and every other LLM is: given this text so far, what word (well, what token) comes next?

To train the model, researchers take absolutely massive amounts of text — we're talking huge portions of the internet, digitized books, code repositories, Wikipedia, &c. — and they use it to train a neural network (the kind we talked about in Lesson 5) on that one job. They show it a passage with the last word removed, and the model has to guess what comes next. Then they tell it the right answer, and it adjusts itself a tiny bit to be more accurate next time. Repeat this billions of times.

Now here's why this turns out to be so much more powerful than it sounds. To predict the next word well, the model has to learn an enormous amount about how language works. It has to pick up grammar, obviously. But it also has to learn facts ("The capital of Oregon is ___"), reasoning patterns ("If all dogs are mammals, and Rover is a dog, then Rover is ___"), writing styles, humor, coding conventions — basically, anything that helps it guess what comes next in a passage of text.

It's like how studying for a trivia competition doesn't just teach you random facts — it forces you to learn how knowledge connects together. The model isn't explicitly taught that Portland has great food carts or that water boils at 100°C. It just picks these things up because knowing them helps it predict text better.

Attention: How the Model Connects Ideas

There's one more piece of the puzzle worth understanding — even if we're going to keep the math out of it entirely.

When you read the sentence "The cat sat on the ___," you know to predict something like "mat" or "floor." How? Because you're paying attention to the important words — "cat" and "sat" — and kind of ignoring less important ones like "the." You're connecting ideas across the sentence.

LLMs do something remarkably similar through a mechanism that researchers — with a rare flash of good naming — actually called attention. The model learns which words in the input are relevant to which other words. When it sees "The president of France visited ___," it knows to pay close attention to "France" and "president" rather than "The" and "of."

This is what lets LLMs handle complex sentences where the meaning of one part depends on something that appeared much earlier. If you write a long paragraph about your dog and then later say "she loves to play fetch," the attention mechanism is what helps the model figure out that "she" refers to your dog and not, say, your sister who you mentioned three paragraphs ago.

The landmark paper that introduced this approach was literally titled "Attention Is All You Need" — and it turned out to be one of the most influential papers in the history of AI.

Context Windows: The Model's Short-Term Memory

Here's something important to understand about using these tools: the model can only "see" a certain amount of text at once. This is called its context window, and you can think of it like the model's short-term memory.

Early LLMs had context windows of only a few thousand tokens — maybe a page or two of text. Newer models can handle much more (some can work with hundreds of pages), but there's always a limit.

Why does this matter? A few reasons:

First, in a long conversation, the model will eventually "forget" what you said at the beginning. If you have a detailed back-and-forth that goes on for pages and pages, the earliest parts of the conversation may drop out of the context window entirely. The model isn't being forgetful on purpose — it literally can't see that text anymore.

Second, if you paste in a really long document and ask the model to analyze it, there's a limit to how much it can take in at once. This is also why you sometimes see models seem to lose track of instructions you gave them earlier — those instructions may have scrolled out of the window.

The practical takeaway: if an LLM seems to have forgotten something you told it, it probably has. Restating important context can help.

Hallucination: When the Model Makes Stuff Up

And now for arguably the most important thing to understand about LLMs: they make things up. Confidently. All the time.

This is called hallucination, and it's not a bug that will be fixed in the next software update — it's a fundamental consequence of how these models work. Remember, the model is a prediction machine. It produces whatever text sounds right based on patterns it learned during training. It has no separate fact-checking step. It has no internal sense of "I know this" versus "I'm guessing." It just produces the next most plausible-sounding token.

Here's a concrete example: ask an LLM to cite academic papers about a topic, and it might generate author names, paper titles, journal names, and publication years that look completely real — but are entirely fabricated. The citations look right because the model has seen thousands of real citations and knows what format they should be in. But it's pattern-matching the format, not retrieving actual papers from a database.

Another classic: ask it about a somewhat obscure historical event, and it might confidently blend details from two different events into one, or invent a plausible-sounding date that's wrong by a decade. It does this because it's not "remembering" the event — it's generating text that sounds like it's about the event.

Important: Never trust an LLM's output without checking it against a reliable source. This is especially true for factual claims, statistics, quotes, citations, and anything where accuracy really matters. LLMs are powerful tools for brainstorming, drafting, and exploring ideas — but they are not reliable sources of truth.

This doesn't mean LLMs are useless — far from it! But it means you need to treat them more like a very enthusiastic research assistant who sometimes makes things up rather than an encyclopedia. Verify the important stuff.

Putting It All Together

So let's recap what's actually happening when you type a question into ChatGPT or Claude:

Your text gets broken into tokens. Those tokens, along with all the previous conversation in the context window, get fed into a massive neural network. The network uses attention to figure out which parts of the input matter most. Then it predicts the next token. Then it predicts the token after that, and the one after that, one at a time, until it has generated a full response.

The whole thing is — at its core — an incredibly sophisticated autocomplete. But "sophisticated" is doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence. To predict language well enough to carry on a conversation, explain a concept, or write a poem, the model has had to internalize an astonishing amount of knowledge about how language and the world work.

Whether that counts as "understanding" or is "merely" very impressive pattern matching is one of the great open questions in AI right now — and honestly, nobody has a definitive answer yet.

Try it yourself:

Open ChatGPT, Claude, or another LLM and ask it to tell you about a specific historical event — maybe something from Oregon history, or a scientific discovery you're interested in. Then fact-check at least three of its claims using a reliable source (a textbook, a reputable website, &c.). Did it get anything wrong? Did it present anything in a misleading way? What does this tell you about how you should use these tools?
