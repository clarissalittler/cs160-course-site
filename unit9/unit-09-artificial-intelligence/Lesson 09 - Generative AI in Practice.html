Lesson 9 - Generative AI in Practice

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what makes an AI tool "generative" and name some of the common ones
  • Describe tasks these tools can support and situations in which their output requires particular caution
  • Explain what a "hallucination" is and why it happens, using what you already know about how these tools work
  • Write clear prompts and evaluate the resulting output

We now have a working account of how these tools are built and trained. This lesson turns to their practical use: choosing suitable tasks, giving useful instructions, and recognizing output that needs verification or revision.

What does "generative" mean?

Earlier examples of AI often involved classification: deciding whether an email is spam or whether a photo shows a cat or dog. In contemporary public discussion, however, "AI" often refers to generative systems.

Generative AI produces new output—such as text, images, code, music, or synthetic speech—rather than only labeling existing input. A person supplies a prompt or other starting material, and the model generates a continuation or transformation.

Common examples include:

  • Text and chat: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot generate responses to written or spoken prompts.
  • Images: DALL-E, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion generate images from descriptions and other inputs.

Other systems generate music, video, and voice. We will concentrate on text and image tools because they make the main opportunities and limitations easy to examine.

Tasks they can support

Generative tools are often useful when a quick first pass provides material that a person can review and improve:

  • Drafting and brainstorming. A request for several possible titles or approaches can provide a place to begin.
  • Summarizing supplied material. A model can propose a shorter account of a text, which should still be checked against the original.
  • Explaining an idea in several ways. A different analogy or level of detail may make a difficult concept clearer.
  • Revising tone. A model can suggest ways to make an email warmer, shorter, or more formal.
  • Working through code. A model can explain an error, translate a small example, or propose a draft that the programmer then tests and revises.

The common theme is that the output is a starting point, not an automatically trustworthy final answer.

Where they fail - and why

These tools also fail in a characteristic way, and their training objective helps explain why.

A chatbot generates plausible tokens from learned patterns, so it can produce a false citation, date, quotation, or study in the same fluent style it uses for accurate material. Some systems can search the web or retrieve documents, but retrieval does not guarantee that the generated summary represents a source correctly. When an answer includes citations, open them and check that they support the specific claims attributed to them.

Producing confident fakes like that has a name: hallucination. The tool produces something that sounds right but isn't real.

This behavior is not usefully described as lying, which implies an intention to deceive. It arises because generating a likely continuation is not the same as verifying a fact. The practical rule is straightforward: verify anything that matters. Check names, numbers, dates, quotations, and citations against reliable sources. For medical, legal, or financial decisions, use qualified and current sources rather than relying on generated text.

A few other honest limits worth keeping in mind:

  • It can be subtly biased - that's a big enough deal that it's the whole next lesson.
  • It doesn't truly know whether it's right. Confidence in the wording tells you nothing about accuracy.
  • It can be out of date, depending on its training data and whether it retrieves current information.
  • It may accept a false premise. A well-phrased request can still elicit an explanation of something that is not true.

How to actually prompt one

No special syntax is required, but clear instructions help. A useful mental model is a fast drafting partner whose work always requires review: provide the task, relevant context, constraints, and a standard for a successful response.

A few habits that make a huge difference:

  • Be specific. Replace "Write about dogs" with a task that names the subject, purpose, audience, length, and tone.
  • Give context and an example. Explain who the material is for and provide a sample when the desired style matters.
  • State constraints. Specify the length, formality, format, and material the response may or may not use.
  • Ask for revisions. A first answer can be made simpler, shorter, more formal, or more focused through follow-up instructions.

Try it: Pick a small task - say, "write a three-sentence thank-you note to a coworker who covered my shift." Give the exact same prompt to two different chatbots (any two of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Copilot). Read both answers side by side. Where do they differ - tone, length, word choices? Which one would you actually send, and why?

Optional extension: Test a chatbot's citations on an obscure topic. Ask for three sources, then verify that each source exists and supports the claim. Record any invented, mismatched, or incomplete citation.
