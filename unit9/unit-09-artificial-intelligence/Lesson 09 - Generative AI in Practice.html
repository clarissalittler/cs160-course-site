Lesson 9 - Generative AI in Practice

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what makes an AI tool "generative" and name some of the common ones
  • Describe what these tools are genuinely good at - and where they reliably fall down
  • Explain what a "hallucination" is and why it happens, using what you already know about how these tools work
  • Write better prompts and get more useful results

You've now got a sense of how these tools are built and trained. So let's switch gears and talk about the part you'll actually do day to day: using them well. Because there's a real skill to it, and it mostly comes down to knowing what they're good for and what they'll happily get wrong.

What does "generative" even mean?

Earlier in the course, a lot of the AI we looked at was about sorting things into buckets - is this email spam or not, is this photo a cat or a dog. That's incredibly useful, but it's not what people usually mean when they say "AI" in 2026.

"Generative" AI is the kind that makes new things. Instead of labeling something that already exists, it produces something that didn't exist a second ago - text, images, code, music, even synthetic voices. You give it a starting point and it generates the rest.

You've probably already bumped into the big ones:

  • Text and chat: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Copilot. You type, they write back.
  • Images: DALL-E, Midjourney, and Stable Diffusion. You describe a picture in words and they paint it.

There are tools for music, video, and voice too, and more show up every few months. But the chat and image ones are where most people start, so that's where we'll spend our time.

What they're genuinely great at

Here's the honest pitch. These tools are fantastic at a specific shape of work - the kind where a fast, fluent first pass saves you a ton of time:

  • Drafting and brainstorming. Blank-page paralysis is real, and "give me ten possible titles" cures it fast.
  • Summarizing long things. Paste in a dense article and ask for the gist in five bullet points.
  • Explaining a hard idea five different ways until one of them finally clicks for you.
  • Rewording for tone. "Make this email warmer." "Make this sound less stiff." It's good at that.
  • Getting unstuck on code, translating between languages, or making a rough first draft you then go in and fix.

Notice the theme: they shine when you treat the output as a starting point, not a finished answer. A first draft you improve, not a final word you trust.

Where they fail - and why

Now the part everybody needs to hear. These tools fail in a very particular way, and once you understand why, you'll spot it coming.

Remember the mechanism: a chatbot is predicting plausible next words from the patterns it learned - which means it can be confidently, fluently wrong. It can hand you a fake citation, a wrong date, an invented quote, a study that never happened - all written in the same smooth, sure-sounding voice it uses for the true stuff. Now, these days most chatbots can look things up - they'll run a web search mid-answer and hand you links. But the model underneath still isn't pulling your answer out of a database of verified facts; it's generating text, and search results are just more raw material it can garble. So the habit stays the same: even when an answer comes with citations, click one and check that the source actually says what the summary claims. (Sometimes the link is real but the "fact" attributed to it is not.)

Producing confident fakes like that has a name: hallucination. The tool produces something that sounds right but isn't real.

And here's the important nuance - it's not lying. Lying needs intent, and there's no "someone in there" trying to fool you. It's pattern-completion with no built-in fact-checker. The tool has no little voice that says "wait, are you sure?" It just produces the most plausible-looking continuation and moves on. So the rule writes itself: verify anything that matters. Names, numbers, dates, quotes, citations, medical or legal or money stuff - check it against a real source before you rely on it.

A few other honest limits worth keeping in mind:

  • It can be subtly biased - that's a big enough deal that it's the whole next lesson.
  • It doesn't truly know whether it's right. Confidence in the wording tells you nothing about accuracy.
  • It can be out of date, depending on when it was last trained (and whether it bothered to run a search this time).
  • It will cheerfully agree with a wrong premise. Ask "why is the moon made of cheese?" and a lot of tools will gamely explain. Garbage in, fluent garbage out.

How to actually prompt one

The good news is you don't need any special syntax. You just need to be clearer than you'd think. A useful mental model: treat it like a fast, eager, sometimes-unreliable intern - bright and quick, but it needs you to spell things out and it needs checking.

A few habits that make a huge difference:

  • Be specific. "Write about dogs" gets you mush. "Write a 100-word funny paragraph about why my dog is afraid of the vacuum" gets you something usable.
  • Give context and an example. Who's it for? What's the situation? "Here's a sentence I like the sound of - match that vibe."
  • Say how long and how formal. Two sentences or two pages? Casual or professional? Tell it up front.
  • Ask for revisions. This is the secret sauce. The first answer is rarely the best one. "Make it simpler." "Shorter." "More formal." "Lose the third paragraph." It's a conversation, not a vending machine.

Try it: Pick a small task - say, "write a three-sentence thank-you note to a coworker who covered my shift." Give the exact same prompt to two different chatbots (any two of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Copilot). Read both answers side by side. Where do they differ - tone, length, word choices? Which one would you actually send, and why?

Try it (optional, the fun one): Go hunting for a hallucination. Ask a chatbot for three sources or quotes on a fairly obscure topic - a niche local history question, a minor historical figure, something off the beaten path. Then actually try to verify them. Are the sources real? Do the quotes exist? See if you can catch it inventing something with a totally straight face.

Get comfortable with both sides of this - the genuinely useful and the confidently-wrong - and you'll get far more out of these tools than someone who treats every answer as gospel.
