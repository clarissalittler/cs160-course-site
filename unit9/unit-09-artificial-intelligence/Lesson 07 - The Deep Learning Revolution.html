At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what the “deep” in deep learning means and why multiple layers can be useful
  • Describe several factors behind the rapid progress in neural networks around 2012: data, computing power, architectures, and training methods
  • Use AlexNet, AlphaGo, and improvements in speech systems as landmarks in that period
  • Identify examples of deep learning in everyday devices and services

The last few lessons explained how a neural network can be trained by updating many parameters to reduce a loss. We will now step back from that mechanism and look at the conditions that made deep neural networks broadly effective.

What does “deep” mean?

A deep neural network contains multiple layers of learned representations. Modern networks may have dozens or hundreds of layers; the word “deep” refers to this sequence of transformations.

But why would stacking layers help? Because each layer gets to build on what the layer below it figured out. Picture a network learning to recognize photos. The first layer learns to spot the simplest possible things — little edges, a light-to-dark boundary here, a slanted line there. The next layer takes those edges and notices when they combine into shapes — corners, curves, blobs. The layer after that puts shapes together into parts — an eye, an ear, a wheel, a window. And a layer further up assembles those parts into whole things — a face, a cat, a car.

A programmer did not specify each of those visual features directly. Training shaped the representations layer by layer. Depth allows a network to compose simpler features into more complex ones, although architecture, data, and training choices matter as well.

The turning point: around 2012

Neural networks are not new; their history reaches back to the mid-twentieth century. For long periods, limited data, computing resources, and training techniques restricted what they could do, and other approaches often dominated the field.

Around 2012, several developments came together and produced rapid improvements:

  • Data. Large labeled datasets became available, including millions of images as well as extensive collections of text and recordings. Such datasets supported the training and evaluation of larger models.
  • Computing power. Graphics processing units, or GPUs, were designed to perform many graphics calculations in parallel. The same parallelism proved useful for the matrix operations used to train neural networks.
  • Architectures and methods. Researchers improved network designs, activation functions, regularization, and training procedures while building on gradient descent and backpropagation.

One important demonstration came in 2012. A deep network called AlexNet won the ImageNet image-recognition competition and reduced the error rate substantially compared with the other entries. Its result helped accelerate the field's shift toward deep learning.

Then the cascade

Progress followed in speech recognition, computer vision, and machine translation. Voice assistants became more capable, and translation systems began producing more fluent results across many language pairs.

In March 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol, one of the world's strongest Go players. Go has an enormous search space, and many researchers had expected top-level machine play to remain much farther away. The match became a visible milestone for modern learning systems.

Deep learning in everyday systems

These developments now appear in ordinary devices. Photo software groups similar faces, dictation converts speech into text, and camera-translation tools combine vision and language processing. Deep learning is one important family of methods behind features like these.

Because these systems often operate in the background, it is easy to overlook how much the technology has changed since 2012.
