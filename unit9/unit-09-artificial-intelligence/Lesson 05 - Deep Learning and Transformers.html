The last two lessons were about the engine: the training loop of guess, measure, blame, and nudge. This lesson is about what happened when that engine got far more fuel.

What makes it "deep"?

A deep neural network is one with many layers. Modern networks can have dozens or even hundreds. Why would piling up layers help?

Because each layer gets to build on what the layer below it figured out.

Picture a network learning to recognize photos. In networks like this, researchers have found that the first layers tend to respond to really simple things, like edges and spots where light turns to dark. Later layers combine those into shapes like corners and curves. Layers after that combine shapes into parts, like an eye, a wheel, or a window. And layers near the top combine parts into whole things, like a face or a car.

Nobody programmed any of those features in. They came out of training, layer by layer, because they turned out to be useful for making fewer mistakes. That's what's so striking about deep learning: you set up the layers and the loop, and the network works out its own way of seeing.

The turning point: around 2012

Neural networks aren't new. The basic ideas go back to the 1950s, and backprop was popularized in the 1980s. But for decades they mostly didn't work well enough to beat other methods. Then, around 2012, three things came together:

  • Data. The internet made huge collections of examples available, like ImageNet, a dataset of millions of labeled photos.
  • Computing power. Graphics cards (GPUs), built to draw video-game graphics, turned out to be great at doing the kind of math neural networks need, lots of it in parallel.
  • Better methods. Researchers found improvements to network designs and training tricks that made deep networks actually trainable.

In 2012, a deep network called AlexNet won the ImageNet image-recognition contest by a huge margin, and the whole field pivoted toward deep learning almost overnight.

Speech recognition and translation got dramatically better over the next few years. In 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, one of the world's best players at Go, a game many people had expected computers to need another decade to master. And in 2020, DeepMind's AlphaFold predicted the 3D shapes of proteins accurately enough to transform part of biology, work that later earned a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Transformers and language models

In 2017, a paper with what I think is one of the most delightfully cocky titles in computer science, "Attention Is All You Need," introduced a new kind of network called the transformer. Nearly every chatbot today is built on it.

The key idea is called attention. Think about the sentence "The dog chased the ball because it was excited." What does "it" mean? You know it's the dog, because you connected "it" back to "dog" and not "ball." Attention gives a network a way to do something similar: for each word, it can look at all the other words in the passage and work out which ones matter for understanding it.

Transformers also happen to be very efficient to train on lots of computers at once, which turned out to be just as important.

A large language model (LLM) is a giant transformer trained on one very simple-sounding task: predict what comes next. Given "The cat sat on the ___," what's the next word? Text gets broken into small pieces called tokens (a word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark), and the model learns to assign probabilities to what the next token will be. Then it does this across an enormous pile of text and code, using the same training loop from Lesson 4.

Here's the surprising part. To get really good at predicting the next token across that much text, a model ends up having to pick up grammar, facts, writing styles, how arguments are structured, how code works, &c. And researchers found that making the models bigger, and training them on more data with more computing power, kept making them better, often across lots of tasks at once. That pattern is called scaling, and it's a big part of why the last few years moved so fast.

One more ingredient turns a raw text-predictor into a helpful assistant. After the main training, developers do additional training using examples of good answers and human judgments about which responses are better. A well-known version is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which is the reinforcement learning from Lesson 2, with human preferences as the reward. That's a big part of why a chatbot answers your question politely instead of just rambling on in some random direction.

Why chatbots make things up

Now you know enough to understand the most famous failure of these tools. A language model produces plausible text, one token at a time. It's not looking facts up in a database. So it can produce a fake citation, a made-up quote, or a wrong date in exactly the same confident, fluent style it uses for true things. This is called hallucination.

It isn't really "lying," which would mean the model intends to deceive you. It's what happens when "what sounds right" and "what's true" come apart. Some chatbots can search the web or read documents you give them, which helps, but they can still misrepresent what a source says. So what can you do about it? Since the made-up stuff looks exactly like the real stuff, the only way to tell them apart is to check anything that matters. Open the citations. Check names, numbers, dates, and quotes. And for medical, legal, or money decisions, go to a qualified source.

A few habits make these tools much more useful:

  • Be specific. "Write about dogs" gets you mush. Say what you want, who it's for, how long it should be, and what tone to use.
  • Give context or an example if the style matters.
  • Ask for revisions. The first answer is a draft. "Shorter," "simpler," and "more formal" all work.
  • Treat the output as a starting point, not a finished, trustworthy answer, since, as we just saw, a model sounds every bit as confident when it's wrong.

The Week 9 assignment asks you to try all of this out for real: prompting, debugging, and catching a hallucination.

Try it: In any chatbot, type the beginning of a familiar saying, like "A penny saved is..." and see how it continues. Then ask it to finish the saying in a way that avoids the usual ending. How strongly does it get pulled toward the familiar pattern? What does that tell you about next-token prediction?

Getting better: tools and second chances

So what can be done about hallucination? Some of it improves on its own as models get more sophisticated: bigger, better-trained models make fewer of these mistakes. But the biggest improvements have come from a different direction. Instead of asking a model to produce an answer in one go, developers give it its own tools, like a search engine, a calculator, or a way to run code, and they give it multiple rounds to check its own work and fix it. A model that can look something up, notice that it got something wrong, and try again is much less likely to hand you a confident fake.

Models with those abilities are called agents, and they're much closer to how you work than the pure chatbot mode is. Think about it: asking a plain chatbot a question is a bit like someone asking you a hard question and making you answer instantly, off the top of your head, without any time to think. You'd get some things wrong too! Give yourself a library, a calculator, and a chance to check your answer before handing it in, and you'd do much better. That's the idea behind agents, and it's what the next lesson is about.
