Lesson 6 - Backpropagation: Assigning the Blame

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain the problem backpropagation solves: figuring out which way to turn every knob in a huge network.
  • Describe backpropagation as passing the "blame" for a mistake backward through the layers.
  • Put the whole picture together: backprop finds the downhill direction, gradient descent takes the step, repeat.

We've now got every piece but one. We have a network - a giant pile of knobs. We have gradient descent - the rule that says "turn each knob in whatever direction lowers the wrongness." The missing piece is a practical one, and it's a doozy: in a network with millions of knobs, how does each knob find out which way is downhill for it?

Why you can't just check each knob

The obvious approach is hopeless. You could wiggle one knob, measure whether the wrongness went up or down, write that down, set it back, and move to the next knob. For a model with a few hundred billion knobs, that's a few hundred billion measurements for a single step downhill - and you need millions of steps. The sun would burn out first. We need a way to figure out the downhill direction for every knob at once. That way is backpropagation (or "backprop" to its friends).

The kitchen at the end of a bad night

Here's the picture to hold onto. A restaurant sends out a dish and it comes back wrong - too salty. The head chef doesn't just shout "be better!" at everyone. She traces the mistake backward down the line. The plating station was fine. The sauce station added too much salt - a lot of the blame lands there. And the prep cook over-salted the stock that fed the sauce, so some of the blame passes further back to them. Each station ends up with a fair share of the blame, and - this is the key - each share comes with a specific instruction: "use this much less salt next time."

Backprop is exactly that, run on a neural network. Start at the output, where you can measure the mistake directly. Then ripple that blame backward, layer by layer: each connection gets a share of the blame based on how much it contributed to the error, and the blame flows back through it to the layer before. By the time you reach the front of the network, every single knob has been handed its own personal instruction: nudge this much, in this direction. And the wonderful part is that the math to do this works out to be fast - one sweep backward through the network hands you the downhill direction for all million knobs together.

Watch the blame flow backward

Here's a tiny network - one input, two "hidden" neurons, one output - with a knob on each connection. It's trying to turn the input into a target number, and right now it's wrong. Press "Learn one round" and watch: first the blame ripples backward (the arrows flash red - thicker and redder where a knob is more to blame), then every knob gets nudged, and the output creeps toward the target. Press it again and again, or hit Run, and watch the error melt away. Drag the Target to give it a new goal and watch it re-learn.

Play and wonder: run it until the error hits zero, then drag the Target way up or down. Watch the output miss badly again, and watch the blame come roaring back to push the knobs to new settings. That loop - guess, measure the miss, blame the knobs backward, nudge - is the entire act of training, just with four knobs instead of four hundred billion.

The whole machine, in one breath

Step back and look at what we've built across these lessons. Training any neural network, from this toy up to the giant behind ChatGPT, is just this loop spinning millions of times:

  1. Guess. Run an example through the network and see what comes out.
  2. Measure the miss. Compare the output to the right answer - that's the wrongness.
  3. Blame the knobs (backprop). Ripple that miss backward to find which way each knob should move.
  4. Nudge (gradient descent). Take one small step downhill on every knob.
  5. Repeat. A few million times.

That's it. That's the engine. Everything else - the size of the network, the kind of data, the clever tweaks - is decoration on this one loop. When you hear that a model was "trained on the internet for three months," picture this loop running, quietly, the whole time.
