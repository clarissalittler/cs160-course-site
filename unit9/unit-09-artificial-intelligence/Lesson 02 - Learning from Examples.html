Lesson 2 - Learning from Examples

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how machine learning flips the usual approach to programming — from writing rules to showing examples
  • Describe what "training data" is and why labeled examples matter
  • Tell the difference between supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning in plain language
  • Explain, in everyday terms, what "finding the pattern" actually means inside the machine

The big flip

Back in the Python units, you wrote programs the old-fashioned way — you spelled out the rules yourself. If the balance is below zero, print a warning. If the score is above 700, approve the loan. if this, elif that, else the other thing. You were the one deciding every step, and the computer just did exactly what you said.

That works beautifully for some things. Tax forms, for instance — the rules are written down, they're fussy but finite, and a human could (in principle) follow them too. You can spell those out.

But now try something that sounds way easier: write the rules that tell a cat photo apart from a dog photo.

Go ahead, give it a shot. "A dog has pointy ears" — except lots of dogs have floppy ears, and plenty of cats have pointy ones. "A dog is bigger" — not next to a Great Dane and a housecat, and definitely not next to a Chihuahua. Fur color, whiskers, tail shape — for every rule you invent, there's a thousand exceptions, weird angles, bad lighting, a corgi in a hot dog costume at a Portland dog park. You will never finish. The rules just aren't writeable.

Same story with sorting spam from real email, or flagging a fraudulent credit-card charge. These tasks are fuzzy. The pattern is real — you can see it instantly — but you can't hand it over as a tidy list of if statements.

So we stop writing rules

Here's the whole idea behind machine learning: don't write the rules at all. Instead, show the computer thousands of examples — cat photos labeled "cat," dog photos labeled "dog" — and let it figure out the pattern on its own.

Those examples are called the training data. The more of them you have, and the better they're labeled, the more the computer has to learn from. You're not programming the answer anymore — you're handing over a giant pile of "here's what right looks like" and letting the machine sort out the rest.

It's honestly a lot like how a little kid learns the word "dog." Nobody sits a toddler down and recites a definition about snout length and ear angles. The kid just sees dog after dog after dog while somebody says "dog!" — a beagle on a walk, a cartoon dog, the neighbor's enormous slobbery good boy — and somewhere in there the pattern clicks. Ask them to define "dog" and they'll shrug. But they'll never miss one. That's learning from examples.

Two main flavors (and a bonus)

When people talk about machine learning, they're usually talking about one of two styles, and it comes down to a simple question: does each example come with the right answer attached?

  • Supervised learning is learning with an answer key. Every example is labeled with the correct answer — "cat," "spam," "fraud" — and the model practices predicting that label, checking its guess, and getting a little better each time. Most of the everyday AI you bump into works this way.
  • Unsupervised learning is learning with no answer key. You just hand the computer a heap of data and ask it to find structure on its own — to group similar things together. A company might feed in customer purchase histories and let the model cluster shoppers into natural groups, without anyone telling it ahead of time what those groups should be. Nobody says "this one's right" — the model just notices what hangs together.

And there's a third flavor worth a single sentence: reinforcement learning is learning from reward and feedback, like training a dog with treats — try something, get a "yes!" or a "nope," and adjust. We'll see it come back later when we talk about how chatbots get tuned to be helpful.

But what does "find the pattern" actually mean?

This is the part that sounds like magic, so let's deflate it a little, because the rest of this unit depends on it.

Inside the model there are a bunch of internal numbers — think of them as settings, or little dials. When the model makes a guess, those numbers are what produce the guess. "Finding the pattern" just means adjusting those numbers, over and over, until the model's guesses line up with the examples you gave it. Wrong guess? Nudge the dials. Try again. Nudge again. That's the entire move, repeated a staggering number of times.

The simplest possible version of that — so simple you can watch every number change — is fitting a straight line through a scatter of dots. No cats, no spam, just dots and a line. And that turns out to be a real, honest machine that learns, which is exactly where we're headed next.

Try it: Think of a task you can do easily but could never write down as step-by-step rules — recognizing a friend's voice on the phone before they say their name, telling whether a joke is actually funny, knowing when a song has a good beat. Jot one down. That kind of task — easy to do, impossible to spell out — is exactly what machine learning is for.

Coming up next: Fitting a Line — the simplest machine that learns. We'll start with a handful of dots, draw a line through them, and actually watch those internal numbers get adjusted until the line fits. Once you've seen it happen on dots, the rest of AI is just the same trick at a much bigger scale.
