Unit 9 Summary

Wrap up

This unit opened up part of AI's black box. "AI" has meant many different things over the years, from searching through chess moves to chatbots, but most of what people mean by it today is machine learning: a model full of adjustable numbers, trained by measuring how wrong it is and nudging those numbers to be a little less wrong, over and over. You watched that happen with a one-knob tip model and a tiny neural network. Scale the same loop up with enormous data and computing power and you get deep learning, transformers, chatbots, and agents.

All of it is still built, trained, tested, and deployed by people. So the three questions from the start of the course aren't only for the last lesson. You can ask who benefits, who's harmed, and who decides about the spam filter in your inbox just as much as about Project Glasswing.

What to carry forward

  • "AI" is more of a goal than one technology. Over the years it has meant search, hand-written rules, machine learning, and now large language models and agents, and most of the AI you run into every day is narrow, built for one particular kind of task.
  • You can picture the vocabulary as boxes inside boxes: AI contains machine learning, which contains neural networks, which contain deep learning.
  • Machine learning learns from examples instead of rules someone wrote by hand. What really counts is how well a model does on examples it has never seen (that's generalization), which is why the test data has to stay out of training.
  • Training is a loop: make a guess, measure how wrong it is, use backpropagation to work out which knobs are to blame, use gradient descent to nudge every knob a little way downhill, and repeat. The step size (the learning rate) matters, since steps that are too big or too small both cause trouble.
  • Deep learning took off around 2012, when big datasets, GPUs, and better methods all came together. Transformers (2017) use attention, and large language models are transformers trained to predict the next token.
  • A language model can hallucinate, because text that sounds plausible isn't the same as a fact somebody checked. So when the answer matters, check it somewhere else, the same way you'd double-check a friend who sounds very sure of themselves.
  • Agents are models using tools in a loop. That lets them take on much longer tasks than a chatbot can, and it also means their mistakes cost more, because they can take actions in the world.
  • The social questions run from narrow systems that pick up bias from historical data, to the risks of powerful agents (which is why the model behind Project Glasswing wasn't released to the public), to new questions about credit and understanding in fields like mathematics. Every one of them comes back to choices people make, which means none of the bad outcomes are inevitable.
