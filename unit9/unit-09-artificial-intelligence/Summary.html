Unit 9 Summary

Wrap up

This unit opened up part of AI's black box. "AI" has meant a lot of different things over the years, from searching through chess moves to chatbots, but most of what people mean by it today is machine learning: a model full of adjustable numbers, trained by measuring how wrong it is and nudging those numbers to be a little less wrong, over and over. You watched that happen with a one-knob tip model and a tiny neural network. Scale the same loop up with enormous data and computing power and you get deep learning, transformers, chatbots, and agents.

All of it is still built, trained, tested, and deployed by people. That's why the three questions from the start of the course matter here more than anywhere: who benefits, who's harmed, and who decides?

Summary

  • "AI" is a goal, not one technology. It has covered search, hand-written rules, machine learning, and now large language models and agents. Most everyday AI is narrow, built for particular tasks.
  • Boxes inside boxes: AI contains machine learning, which contains neural networks, which contains deep learning.
  • Machine learning learns from examples instead of hand-written rules. What counts is doing well on new examples (generalization), so test data stays out of training.
  • The training loop: guess, measure the wrongness, use backpropagation to assign the blame, use gradient descent to nudge every knob downhill, and repeat. The step size (learning rate) matters.
  • Deep learning took off around 2012 when big datasets, GPUs, and better methods came together. Transformers (2017) use attention, and large language models are transformers trained to predict the next token.
  • Language models can hallucinate: plausible-sounding text isn't the same as verified fact. Check anything that matters.
  • Agents are models using tools in a loop. They can do much longer tasks than a chatbot, and their mistakes cost more, because they can take actions.
  • Social impacts range from bias learned from historical data in narrow systems, to the risks of powerful agents (Project Glasswing), to questions about credit and understanding in fields like mathematics. They all come back to human choices, which means none of the bad outcomes are inevitable.
