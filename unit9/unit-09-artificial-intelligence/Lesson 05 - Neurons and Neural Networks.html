Lesson 5 - Neurons and Neural Networks

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe what a single artificial neuron does: combine weighted inputs and apply an activation function.
  • Explain how neurons stack into layers to form a neural network, and what the input, hidden, and output layers do.
  • Explain how a network's many adjustable weights connect to gradient descent.

We now have an optimization method: adjust parameters in a direction that reduces the loss. A line gave us two parameters. We will next examine a structure that can contain millions or billions of them: the neural network.

One artificial neuron

Biological neurons receive and transmit signals. An artificial neuron is a simplified mathematical model inspired by that idea. Its basic operation is:

  1. Take in some inputs (numbers).
  2. Multiply each input by a weight—an adjustable number that determines how strongly that input affects the result.
  3. Add up the results.
  4. Pass the total through an activation function, which determines the signal sent onward.

For an analogy, imagine deciding whether to walk to a food cart for lunch. Inputs might include hunger, the length of the line, and the cost. Giving each factor a different weight and combining the results produces a score that influences the decision. In an artificial neuron, changing the weights changes how the inputs affect its output.

Stacking neurons into a network

A network connects many neurons in layers, so that one layer's outputs become the next layer's inputs:

  • The input layer is the raw data - the pixels of a photo, the words of a sentence, the numbers in a spreadsheet.
  • The hidden layers transform one representation into another. In some image networks, earlier layers respond to simple features such as edges, while later layers combine features into more complex patterns. These representations emerge from training rather than from a programmer specifying each one.
  • The output layer gives the final answer - "92% cat, 8% dog," say.

A network with many hidden layers is called deep, which gives us the term deep learning. The important connection to the previous lessons is that the links between neurons have adjustable weights. Training a network means adjusting those weights, along with other parameters, to reduce a loss.

So how does it actually learn?

A network can have millions of parameters. Gradient descent requires a direction for each one, but testing parameters individually would be prohibitively slow. The efficient calculation used to obtain those directions is backpropagation.
