Lesson 5 - Neurons and Neural Networks

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe what a single artificial neuron does: weigh some inputs, add them up, and decide whether to fire.
  • Explain how neurons stack into layers to form a neural network, and what the input, hidden, and output layers do.
  • Explain why a whole network is "just a giant pile of knobs," and connect that back to gradient descent.

We've got the engine of learning - roll the knobs downhill to shrink the wrongness. A line gave us two knobs to play with. Now let's build the machine that modern AI actually uses, the one with millions or billions of knobs: the neural network. The good news is that it's built out of a piece so simple you already half-understand it.

One neuron: a tiny decision-maker

The brain has cells called neurons that take in signals and pass along a signal of their own. An artificial neuron is a cartoon of that idea, and honestly it's not much more than the line from last lesson. Here's the whole job of a single neuron:

  1. Take in some inputs (numbers).
  2. Multiply each input by a weight - a knob that says how much that input matters.
  3. Add up the results.
  4. If the total is high enough, fire (send a strong signal on); if not, stay quiet.

Picture deciding whether to walk to a food cart for lunch. Your inputs might be how hungry am I, how long is the line, and how broke am I. You weight them - hunger matters a lot, the line matters a little, your budget matters somewhere in between - add it all up, and if the total clears your personal bar, you go. That's a neuron. The weights are exactly the knobs from the last two lessons: turn them and you change how the neuron decides.

Stacking neurons into a network

One neuron makes one little decision. The magic comes from wiring lots of them together in layers, where each neuron's output becomes the next layer's input:

  • The input layer is the raw data - the pixels of a photo, the words of a sentence, the numbers in a spreadsheet.
  • The hidden layers in the middle are where the real work happens. Each neuron looks at what the layer before it noticed and builds something a little more sophisticated out of it. In an image network, an early layer might fire on tiny edges; the next combines edges into shapes; the next combines shapes into eyes and wheels; a later one combines those into "cat" or "car." Nobody programmed those steps - the network worked them out by tuning its knobs.
  • The output layer gives the final answer - "92% cat, 8% dog," say.

When a network has many hidden layers stacked up, we call it deep - that's where the term deep learning comes from, and we'll dig into it in the second half of the unit. For now, the one thing to hold onto is this: a neural network, however fancy, is just a big stack of those tiny weigh-add-fire neurons, and every connection between them has a weight. A network is, start to finish, a giant pile of knobs.

So how does it actually learn?

Here's the thing we've been circling. A network can have millions of knobs. Gradient descent says "turn each knob in whatever direction lowers the wrongness" - fine. But with a million knobs, how does the network figure out which way is downhill for each one without testing them one at a time (which would take approximately forever)? That clever piece of bookkeeping is the last gear in the machine, and it has a name: backpropagation.
