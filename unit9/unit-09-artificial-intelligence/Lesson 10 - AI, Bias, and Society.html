Lesson 10 - AI, Bias, and Society

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how an AI system can absorb human bias from its training data and then apply that bias at scale.
  • Distinguish measured disparities, reported deployment problems, and modeled mechanisms of bias.
  • Use the questions “Who benefits? Who is harmed? Who decides?” to examine an AI system.
  • Discuss AI's social risks without relying on either hype or panic.

At the start of this course, I introduced three recurring questions: Who benefits? Who is harmed? Who decides? They matter especially here. Understanding a neural network's mechanism is useful; understanding what a deployed system does to people is essential.

So let's start with the mechanism, because the rest follows from it.

Where the bias comes from

An AI model learns statistical patterns from data. Given many examples, it becomes better at predicting what tends to follow or accompany a particular input. The patterns it learns depend on the data, objectives, and design choices used to build it.

Much of that data comes from human activity: photographs, resumes, arrest records, and decades of text published online. It therefore contains patterns produced by unequal institutions and biased human decisions. A model can learn those patterns along with the useful ones.

An automated system can then apply a learned pattern rapidly and at large scale. Its numerical output may appear objective, but a model trained on biased records can reproduce or amplify their bias. Human choices remain present in the collection of data, definition of targets, selection of thresholds, and decision to deploy the system.

What the evidence shows

These examples show different kinds of evidence. Keep the finding separate from an explanation of why it happened.

Facial analysis. In Gender Shades (2018), Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru measured commercial gender-classification systems' error rates on a benchmark divided by skin type and binary gender labels. Errors were substantially higher for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men. This establishes a performance disparity on that benchmark. The commercial training data were not disclosed, so the study did not establish their demographic imbalance as the cause. The task also used binary gender labels; it does not establish a person's gender identity. Uneven data can contribute to bias, but that mechanism needs evidence in each case.

Hiring. Jeffrey Dastin's 2018 Reuters investigation described an experimental Amazon resume-screening tool trained on applications submitted over a decade, mostly from men. Sources reported that it penalized resumes containing terms such as “women's” and references to some women's colleges. This was a reported problem with an experimental tool, rather than a controlled study of all hiring systems. It illustrates how historical application data can shape a screening score.

Predictive policing. Historical incident counts can reflect where officers looked as well as where crime occurred. In Runaway Feedback Loops in Predictive Policing (2018), Danielle Ensign and colleagues used a mathematical model and simulations to study this mechanism: dispatching officers to an area can produce more officer-discovered incidents there, which then influence later dispatches. Their results show how a feedback loop can arise under the modeled conditions. They are not a finding that every crime-prediction or risk-scoring system behaves this way.

Across these examples, bias need not appear as an explicit instruction. It can enter through data and design decisions, then be obscured by the apparent neutrality of a numerical score.

Who gets to ask the hard questions?

That brings us to who decides, including who has the authority to question systems inside the organizations building them.

Timnit Gebru is a prominent AI ethics researcher who co-authored a 2021 paper titled "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots." The authors argue that fluent language generation can create an impression of understanding that the output alone does not establish. The paper warned about several harms of very large language models — bias absorbed from internet-scale training data, the heavy energy cost of training them, and the risk of mistaking fluent output for understanding.

Her departure from Google in December 2020, widely reported as a firing connected to disputes over that work, became a flashpoint. The episode raised a structural question: when an organization both builds and profits from a technology, who inside it is able to challenge the risks? This is one form of the question who decides?

The rest of the worry list

Bias is the throughline, but it's not the whole story. A few other real concerns, briefly:

  • Deepfakes and misinformation. Generative AI can fake convincing photos, voices, and video — which is a problem when "seeing is believing" stops being safe.
  • Surveillance. Cheap, automated face and behavior recognition makes it possible to watch a lot of people, all the time, for very little money.
  • Jobs and labor. Some tasks will be automated or reshaped. The benefits and disruptions will not necessarily be distributed to the same people.
  • Environmental footprint. Training and operating large models can consume substantial electricity and water for cooling, even though those costs are invisible at the point of use.
  • Consent and copyright. Training datasets may include text and images collected from the internet without each creator's explicit consent or compensation. Legal and cultural disputes over those practices continue.

From criticism to better choices

These examples do not mean that a technology is itself malicious. They show the consequences of human choices: what data to use, what objective to optimize, where to deploy a system, and who participates in those decisions.

Because these are choices, they can be challenged and changed. Possible safeguards include more representative data, clearer objectives, diverse design teams, testing before and after deployment, independent audits, avenues for appeal, and enforceable limits. When someone says, "The algorithm decided," we should ask which algorithm, trained on whose data, evaluated by what measure, and used for whose benefit.

Reflect: Choose an AI system you use, such as a recommendation feed, navigation app, chatbot, or photo-tagging tool. Apply the three questions. Who benefits from its present design? Who could be harmed if it is wrong, biased, or used at scale, and is that harm evenly distributed? Who decides how it behaves, and whose perspective may be missing? A tidy verdict is not required; the goal is to look beyond "the computer said so."
