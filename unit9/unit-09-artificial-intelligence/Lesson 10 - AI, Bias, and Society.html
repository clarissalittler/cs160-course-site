Lesson 10 - AI, Bias, and Society

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how an AI system can absorb human bias from its training data and then apply that bias at scale.
  • Describe real, documented examples of AI bias in areas like facial recognition, hiring, and criminal justice.
  • Use the three questions — who benefits, who's harmed, who decides? — to think critically about a piece of AI you actually use.
  • Talk about AI's social risks without sliding into either hype or panic.

Way back at the start of this course, I promised we'd keep coming back to three questions: who benefits, who's harmed, and who decides? This is the lesson where all of that lands. If there's one thing I want you to walk away with from Unit 9, it's not how a neural network works under the hood — it's how to think clearly about what these systems do to actual people.

So let's start with the mechanism, because the rest follows from it.

Where the bias comes from

Remember the basic idea: an AI model learns patterns from data. Show it millions of examples, and it gets good at predicting "what usually comes next" or "what usually goes with what." It has no opinions, no agenda, no idea what any of it means. It's pattern-matching, all the way down.

Here's the catch. The data came from us. Photos we took, resumes we wrote, arrests that were made, words we typed into the internet for thirty years. The model learned from us — from oceans of human data — so it picks up our patterns, including the ugly ones. Garbage in, garbage out, except the garbage is our own history.

And then it does something humans can't: it applies that pattern at massive scale, instantly, to millions of decisions — wrapped in a shiny coat of math that makes it feel objective. "The computer said so" sounds neutral. It feels like a fact. But a model that learned from a biased world will reproduce that bias, and now it's doing it faster and to more people than any single biased human ever could. The neutrality is an illusion. There's a person's choice behind every number.

Three places this has actually happened

These aren't hypotheticals. They're documented.

Facial recognition. In her "Gender Shades" research around 2018, computer scientist Joy Buolamwini tested commercial face-analysis systems and found they performed noticeably worse on darker-skinned faces and on women — with the largest error rates of all on darker-skinned women. Why? Largely because the photos these systems trained on skewed toward lighter-skinned men. The system wasn't "racist" in any conscious sense. It just learned best from the faces it saw most. But the consequences are real when that same tech gets used for unlocking phones, tagging photos, or — far more seriously — police identification.

Hiring. Some companies have experimented with AI tools to screen resumes, trained on who the company hired in the past. Sounds efficient — until you remember what it's actually learning. If a company historically hired mostly men for technical roles, a tool trained on that history can learn to favor resumes that look like those past hires. One widely reported case involved a system that reportedly downgraded resumes containing signals associated with women (like having attended a women's college). The tool wasn't told to discriminate. It learned to, from the data, and then dressed it up as a score.

Criminal justice. Risk-scoring tools — software that tries to predict whether someone will re-offend — and "predictive policing" systems both lean on historical crime data. But that data reflects where police already went and who they already arrested, not some clean ground truth about crime. Feed in biased history, and the model can quietly amplify it: sending more patrols to already-over-policed neighborhoods, which generates more arrests there, which "confirms" the prediction. A feedback loop wearing a lab coat.

Notice the pattern across all three. Nobody typed "be biased" into a keyboard. The bias rode in on the data, and the scale and the math made it look like objectivity.

Who gets to ask the hard questions?

That brings us to who decides — including who decides what's even allowed to be questioned inside the companies building this stuff.

Timnit Gebru is a prominent AI ethics researcher who co-authored a 2021 paper with the memorable title "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots." (A "stochastic parrot" is the paper's image for a large language model: something that can stitch together fluent-sounding language without understanding any of it.) The paper warned about several harms of very large language models — bias absorbed from internet-scale training data, the heavy energy cost of training them, and exactly that illusion of understanding we keep coming back to.

Her departure from Google in December 2020 — widely reported as a firing tied to that very work — became a flashpoint. The debate wasn't really about one person or one job. It was about a bigger question: when the people who build these systems also profit from them, who inside is free to raise the alarm? You can take whatever view you like on the specifics. But the structural question is worth sitting with, because it's who decides in its purest form.

The rest of the worry list

Bias is the throughline, but it's not the whole story. A few other real concerns, briefly:

  • Deepfakes and misinformation. Generative AI can fake convincing photos, voices, and video — which is a problem when "seeing is believing" stops being safe.
  • Surveillance. Cheap, automated face and behavior recognition makes it possible to watch a lot of people, all the time, for very little money.
  • Jobs and labor. Some work will be automated or reshaped. Who absorbs that disruption — and who captures the savings — is a who-benefits-who's-harmed question if there ever was one.
  • Environmental footprint. Training and running the biggest models burns a striking amount of electricity and water for cooling. That cost is real, even if it's invisible from where you're sitting.
  • Consent and copyright. These models were trained on huge piles of text and images scraped from the internet — much of it made by people who never agreed to it and weren't paid. The courts and the culture are still sorting out what's fair.

This isn't a horror story

Let me be clear, because it's easy to read all that and conclude "AI is evil." It isn't. None of these problems are the technology being malicious — the technology doesn't want anything. They're the downstream result of human choices: what data we used, what we told the system to optimize for, who was in the room when those calls got made, and who wasn't.

That's actually the hopeful part. Choices can be made differently. Better data, clearer goals, more diverse teams, real testing for bias before deployment, laws that set limits — these all work, and they're already changing how some of this gets built. An informed public is part of that pressure. Which is the whole reason a general-education computer science course bothers to teach you this: so that when someone says "the algorithm decided," you know to ask which algorithm, trained on whose data, optimized for whose benefit.

Reflect: Pick one piece of AI you actually rely on — a recommendation feed, a navigation app, autocomplete, a chatbot, photo tagging, whatever. Walk it through the three questions. Who benefits from it working the way it does (and who profits)? Who could be harmed if it's wrong, or biased, or used at scale — and is the harm spread evenly? Who decides how it behaves, and who was probably not in that room? You don't need a tidy verdict. The point is to practice looking past "the computer said so."
