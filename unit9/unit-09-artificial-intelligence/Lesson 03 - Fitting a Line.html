Lesson 3 - Fitting a Line: the Simplest Learning

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what it means to fit a line to data, and why a line is useful for spotting a trend and making predictions.
  • Describe a line using two adjustable parameters: a slope and an intercept.
  • Explain what makes one line a better fit than another, using the idea of the gaps between the line and the data.

Last lesson, we described machine learning as finding patterns by adjusting internal numbers. We can make that idea concrete with a simple learning problem: fitting a line. The example is small enough for us to inspect every part of the process.

Dots with a trend

Imagine you jotted down, for a handful of study sessions, how many hours you studied and what you scored on the quiz:

  • 1 hour → 60
  • 2 hours → 70
  • 3 hours → 80
  • 4 hours → 90

Plot those as points—hours across the bottom and score up the side—and a trend appears: the points move up and to the right in a straight-line pattern. A line summarizes that pattern and supports predictions between the observed cases. For 2.5 hours, for example, this simple line would predict a score near 75. Predictions beyond the observed range require more caution, because the relationship may not continue indefinitely.

This method is called linear regression: choosing a line that fits a set of observations according to a defined measure of error. It is widely used in data science, and it gives us a manageable first example of a model that learns parameters from data.

A line has two parameters

A straight line has exactly two adjustable parameters, which we can continue to picture as knobs:

  • The slope: how steeply the line tilts. In our quiz data, each additional hour corresponds to about 10 additional points.
  • The intercept: where the line crosses the vertical axis. Our line has an intercept near 50, although interpreting that as a real score for zero hours would require evidence at that end of the range.

Those two numbers determine one straight line. Adjusting the slope changes its tilt; adjusting the intercept moves it up or down.

Try it: Picture a horizontal line across the four points: a slope of zero. Now imagine increasing the slope. The line moves closer to the points until it reaches a particularly good fit. In the next lesson, an interactive model will make this adjustment visible.

What makes one line "better"?

Our eyes can recognize a plausible fit, but a computer needs a numerical objective. For each point, we measure the residual: the vertical gap between the observed value and the line's prediction. A common method squares those residuals and adds them to produce a single loss score. In these lessons, we will also call that score the model's "wrongness."

A line near the points has a smaller loss than one far from them. "Find the best-fitting line" therefore becomes a measurable goal: find the slope and intercept that minimize the loss.

A computer can evaluate that number and search for parameter settings that reduce it. The next lesson explains how it chooses the direction of each adjustment.
