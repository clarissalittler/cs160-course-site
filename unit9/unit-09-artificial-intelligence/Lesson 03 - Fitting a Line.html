Lesson 3 - Fitting a Line: the Simplest Learning

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what it means to fit a line to data, and why a line is useful for spotting a trend and making predictions.
  • Describe a line as a thing with two "knobs" - a slope and a starting point.
  • Explain what makes one line a better fit than another, using the idea of the gaps between the line and the data.

Last lesson we said machine learning is about finding patterns in examples by adjusting some internal numbers. That's a bit abstract, so let's make it as concrete as it can possibly get, with the oldest and simplest learning machine there is: fitting a line. Don't let the plainness fool you - the exact same idea, scaled up, is what trains ChatGPT. We're just going to watch it happen somewhere small enough to see.

Dots with a trend

Imagine you jotted down, for a handful of study sessions, how many hours you studied and what you scored on the quiz:

  • 1 hour → 60
  • 2 hours → 70
  • 3 hours → 80
  • 4 hours → 90

Plot those as dots - hours across the bottom, score up the side - and your eye immediately catches the trend: they march up and to the right, roughly in a straight line. That straight line is doing something genuinely useful. It captures the pattern in a single, simple shape, and it lets you predict: studied 5 hours? The line says somewhere around 100. Studied 2.5? About 75. We've never seen those exact cases, but the line fills them in.

This is called linear regression - a fancy name for "draw the best straight line through the dots." It's one of the workhorses of data science, used to predict house prices, sales, temperatures, you name it. And it's the perfect first example of a machine that learns, because the "pattern" it learns is just a line, and a line is about as simple as patterns get.

A line has two knobs

Remember the knobs from last lesson - the dials a model adjusts? A straight line has exactly two of them:

  • The slope: how steeply the line tilts. A big slope shoots up fast; a small slope is nearly flat. (In our quiz data, every extra hour adds about 10 points - that's the slope.)
  • The starting point (where the line crosses the left edge, sometimes called the intercept): how high up the line begins before the slope takes over. (Our line looks like it starts around 50 - the score for zero hours.)

That's the whole line: pick those two numbers and you've pinned down exactly one straight line. Turn the slope knob and the line tilts; turn the starting-point knob and the whole line slides up or down. Every possible straight line in the world is just some setting of these two knobs.

Try it: picture a flat, horizontal line laid across those four dots (slope zero - the "studying does nothing" guess). Now slowly tilt it up. As you tilt, the line swings closer to the dots. There's one tilt where it hugs them best - and that's the line we're after. Hold that picture; in the next lesson you'll get to do exactly this with your hands on the controls.

What makes one line "better"?

Your eye can spot a good fit, but a computer needs an actual number to chase. So here's the trick that powers everything ahead: for any line, look at each dot and measure the gap between the dot and the line - how far off the line's prediction is from what really happened. Add up all those gaps, and you get a single score for how badly the line misses. Call it the line's wrongness.

A line that slices right through the dots has tiny gaps and a tiny wrongness. A line that wanders off has big gaps and a big wrongness. So "find the best-fitting line" becomes a crisp, countable goal: find the slope and starting point that make the total wrongness as small as possible.

And that's a question a computer can actually attack. It doesn't have your eye, but it can absolutely measure a number and hunt for the knob settings that shrink it. How it hunts - how it knows which way to turn each knob without just guessing forever - is one of the most beautiful ideas in the whole field, and it's the entire next lesson.
