Lesson 7 - AI and Society

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how narrow AI systems have absorbed human bias from their data, using specific, well-documented cases.
  • Describe some risks of powerful AI agents, and explain why Anthropic chose not to release the model behind Project Glasswing.
  • Explain the concerns mathematicians have raised about AI and mathematical research.
  • Use "Who benefits? Who's harmed? Who decides?" to think through an AI system without falling into hype or panic.

Back at the very start of the course, I gave you three questions: Who benefits? Who's harmed? Who decides? We've used them on file formats, on the internet, on if-statements, and on data. This is where they matter most.

In Unit 6, we looked at Robodebt, a rule that ran on everyone and got many people wrong. That rule was written by people, and it wasn't AI at all. Machine learning adds a twist: now the rule is learned from data, which means nobody wrote it down, and sometimes nobody can easily say what it is.

Fair warning: this lesson spends most of its time on problems. That's not because I think the future of AI is doomed! It's because you can't steer around a problem until somebody names it. Almost every worry in this lesson is about a possible future, and for almost every one of them, there are people working hard to make sure it doesn't happen.

Old problems: bias in narrow AI

A model learns patterns from its training data. Most of that data comes from human history, like photos, résumés, arrest records, and decades of text from the internet. That history has unfairness baked into it, and a model can learn the unfair patterns right along with the useful ones. Then it can apply them very fast, to huge numbers of people, behind a number that looks objective.

Here are three well-known cases. Each one is a different kind of evidence, so pay attention to what each one actually shows.

Faces. In the Gender Shades study (2018), Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial face-analysis systems that guessed a person's gender from a photo. The systems made far more mistakes on darker-skinned women than on lighter-skinned men. That's a measured difference in performance. The companies didn't disclose their training data, so the study couldn't prove why it happened, but it made the problem impossible to ignore. And here's the encouraging part: a follow-up study about a year later found that the companies named in the original audit had shrunk those error gaps, so measuring the problem out loud seems to have helped get it fixed.

Hiring. A 2018 Reuters investigation reported that Amazon had built an experimental résumé-screening tool trained on ten years of past applications, most of them from men. According to the report, the tool learned to downgrade résumés that included the word "women's," as in "women's chess club captain." Nobody wrote "prefer men" anywhere; the model picked it up from the history it was trained on. (The good news: people caught it, and Amazon dropped the tool.)

Policing. Crime data partly reflects where police already looked, not just where crime happened. In a 2018 paper, Danielle Ensign and colleagues used math and simulations to show how a prediction system could create a feedback loop: send officers to a neighborhood, record more incidents there, and the system sends even more officers next time. That's a model of a mechanism, not proof that every real system works this way, but it shows how a "neutral" prediction can feed on itself.

There's also a question of who gets to raise these problems. Timnit Gebru co-wrote a 2021 paper, "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots," warning about the risks of ever-larger language models. Her departure from Google in December 2020, widely reported as a firing connected to disputes over that work, raised a hard question: when a company both builds a technology and profits from it, who inside it is allowed to challenge it?

New problems: powerful agents

Bias is an old problem, and people have gotten much better at testing for it. Lesson 6 showed how fast agents' abilities are growing, and some of the ways they still stumble. Scale those up, and you get the main worries people have about powerful agents:

  • Misuse. An agent that can find security holes for defenders can also find them for attackers. An agent that can write a convincing email can write ten thousand personalized scam emails. (Unit 10 is about the receiving end of this!)
  • Mistakes at scale. The stumbles from Lesson 6 (errors piling up, shortcuts, getting tricked) matter much more when an agent is running a company's systems, or when thousands of agents are running at once.
  • Losing oversight. If agents work much faster than people can check their work, and on tasks most people can't evaluate, it gets harder and harder for humans to tell whether the system is doing what we actually want. A big part of AI safety research is about this problem.

The Hugging Face incident from Lesson 6 added one more twist. When Hugging Face's responders asked leading AI models to help them analyze the attack, the models refused, because their safety rules couldn't tell someone defending a system from someone attacking it. The team ended up switching to an open-weight model running on its own computers. Safety rules that block attackers can also block defenders, which is one more version of the question who decides?

None of these are inevitable, and you can see that in how people respond to them. These worries are the reasons agents get limited permissions, the reasons companies test models before releasing them, and the reasons a whole research field exists to keep powerful systems understandable and under human control.

A case study: Project Glasswing

You met Project Glasswing in Lesson 6: Anthropic's unreleased model, Claude Mythos Preview, that could find and exploit software vulnerabilities better than all but the most skilled humans, and found thousands of serious flaws in every major operating system and web browser. For this lesson, the interesting part is what the company decided to do about it.

That's exciting and a little scary at the same time. Every one of those bugs is something a criminal or a hostile government could have used. So instead of releasing the model to the public, Anthropic gave access only to a group of organizations that maintain critical software, including the Linux Foundation and big tech, security, and finance companies, so they could find and fix the holes first. The bet is that defenders can get a head start before tools this capable become widely available.

And honestly, I find that a hopeful story! One of the first big things anyone did with a model this capable was use it to make everyone's software safer. Within about six weeks, partners reported finding more than ten thousand vulnerabilities so they could be fixed, including in free, open-source software that the whole internet depends on.

The care makes more sense when you remember the system-card incident from Lesson 6, where an early version of this same model escaped a locked-down test computer and went further than it was asked. A model that capable, in the wrong hands or without enough testing, is exactly what the people worried about powerful agents have in mind.

Glasswing raises all three of our questions at once. Who benefits? Hopefully everyone who uses software, which is everyone. Who's harmed? Attackers, if it works; everyone, if a model like this gets into the wrong hands. And who decides?

In this case, a private company decided on its own which organizations get access to a very powerful tool, and when. Some critics have pointed out that this concentrates power in very few hands. Whether that was the right call, and who should get to make calls like it in the future, is a real open question.

What happens to mathematics?

You saw in Lesson 6 that AI systems have started solving real open math problems. You might expect mathematicians to be thrilled, and some of them are! Others are asking hard questions about what this means for their field, and some have organized about it.

One group calls itself the Association for Human Mathematics (AHM). Its position is right there in the name: mathematics is a human activity. The group wants to protect it from the risks AI brings, and to keep the mathematical community independent of the companies building these systems. Here are the main concerns it raises:

  • Understanding is the point. The AHM argues that "mathematical progress requires human expertise, cultivated through writing and careful thinking." A proof isn't just a stamp that says "true." Working through one is how people come to understand why something is true, and that understanding is what the next generation of ideas gets built on. When machines produce mathematics and other machines consume it, the AHM warns, it can "undermine the intellectual foundations of our field."
  • Students. If an AI can do all the exercises, students may never build the deep understanding that makes someone a mathematician. (This one isn't only about math! It's the same reason the Week 9 assignment asks you to test and explain AI-written code instead of just pasting it in.)
  • Journals and checking. Math journals are getting a flood of papers written partly by AI, and the people who carefully check papers for a living can't keep up.
  • Math as advertising. The AHM points out that the AI industry "has chosen mathematics to advertise the capabilities of their products." A famous solved problem makes a great headline for a company, whether or not mathematicians learn anything from it.

That last point is what the AHM had in mind when it responded to the announcements you saw in Lesson 6. The Navier–Stokes claim got big headlines before experts could check it, and mathematicians who had been working on closely related ideas raised questions about credit. And when OpenAI released more than 700 unreviewed manuscripts all at once, the AHM called the release "a demonstration of power" rather than scholarship, and urged mathematicians to stop collaborating with the company.

Not every mathematician agrees with the AHM, and plenty are excited about AI as a research tool. Some already use AI systems as a kind of tireless collaborator, trying out ideas or turning a proof into Lean so a computer can check every step, while keeping the human understanding front and center. Others have taken middle-ground positions. The Leiden Declaration, endorsed by the International Mathematical Union, mostly asks mathematicians to disclose how they use AI and to stay responsible for anything published under their name.

So where does that leave things? Honestly, unsettled. Mathematicians are deciding right now what norms they want: how to credit AI help, how to review AI-assisted papers, and how to teach so that students still build real understanding. The AHM's questions also reach way beyond math. In any field where AI starts doing some of the work (including programming, the one closest to this course), you get to ask: what's the work for, who gets the credit, how do we know it's right, and who gets to decide?

A few more things to keep an eye on

  • Deepfakes and scams. Fake photos, voices, and video are cheap to make now. Unit 10 is all about protecting yourself from this.
  • Jobs. Some tasks will be automated or reshaped, and new kinds of work will show up too. The big question is who shares in the benefits.
  • Consent and copyright. Training data often includes writing and art collected from the internet without asking the people who made it. The legal fights over this are still going on.
  • Power and energy. Training and running these models takes a great deal of electricity in total, and new data centers can put real strain on local power grids. Where they get built, and who pays for the infrastructure, are local decisions that deserve attention.

What we can do

None of this means AI is evil, or that it's magic, and none of the scary versions of these stories are guaranteed. Everything in this lesson comes back to human choices: what data to use, what to optimize, what to release, who gets access, and who's in the room when those decisions get made.

And because they're choices, they can be made well. Better data, independent testing before and after a system is deployed, outside audits, ways for people to appeal a decision, limits on what agents are allowed to do, and good laws are all options, and people are working on every one of them. You saw it in this lesson: an audit that got face analysis fixed, a biased tool that got caught and dropped, a dangerous capability that got pointed at defense first. So the next time someone says "the AI decided," you'll know what to ask: which system, trained on what, tested how, deployed by whom, and for whose benefit? You don't have to build AI to ask those questions, and the more people who ask them, the harder they are for the people deploying these systems to ignore.

Reflect: Pick an AI system you use or have heard about, like the feed that picks your next video or a chatbot you've asked for help. Apply the three questions. Who benefits from how it's designed right now? Who could be harmed if it's wrong, biased, or misused, and is that harm spread evenly? Who decides how it behaves, and whose perspective might be missing? You don't need a tidy verdict. The goal is to get past "the computer said so."
