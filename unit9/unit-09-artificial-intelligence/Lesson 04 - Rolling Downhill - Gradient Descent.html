Lesson 4 - Rolling Downhill: Gradient Descent

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain "learning" as the act of adjusting numbers to make a mistake smaller.
  • Describe gradient descent using the idea of feeling your way downhill to the lowest point.
  • Explain what the learning rate (step size) does, and why too big or too small both cause trouble.

Last lesson we fit a line to some dots, and I waved my hands a little about how the computer finds the right line. It picks two numbers - a slope and a starting point - and somehow lands on the pair that fits best. This lesson is about that "somehow," because it turns out to be the single most important idea in all of modern AI. When people say a neural network "learns," or that a model was "trained," this is the thing they're talking about. Once it clicks, a huge amount of the mystery drains out of ChatGPT, image generators, the whole business.

Here's the one sentence to hang onto: learning is just adjusting numbers, over and over, to make a mistake smaller. That's it. Everything else is detail.

Knobs and wrongness

Think of any model as a machine with knobs on the side. Each knob is a number you can dial up or down. A line has two knobs. A big language model has hundreds of billions of them. Turning the knobs changes the answers the machine gives.

Now, for any setting of the knobs, we can ask: how wrong is this machine right now? We measure it, get a single number - call it the wrongness - and the whole goal of learning is to turn the knobs until that number is as small as it can get.

Let's make that concrete with the smallest possible example. Suppose we're trying to predict the tip someone leaves from the size of their bill, and we believe tip is just some fraction of the bill:

predicted tip = knob × bill

One knob. Here's the real data from three tables:

BillActual tip
$10$2
$20$4
$30$6

The machine doesn't know it yet, but the knob "should" be 0.2 (everyone tipped 20%). Let's watch it find that out without being told.

Feeling our way downhill

Say the machine starts with a bad guess: knob = 0.5. Its predictions, and how far off each one is:

BillPredicted (0.5 × bill)ActualGap
$10$5$23
$20$10$46
$30$15$69

Add up the gaps: 3 + 6 + 9 = 18. That's our wrongness. Pretty bad - every prediction is way too high, which tells us the knob is cranked up too far. So we nudge it down and measure again:

KnobTotal wrongness
0.518
0.412
0.36
0.20

Each step downhill, the wrongness drops, until at 0.2 it hits zero and we've nailed it. Notice we never solved anything - we just kept asking "does nudging this way make things less wrong?" and followed the answer. That's the whole move.

Here's the picture worth burning into your memory. Imagine standing on a mountainside in thick fog. You want to reach the lowest point in the valley, but you can't see it - you can only feel the ground right under your feet. So you feel which way slopes downhill, take a step that direction, and repeat. Step after step, you end up at the bottom, even though you never once saw the whole landscape.

That is gradient descent, and it's exactly what we just did. The valley floor is the lowest wrongness. Your position on the hill is the current knob setting. "Feeling which way is downhill" is checking whether nudging the knob up or down makes the wrongness shrink. ("Gradient" is just a fancy word for the direction of the slope; "descent" because we're heading down.)

Try it in your head: our knob started at 0.5 and the right answer was 0.2. What if it had started at 0.1 instead - too low? The predictions would come out too small, so the gaps would push the knob in the other direction. Which way would gradient descent nudge the knob: up or down? (It would feel the downhill slope and step up toward 0.2 - downhill in wrongness can mean turning a knob up.)

How big a step? The learning rate

There's one dial we haven't talked about: how big a step do you take each time? This is called the learning rate, and getting it wrong is the most common way training goes sideways.

  • Steps too big: you take a giant leap downhill, sail clear over the bottom of the valley, and land partway up the other side. Next step you overshoot back again. You bounce back and forth across the valley and never settle.
  • Steps too small: you inch downhill a hair at a time. You'll get there eventually, but it might take a million tiny steps - which, when each step costs real computer time, can mean days instead of minutes.
  • Steps just right: big enough to make real progress, small enough to settle gently into the bottom.

A lot of the "art" in training AI is picking a good learning rate - not too greedy, not too timid.

From one knob to a billion

Our tip example had a single knob, so "downhill" was just left or right. A real neural network has billions of knobs, so the landscape isn't a simple valley - it's a wild, many-dimensioned terrain no human could ever picture. But here's the beautiful part: the recipe doesn't change at all. Feel which way is downhill, take a step, repeat. The computer does this for all billion knobs at once, millions of times over, and slowly the whole machine slides toward "less wrong." That, start to finish, is what "training a model" means.

The one piece we've glossed over is how a network with billions of knobs figures out which way is downhill for each knob without testing them one at a time (which would take forever). That clever bit of bookkeeping has a name - backpropagation - and it's the next lesson.
