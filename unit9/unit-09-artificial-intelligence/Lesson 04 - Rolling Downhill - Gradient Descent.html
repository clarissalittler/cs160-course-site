Lesson 4 - Rolling Downhill: Gradient Descent

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain "learning" as the act of adjusting numbers to make a mistake smaller.
  • Describe gradient descent using the idea of feeling your way downhill to the lowest point.
  • Explain what the learning rate (step size) does, and why too big or too small both cause trouble.

Last lesson, we left one question unresolved: how does a computer find a line with a low loss? It must adjust two parameters—a slope and an intercept—and decide which changes improve the fit. This lesson introduces gradient descent, a method central to training many machine-learning models.

The central idea is simple: training adjusts parameters repeatedly to reduce a measured error.

Knobs and wrongness

Think of a model as a machine with numerical parameters, or knobs. A line has two; a large neural network may have billions. Changing the parameters changes the model's output.

Now, for any setting of the knobs, we can ask: how wrong is this machine right now? We measure it, get a single number - call it the wrongness - and the whole goal of learning is to turn the knobs until that number is as small as it can get.

Let's make that concrete with the smallest possible example. Suppose we're trying to predict the tip someone leaves from the size of their bill, and we believe tip is just some fraction of the bill:

predicted tip = knob × bill

One knob. Here's the real data from three tables:

BillActual tip
$10$2
$20$4
$30$6

For these three observations, the best knob setting is 0.2: each recorded tip was 20% of the bill. We can watch the model approach that value without being given it directly.

Feeling our way downhill

Say the machine starts with a bad guess: knob = 0.5. Its predictions, and how far off each one is:

BillPredicted (0.5 × bill)ActualGap
$10$5$23
$20$10$46
$30$15$69

Add the absolute gaps: 3 + 6 + 9 = 18. That is the loss measure used in this small example. Every prediction is too high, so we reduce the knob and measure again:

KnobTotal wrongness
0.518
0.412
0.36
0.20

At each step, the loss drops until it reaches zero at 0.2. Rather than solving for the answer directly, the process repeatedly asks which local change will reduce the loss.

A useful picture is a person standing on a mountainside in thick fog. The person cannot see the whole landscape, but can feel the local slope, take a step downhill, and repeat. Those local steps can lead toward a low point without requiring a complete view of the terrain.

That is the intuition behind gradient descent. The landscape represents the loss for different parameter settings, and the current position represents the current setting. The gradient describes the local direction and steepness of increase, so moving in the opposite direction lowers the loss.

Try it: Our knob started at 0.5, while the best setting was 0.2. Suppose it began at 0.1 instead. The predictions would be too small. Would gradient descent move the knob up or down? It would move it up toward 0.2: moving downhill in loss can require increasing a parameter.

How big a step? The learning rate

One choice remains: how large should each step be? This value is the learning rate, and it strongly affects training.

  • Steps that are too large can overshoot a low point and oscillate or diverge.
  • Steps that are too small may improve the model but require many costly iterations.
  • A suitable step size makes steady progress without repeatedly overshooting.

Choosing and adjusting a useful learning rate is therefore an important part of training.

From one knob to a billion

Our tip example has one parameter, so "downhill" is left or right. A neural network has many parameters, producing a high-dimensional loss landscape that we cannot picture directly. The basic pattern remains: estimate a useful direction, update the parameters, and repeat. The calculations and training choices are much more involved, but the optimization principle is recognizable.

We have not yet explained how a network finds a direction for each parameter efficiently. That calculation is called backpropagation.
