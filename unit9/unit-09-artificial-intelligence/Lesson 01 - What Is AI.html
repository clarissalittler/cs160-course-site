Lesson 1 - What Is AI?

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain what people mean by "artificial intelligence," and why the word has covered so many different technologies.
  • Describe a few big eras in the history of AI: search and logic, expert systems, machine learning, and large language models.
  • Distinguish narrow AI from the contested idea of artificial general intelligence (AGI).
  • Ask "what kind, and for what task?" when something is described as AI.

So what is AI, anyway?

Here's an honest but slightly annoying answer: "AI" isn't one technology. It's a goal. Artificial intelligence is the project of getting computers to do things we'd call intelligent if a person did them, like playing chess, finding a route across town, recognizing a face, translating a sentence, or writing an essay.

Those tasks don't have much in common, and that's kind of the point. Over the last seventy years, many very different techniques have all been called AI, because each one tackled something that used to seem like it needed a human brain.

There's an old joke in the field that AI is "whatever hasn't been done yet." As soon as a computer can do something reliably, people stop calling it AI and start calling it just a program. Your maps app finding a route was cutting-edge AI research once! Now it's a feature nobody thinks about.

So when someone tells you a product "uses AI," a really useful follow-up question is: what kind, and for what task? That one question separates a technical description from a marketing sticker.

A very short history

The name "artificial intelligence" comes from a 1956 summer workshop at Dartmouth College, where a group of researchers proposed that every feature of intelligence could, in principle, be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it. They were a little optimistic about the timeline. But the history since then is a great way to see why the word covers so much.

Search and logic (1950s onward). Early AI often treated intelligence as searching through possibilities. To solve a puzzle, play checkers, or prove a logic theorem, a program would explore the possible moves, look ahead, and pick the most promising path.

This is the same family of ideas as the searching algorithms from Unit 7, and it's still how your phone finds the fastest route home. The famous high point was 1997, when IBM's Deep Blue beat world chess champion Garry Kasparov, mostly by searching through enormous numbers of possible moves very, very fast.

Expert systems (1970s and 1980s). Another approach was to interview human experts and write their knowledge down as rules: if the patient has these symptoms and this test result, then suspect this infection. You already know how to write rules like that, from Unit 4! Some of these systems worked well in narrow areas, but they were expensive to build and brittle. Every new situation needed new rules, and the real world kept producing situations nobody had written a rule for.

Both of those approaches went through cycles of excitement and disappointment. There were stretches, sometimes called "AI winters," when results didn't live up to the hype and funding dried up.

Machine learning (1990s onward). The big shift was to stop writing the rules yourself and have the computer learn them from examples. That's what made it possible to sort spam, recognize speech, and spot faces in photos, which are all tasks that are hopeless to describe with hand-written rules. Around 2012, a style of machine learning called deep learning took off and made computer vision and speech recognition suddenly far better. We'll spend most of this unit on how that learning works.

Large language models and agents (2020s). In late 2022, ChatGPT put a large language model in front of millions of people, and for most of the public, "AI" started to mean "chatbot." More recently, those models have been hooked up to tools so that they can take actions, not just write text. We'll get to both of those later in the unit.

One thing I really like about this history is that each era didn't replace the one before it. Route-finding still uses search. Tax software is still full of hand-written rules. When people say "AI" today, they usually mean the newest layer, but all the older layers are still out there, working away.

Narrow AI and general AI

There's one distinction to keep in your pocket for the whole unit.

Narrow AI is built for a limited range of tasks. A spam filter sorts email but can't drive a car. Deep Blue could beat the world chess champion and couldn't play tic-tac-toe unless someone reprogrammed it.

Chatbots make this messier, because one model can write a poem, fix your code, and explain photosynthesis in the same afternoon. That breadth is really impressive! But doing well across lots of prompts doesn't automatically mean a system understands the world the way you do. Later lessons will look at what's actually going on underneath.

Artificial general intelligence, or AGI, is the idea of a system that could learn and reason flexibly across pretty much any domain, the way people can. There's no agreed definition of AGI and no agreed test for it, and experts disagree sharply about how close current systems are. So the grounded position is to skip the labels and ask concrete questions: What can this system do? How was it built and tested? Where does it fail?

Try it: Think back over the last 24 hours and list five times an AI system might have been involved: a route on your phone, a recommendation, a spam filter, an autocorrected word, a face unlock, &c. For each one, guess which era from the history above it belongs to. Is it searching? Following rules? Learning from examples?
