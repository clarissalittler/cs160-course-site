Lesson 1 - What Is AI?

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain, in plain words, what people mean when they say "AI."
  • Spot the AI that's already woven through your everyday life.
  • Tell the difference between "narrow AI" (what we actually have) and "general AI" (what we don't).
  • Describe the big shift that made modern AI work — letting computers learn from examples instead of being told every rule.

So what is AI, really?

Let's start with the least helpful, most honest answer: AI is a goal, not a gadget. "Artificial intelligence" is the name we give to the whole project of getting computers to do things we'd normally call smart if a person did them — recognizing a friend's face in a photo, understanding a sentence you typed, recommending a song you end up loving, finding the fastest way across town, driving a car.

Notice that those tasks don't have much in common with each other. That's the point. AI isn't one specific technology — it's an ambition. Lots of very different tools all get filed under "AI" because they're all chasing the same thing: making a machine handle a job that used to require a human brain.

So when someone says "we added AI to it," that tells you roughly as much as "we added an engine to it." Okay — what kind? To do what? Hold onto that healthy suspicion. It'll serve you well for the rest of the unit.

It's not what the movies told you

Say "AI" and most people picture a conscious robot with glowing eyes, plotting something. Twin Peaks weirdness, but make it a laptop. Forget all of that. Real AI today isn't a mind, it isn't conscious, and it mostly doesn't have a body. It's software — usually invisible software — quietly doing one job in the background.

And here's the thing: you're already swimming in it. Today, before you even got to this lesson, you almost certainly used a pile of AI without noticing:

  • Your email quietly dumped junk mail into a spam folder.
  • A maps app figured out a route and guessed your arrival time.
  • Your phone's photo app grouped pictures by who's in them.
  • Autocomplete finished your words (sometimes hilariously wrong).
  • Netflix, Spotify, or YouTube lined up the next thing to watch or hear.
  • Your bank flagged a weird charge as possible fraud.
  • Your face unlocked your phone in a quarter of a second.

None of that has glowing eyes. It's just ordinary AI — woven so deeply into daily life that it's become invisible, like the wiring in the walls. The dramatic robot is fiction. The spam filter is the real thing.

Narrow AI vs. general AI (this is the big one)

If you remember one distinction from this whole lesson, make it this one.

Narrow AI is good at exactly one thing. The spam filter is fantastic at sorting junk mail and completely useless at anything else — it can't recommend a song, can't drive, can't chat. The route-finder finds routes and does nothing else. Each one is a specialist with a single trick, and it does not understand the world beyond that trick. Every real AI system in existence today is narrow AI. Every single one.

"But what about ChatGPT? It can do everything!" It really can seem that way — it'll write a poem, debug code, explain photosynthesis. But it's still doing one kind of task underneath: predicting plausible text. That one trick turns out to be shockingly flexible, which is genuinely impressive — but it's a very good tool, not a mind. It doesn't know things the way you know things, and it isn't aware of itself any more than your calculator is. (We'll dig into how it actually works later in the unit.)

General AI — often called AGI, for "artificial general intelligence" — is the hypothetical other thing: a system that could learn and reason across anything, the way a person can. The same mind that cooks dinner, files taxes, comforts a friend, and picks up a new hobby. We don't have this. Not close, not kind-of, not "almost." It doesn't exist.

And honestly, the conversation about AGI is a bit of a mess. Ask ten experts what AGI even means and you'll get ten different answers. Ask when it'll arrive — or whether it ever will — and you'll get everything from "a few years" to "never," with no agreement in sight. So here's the calm, non-hyped version to carry with you: today's AI, however jaw-dropping it looks, is a collection of narrow tools. Not minds. Don't buy the doom, don't buy the hype — just keep that grounded picture in your head.

Try it: Think back over the last 24 hours and write down five times an AI did something for you — a recommendation, an unlocked phone, a spam folder, an autocompleted word, a flagged charge, whatever. Bet you can find more than five once you start looking. That's the whole point: it's everywhere, and it's nearly invisible.

Where this is all headed

Here's a question worth sitting with: if AI is so woven into everything now, why did it take so long? Computers have been around for ages.

For decades, the plan was to hand-program intelligence directly. Smart people would sit down and try to write out every rule a computer would need. Want a program to recognize a cat? Just describe a cat in rules — has fur, has whiskers, has pointy ears... and it falls apart almost immediately. What about a hairless cat? A cat curled into a ball? A cat in shadow? A cat that's mostly behind a food cart? You can't write enough rules to cover the messy, fuzzy real world, and for a long time, that's exactly where AI got stuck. It could win at chess (rigid rules, clean board) but couldn't reliably tell a dog from a muffin in a photo.

The breakthrough was to stop writing the rules at all. Instead of telling the computer what a cat looks like, you show it thousands of pictures labeled "cat" and "not cat," and you let it figure out the pattern itself. The computer learns from examples — the same way you learned what a good dog looks like without anyone handing you a definition.

That idea — machine learning — is the engine under almost all the AI you actually use, and it's where we're headed next.
